A register, not a broker

The Retreat Register is an independent reference work: a national directory of curated corporate retreat venues, built because finding one still takes weeks. It is compiled the way a reference book is compiled, which means it publishes what it cut as well as what it kept.

How the register is built

  1. We scout and filter

    We search a market end to end and shelve what does not meet the standard.

  2. We keep it real

    We compare listings, contact details and photos. When we cannot confirm something, we say so.

  3. We show the receipts

    Coverage snapshots, findings from the data, and how many got cut.

  4. Owners take the wheel

    Claiming is free for the first 60 days after the register opens. We hand the keys back to venue owners, inviting them to claim, polish, and perfect their listings.

Why this exists

Ask anyone who has planned a company offsite. The searching is the worst part. Search results bury pricing behind quote forms and circle the same ten famous destinations. Planners end up re-checking every listing against the venue’s own website anyway, because the listing cannot be trusted on its own.

The gap is not a shortage of venues. It is a shortage of a trustworthy list. A planner searching for a lodge that sleeps sixty in the mountains does not need another marketplace. They need someone to have already thrown out the budget motel that calls itself a retreat, the convention hall with no beds, and the listing whose phone number stopped working in 2023.

That is what a register is for.

What a reference work is

A reference work is a compiled record that is meant to be checked. An almanac, a directory of licensed trades, a catalogue of a museum’s holdings: none of them sell you anything, and all of them can be held to account for a wrong entry. The value is not in how many rows they hold. It is in the fact that somebody decided what did not go in.

Three things follow from building this way, and they are the whole difference between a register and a listings site.

It publishes its cuts as well as its additions. A list that only grows tells you nothing about its standards. The cuts are the evidence. On July 11, 2026 a person read every published row in this register, every name and every website, and cut 274 entries the software had missed.2 On July 12, 2026 the register put 2,811 of its own past rejections back on trial and found that 606 of them had been wrongly cut, while 2,193 rejections were upheld and 12 were set aside as obvious junk.3 Both of those numbers are uncomfortable. Both are published.

It has to say when it does not know. A wrong number is worse than a gap. Where a venue does not publish its capacity or its pricing, the listing says so instead of filling the space with an estimate.

Who it is for

  • Planners. The person who has been handed the offsite on top of their actual job, and has three weeks to find somewhere for forty people. They need a shortlist that survives a phone call.
  • Small teams without a travel department. Companies that have no agency, no negotiated rates, and no way to tell a real retreat property from a hotel with a banquet room.
  • Congregations and community groups. Faith-based retreat centers are in the register on the same terms as everything else. They are frequently the properties that large platforms ignore entirely.
  • Venue owners. Especially the ones in markets nobody scouts. Claiming a listing is free for the first 60 days after the register opens.
  • Anyone who wants the numbers rather than the pitch. The markets page shows state-by-state coverage with the date it was measured, and the methodology page walks through the checks a property has to survive.

The rules it runs on

  • A venue is either good enough to be in the register, or it is not in it. That rule was set on the first day of the project, July 7, 2026, and nothing since has loosened it.1
  • The venue’s own website is the reference. Software reads the property’s own site rather than a listing about the property, because a listing about a property inherits whoever wrote it. The full methodology is public.
  • No invented data. Where pricing or capacity is not public, the listing says so instead of guessing.
  • Small markets count. Coverage runs across the whole map, and the thin states are searched first rather than last. See where it stands today.
  • Corrections are welcome and they get made. Errors in a register built from public data are inevitable. Here is how to report one.

What the register refuses to do, and why

It does not run on reviews. Fake reviews, including AI-generated ones, became illegal in the United States on October 21, 2024.5 The register has never used reviews at all, so it has nothing to police. A venue’s own website, checked, is harder to fake than a star rating.

It does not stand between you and the venue. Every listing points at the property’s own website and its own phone number. On July 18, 2026, 1,928 of the register’s 2,125 venues carried a direct number, which is 91%.7

It does not print a price it has not seen. Most retreat venues quote per group, and the register measured how few publish a number: 133 of 1,374 venues on July 12, 2026.4 Guessing the other 1,241 would have made the site look more useful and been wrong more than nine times out of ten.

It does not quietly refresh a number. Old figures stay up with their dates on them. A count from July 12 stays labelled July 12, even after the count changes.

Who is behind it

The Retreat Register is independently built and operated. It is a small operation with no ownership ties to any venue or chain. The work runs on a purpose-built review pipeline, and the process is documented openly on the methodology page and in the updates.

Where it stands

The last exact total the register published was 2,152 curated venues on July 19, 2026.6 Coverage runs across the whole country and is deliberately uneven, with the thinnest states at the front of the search queue. Progress is published with real numbers, including what gets cut and why. Follow it on Updates.

How to Plan a Women's Retreat: A Checklist From Venue to Last Night

November 3, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Start with the group's purpose and size, then book a venue that sleeps everyone, then plan the days around rest and time together. That sequence answers how to plan a women's retreat.

The planner who skips the purpose step ends up with a schedule that feels crowded and a budget that breaks before the first meal. Write the purpose down first. Count the people who will actually come. Those two numbers drive every decision that follows.

How to run the numbers

Sit with the person who asked for the retreat and ask what change they want to see when the group goes home. A church group may want deeper prayer habits. A friends group may want uninterrupted conversation. Write the answer in one sentence and tape it to your planning notebook.

Count the confirmed attendees, not the maybes. Add two extra beds for late decisions or a speaker who stays over. That number tells you the minimum bedrooms and bathrooms you need. To find your guest per bathroom ratio divide the total guests by the bathrooms in the venue you are considering.

If the ratio climbs above four guests per bath the morning line will stretch past breakfast. Plan for that now or hear about it later.

Record the purpose and the final headcount in a shared document so every helper sees the same numbers.

Choose a venue that fits the numbers and the purpose

Search for properties that sleep your total with at least one extra bed. Filter for the bathroom ratio you worked out. Note the kitchen size because a crowd of twelve needs more than a two burner stove.

Read the rental agreement before you commit. Some venues charge extra for linens or for using the fire pit. Request the full written estimate before you sign.

Ask for photos of the common room at night. A space that looks bright at noon may feel dim when the group gathers for evening prayer or stories.

Confirm the cancellation policy in writing. Email the owner with your dates and your headcount and save the reply.

Build the daily rhythm around rest and connection

Sketch the days in blocks of three hours. Morning block for teaching or guided activity. Midday block for meals and long walks. Afternoon block for choice time where each person picks rest or a craft or a nap. Evening block for shared reflection.

Protect at least two hours of unscheduled time each full day. The best conversations happen when no one is watching the clock.

Limit formal sessions to ninety minutes. Attention fades after that and the room grows stale.

Post the schedule on a whiteboard in the kitchen. Let people move a magnet if they need to shift a walk to morning.

Handle food and practical details without surprise

Decide early whether you will cook together or hire a cook. A shared meal plan builds community but needs a lead cook who knows the kitchen. A hired cook frees the group but adds cost.

Collect dietary restrictions in a simple spreadsheet. Note allergies separately from preferences. Share the list with whoever shops or cooks.

Buy staples in bulk before you arrive. Bring coffee tea sugar oil salt and snacks in bins labeled by day. The legal pad taped to the refrigerator tracks what runs low.

Assign one person per meal to clean up. Rotate the duty so no one misses the evening fire.

Edge cases that derail the week

You will hear that someone cannot climb stairs after you book the loft bedroom. Ask about mobility before you sign the lease.

You will discover the wifi fails when the whole group streams video. Download any teaching videos ahead of time.

You will find the propane tank empty on the second night. Check the gauge on arrival and locate the supplier phone number.

If a detail can stop the group from sleeping eating or meeting then verify it before you leave home. A missing pillow is a nuisance, but a missing stove rewrites the whole weekend.

After the purpose sentence is taped, open a shared spreadsheet and create two columns: one for the purpose statement and one for the confirmed headcount. Update the headcount each time a guest replies yes, and lock the sheet so only the lead planner can edit the purpose line. When the final number settles, copy the sheet into the master planning document. A single source prevents the confusion that arises when someone works from an outdated count. Keep the spreadsheet open on a laptop during the final week so any last minute change can be entered without delay. Print a one page summary and tape it beside the whiteboard for quick reference.

Before you sign, walk the property at the same time of day the group will arrive and note the lighting in the common room and the condition of the stairs. Ask the owner for a written list of any extra fees such as cleaning deposits or firewood charges so the budget stays intact. Verify that the kitchen appliances match the meal plan you drafted, especially the oven capacity and the number of burners. A quick walk-through now saves a frantic email later. Take photos of each bedroom and the bathroom layout to compare with the floor plan you printed. If the owner cannot provide a written fee schedule, treat that as a red flag and keep looking.

Insert a quiet hour into the schedule after the evening block so the group can settle before lights out. Use a simple kitchen timer set for sixty minutes and place it on the table where everyone can see it. When the timer rings, invite anyone who wishes to share a brief reflection or a prayer before the night deepens. This small ritual marks the transition from activity to rest without a formal announcement. Keep the timer visible for the next morning so the same cue can start the day with a moment of stillness. If a guest prefers to skip the quiet hour, note the preference on the whiteboard and honor it without comment. The group learns to expect the pause.

Prepare a backup meal kit that contains shelf stable proteins, dried vegetables, and a few comfort snacks in case the primary plan falls through. Store the kit in a clearly labeled bin on the top shelf of the pantry so it can be reached without rummaging. Write the preparation steps on a card and tape the card to the bin lid for quick reference. When the main cook is delayed, the backup kit keeps the schedule moving and the group fed. Include a small notebook in the bin for the cook to log what was used and what remains. Rotate the backup kit each retreat so the contents stay fresh and the group learns the routine.

Check the propane gauge on arrival and write the reading on the legal pad. If the needle rests at zero, the stove will not work. Record the reading in the shared spreadsheet too. A missing gauge reading is a small detail that becomes a large problem when the kettle refuses to boil.

Open your notebook today. Write the purpose in one sentence. Count the confirmed guests. Calculate the bathroom ratio. Those three steps come first.

When the group gathers next season you will have a plan that holds because it started with the real numbers and the real reason you came together.

Write a Retreat RFP Email: What to Ask Every Venue in One Message

August 20, 2026 · The Retreat Register

One complete email to every venue gives you replies you can set side by side. The message needs your dates, your head count, the rooms and meals, your access questions and a reply date.

Why one complete message beats several short ones

A venue that gets a short note will answer the short note. Ask only for availability and you will get dates. Ask for a price and you will get a figure without its conditions. Each follow-up question adds a round of emails, and open dates may fill while you wait.

A single message that covers everything lets a venue answer once, and it makes the replies comparable. Three venues that all answered the same ten questions give you three columns you can read across. Three venues that each answered a different mix of questions give you three documents that need translating. The time you spend on the first message comes back many times over, because every later step starts from the same shared set of facts.

Write it as a plain email. A venue does not need a formal document, and some small properties are run by a few people who read email between tasks. Short paragraphs, one question per line and a clear last line asking for a reply by a named date work better than a long page of prose.

Keep the message to one screen if you can. A venue owner who reads it on a small screen can find each question without scrolling back, and your own reading of the replies is quicker when every message answers the same short list.

What goes in the message

Start with the facts the venue cannot answer without. Give the dates you want and say how flexible they are. State the head count and whether it is firm. Name the type of group, such as a team retreat or a board planning session, because a venue plans a meeting room differently from a family gathering. Say how many nights you need and whether the group arrives all at once or in waves.

Describe the space next. Say how many people sit together in the main working room, whether you need smaller rooms for breakouts, and what equipment the sessions use. Say whether guests share rooms or need their own, and whether anyone needs to stay in a particular kind of room. Describe the meals in a line or two: which meals you want, whether the venue cooks them, and any dietary needs you already know about.

Then ask what the venue wants to know. A venue may need a deposit, a signed contract or a final head count by a certain date. Asking for those terms in the first message saves a round of emails and lets you compare the conditions along with the figures.

Put the access questions in the first message

The Americans with Disabilities Act applies to places of lodging, and the ADA National Network has a factsheet that sums up what lodging has to do. It says people with disabilities must be able to make reservations for accessible guest rooms, and that places of lodging must identify and describe the accessible features of the property and its accessible guest rooms.1 That is a good reason to ask for a description in your first message and to expect a real answer.

The factsheet also says customer service staff must be trained to respond to specific inquiries about the features of the facility, and that accessible guest rooms must be held back until all other rooms of that type have been rented.1 So a question such as which rooms are accessible, what features each one has and how the venue holds them for guests who need them is an ordinary question that the venue's staff should be ready to answer.

The factsheet describes one exemption: facilities that contain five rooms or fewer for rent where the proprietor actually lives.1 If you are writing to a very small owner-occupied place, a different answer may reflect that exemption, and it is worth asking the owner to explain rather than assuming.

Write the question for the whole group and not for one named guest. A line such as which rooms and shared spaces can a guest who uses a wheelchair reach, and can you describe the route, lets the venue answer for every room. If a particular guest later shares a need with you, follow up with that guest's permission and with only the detail the venue needs.

Ask for the full cost and what sits outside it

Price is the part of an RFP that can come back in a form you cannot compare. A federal rule on unfair or deceptive fees gives you a useful model for the wording. For covered consumer prices, such as short-term lodging and live-event tickets, it says a business that shows a price must disclose the total price more prominently than any other pricing information.2 It also says that, before the buyer agrees to pay, the business must disclose the nature, purpose and amount of any charge left out of the total, along with the final amount of payment.2

The rule covers consumer prices, so a group contract may or may not fall under it, and you should not draw a legal conclusion from it. It is still a good yardstick for your message. Ask each venue to give one total for the stay, to list every charge that sits outside the total with what it is for and how much it is, and to say which of those charges are required and which are optional.

Ask about the terms that sit behind the figure. When is the deposit due, and how much of it can be returned? What are the cancellation terms? Can the venue hold your dates for a short period while you decide? A quote that does not mention these is incomplete, and the venue's answer to a direct question shows how it handles detail.

Set a reply date and keep the thread

End the message with a date. Give the venue a few working days, say that you are comparing several properties, and invite the venue to tell you early if it cannot host the group. A plain refusal helps, because it saves you from waiting on a venue that has no room.

When the replies arrive, put them in one folder and read them side by side. Mark each question a venue left unanswered, and send a second short message that asks only for those answers. Note the date each reply arrived. If a venue promises something in an email, ask that it be written into the contract, since an email is easy to lose, and the signed booking agreement is the document both sides rely on.

A short template helps you start, and you can keep it for the next retreat. Open with who you are and what the group is. Give the dates, head count and nights. Describe the rooms, meals and equipment. Put your access questions in a short list. Ask for the total cost, what sits outside it and the deposit and cancellation terms. Close with the reply date. Send it to each venue unchanged, and keep your own copy so that you can see later exactly what each venue was asked.

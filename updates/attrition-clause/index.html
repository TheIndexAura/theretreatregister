Fix the attrition clause before you sign a venue contract

August 21, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Your group shrank by a few people, and the invoice stayed the same.

The reason sits in one section of the venue contract, the attrition clause. It sets a minimum commitment. If your final numbers fall below it, your group pays for the gap anyway.

The usual cause is a signature that goes on before the headcount is firm. An early estimate feels like a rough guess when you give it, and the contract can treat it as the number everything else is measured against.

So find the clause first. Look for the minimum percentage of rooms or guests it requires, and for the date your final count is due. Both can move. Ask for a lower percentage and a later review date, and get whatever the venue agrees to in writing.

The sections below quote a sample hotel room sales agreement from the University of Minnesota. Hold your own contract next to it and see where the wording differs.

What one written attrition term looks like

A sample hotel agreement posted by the University of Minnesota shows the mechanics in plain words. The hotel says it is "relying on the Group" to use a stated number of room nights. If actual use falls below 75 percent of that total, the group pays for the gap at the average group rate, plus taxes. Treat it as one example, because other contracts will differ. The file is from 2017, and it still has blanks where the numbers go (Read the sample agreement).

Why the wording says damages

The same sample calls the payment "liquidated damages and not as a penalty". Cornell's Legal Information Institute explains the idea. Liquidated damages are an amount, or a formula, that both sides set in the contract before they sign. Courts will not impose the amount when the clause is punitive. Do not plan around that safety net. Settle the number while you still have the pen (Cornell Legal Information Institute on liquidated damages).

The release date leaves the duty in place

In the sample, unreserved rooms go back on sale to the public at the cut-off date, yet the agreement says that release "does not affect" the group's duty to use the rooms. Two other terms soften the blow. The hotel credits the group when it resells a room, up to the agreed rate. And the group may re-evaluate its block until a set date, with the cut capped at 20 percent of the contracted amounts and every change made in writing and signed by both parties (Room sales agreement, cut-off and re-evaluation terms).

Cancellation sits next to attrition

The sample prices a cancellation on a sliding scale of total revenue. The share owed climbs from 0 percent to 15, then 40, then 60, and finally 75 percent as arrival gets close. The day counts sit in square brackets, which marks them as open to change. Draw that ladder for your own contract on a legal pad and mark where today falls on it (Room sales agreement, cancellation schedule).

Questions to email the venue

Hotel paperwork counts room nights. A lodge, a ranch or a farm stay may count beds, meals or the whole property instead. Ask which unit the minimum counts, and ask whether a resale credit and a re-evaluation window can be added. Get the answers into the signed agreement. The sample requires changes in writing, signed by both parties, and yours should meet the same test (Room sales agreement, University of Minnesota).

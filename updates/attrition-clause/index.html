Check the attrition clause in a venue contract before you sign

August 21, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A venue contract can set an attrition minimum and a charge for a shortfall, as a hotel sample agreement does. Ask what your own contract's minimum says and what its charge for a shortfall is called.

Find the clause before you sign

What happens to the invoice if your group shrinks by a few people? The answer sits in the venue contract, in a section that may carry a heading such as attrition. Read the contract for any minimum the group agrees to use, and for what the group owes if actual use falls below it. If the group signs on an early estimate, ask what figure the contract measures everything against, and decide before anyone signs whether that estimate is one the group can stand behind.

Read the whole contract for the clause, and read it slowly. Look for the minimum the venue expects the group to use, any date that fixes or changes the count, and the formula for any payment if the group falls below the minimum. Look also for any date after which the venue may sell rooms the group has not filled. Write each of these on a single sheet in plain words. Everyone on the planning team can then see what you are agreeing to.

Ask the venue whether the minimum and the dates are fixed or can be discussed before signature. Only a lawyer can say what your own contract means. Before anyone signs for the group, have a lawyer read the final draft, including the sections on attrition and cancellation.

See how one sample agreement words it

A useful way to learn the wording is to read a real template. The University of Minnesota posts a sample hotel room sales agreement in its policy library, with blanks where the numbers go. Its attrition section opens by saying the hotel is relying on the group to use a stated number of room nights, and the group agrees that the hotel would incur a loss if room nights used fall by more than twenty-five percent.1 Treat it as one example. Another venue's paper may be written very differently.

The sample then sets the trigger. If the room nights actually used are below seventy-five percent of the total, the group agrees to pay the difference between that seventy-five percent mark and actual use, multiplied by the average group rate plus any applicable taxes.1 In this sample, the charge is built on the gap between that seventy-five percent mark and actual use. Whether your own contract sets a similar mark, and at what figure, is a question for the venue before you sign.

Read your own contract the same way. Find the sentence that states the minimum, find the sentence that states the payment, and mark the unit being counted. The sample counts room nights. Ask the venue what unit your contract counts, whether that is rooms, beds, meals or something else, and how a shortfall in that unit is turned into a payment.

Understand what liquidated damages means

The same sample describes the payment as liquidated damages "and not as a penalty."1 It is worth knowing what that phrase refers to. Cornell's Legal Information Institute says liquidated damages are "an exact amount of money, or a set formula to calculate the amount of money," and that the clause must be clearly stated in the contract and agreed by the parties before they enter it.2 The sample names a threshold, a rate and a method of calculation for its payment, so a planner can work out the figure from the contract itself.

Cornell also gives the purpose. It says parties use liquidated damages where actual damages, though real, are difficult or impossible to prove.2 The sample says something similar about cancellation, that it would be difficult to determine the hotel's actual harm.1 The Cornell page adds that courts will not impose liquidated damages if the clause is punitive, illegal, unconscionable or contrary to public policy.2

That last point is not a plan. The Cornell page does not explain how a court decides whether an amount is punitive, and it does not describe how the rules differ from state to state. The time to question a figure is before signing, while you can still ask for a different one. If the number looks high to you, ask the venue about it, and ask a lawyer who knows the law where the venue sits what the clause means for your group.

Watch the release date and the resale credit

The sample contains a cut-off date, and it shows what that date does and does not do. On the cut-off date, it says, all unreserved rooms are released for sale to the general public, and requests received after that date are accepted on a space and rate available basis.1 A planner might read that as the moment the venue takes the empty rooms back and the matter is closed. The sample states otherwise. It says that the release of rooms for general sale after the cut-off date does not affect the group's obligation to use guest rooms.1

The sample also contains a term that softens the payment. If the hotel determines that part of the room block is available for resale, it says the hotel will attempt to sell the unused portion. If a group room is resold, the attrition charge is reduced by the room rate received, up to the rate in the agreement. If unused group rooms are returned to inventory for attempted resale, they are the last to be sold.1 Whether your contract has a resale credit is something to look for and, if it is missing, something to ask the venue about. The sample sets out the credit and the order of sale, and it does not describe any other effort the hotel must make to resell.

Cancellation sits close to attrition in the same document. The sample asks for written notice of a decision to cancel, says the group agrees that cancellation would be a breach that would harm the hotel, and sets out a chart of liquidated damages with a bracketed note that the sliding scale reduces damages for early cancellation.1 The day counts in the sample chart are in square brackets, and the document is a template with blanks. Ask whether the numbers in your own contract can change. Draw the chart for your own contract on a sheet of paper and mark where today falls on it.

Put these questions to the venue in writing

Decide your best honest estimate of attendance before you ask for a number, and decide who in the group has the authority to confirm the final count. Then send the venue a short list of questions by email. The answers are then on the record. Ask what unit the minimum counts, and what share of that unit the group must use before any payment applies. Ask whether the percentage can be lowered, and whether the venue will review it after the registration deadline.

Ask for a resale credit to be written into the contract if it is not there, and ask how the venue treats rooms or beds that it fills with other guests. Ask which date the final count is due, and what happens if you need to change it after that date. Ask whether the minimum applies to the group as a whole or to each night separately, and ask for a worked example using your own dates.

The sample gives one more clue about timing. It says no space or guest room arrangements in the agreement are binding on the hotel until the agreement is fully signed by both parties, and that the hotel must receive a signed copy by the decision date or reserves the right to release all space for resale.1 Decide before that date whether the headcount is firm enough to sign against. If you need more time, ask for a later date in writing rather than signing against an estimate. Keep every reply, and make sure that any agreed change appears in the contract itself before anyone signs for the group, then give the whole document to a lawyer for review.

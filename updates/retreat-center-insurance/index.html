Planner Guide

Learn what retreat center insurance terms mean before you book

October 20, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A retreat center owner may hold several kinds of insurance, and the names on the paperwork are easy to misread. Two public insurance pages, one federal and one from a state regulator, give plain words for the labels, and a planner can turn them into questions for a venue before signing.

Learn the two labels you will see first

The Small Business Administration publishes a page on getting business insurance, and it uses plain labels that a planner can borrow. It describes general liability as coverage that "protects against financial loss as the result of bodily injury, property damage" and the sentence goes on to name more kinds of loss, including medical expenses and defending lawsuits.1 It describes commercial property as coverage that protects a business against loss and damage to company property due to a wide variety of events.1

Those two labels are worth knowing when you ask what the venue carries. The first covers injury and damage claims. The second covers the business's own property. Knowing which is which makes the conversation shorter. That is the point.

The same SBA page describes a business owner's policy as an insurance package that combines all of the typical coverage options into one bundle.1 So when an owner says the venue has one policy that handles everything, ask what is inside the bundle. What any one venue's policy holds is a question for the venue and its licensed insurance agent.

See what a state insurance guide says about liability

One state guide shows how a regulator explains the terms. Virginia's insurance bureau publishes a consumer guide for commercial insurance, written for Virginia businesses. How its terms apply in the venue's own state is a question for a licensed insurance agent. The guide itself says it is for educational purposes only and is not an opinion on coverage under any specific policy.2

On liability, the guide says commercial general liability provides many liability coverages under one contract.2 It says premises and operations coverage pays bodily injury and property damage claims to members of the public, and that a liability policy generally will provide for your legal defense.2 For a planner, the member of the public in that sentence is a useful picture. Ask the venue's licensed agent whether an injury to a guest at the lodge, such as a fall on a loose step, would be treated as that kind of claim under the venue's own policy, and ask what the policy says about legal defense.

Keep it plain. Treat the guide as a source of vocabulary for commercial policies in general. Ask the venue for its actual policy documents, and take what they say about limits and covered events to a licensed insurance agent.

Read the property side with its limits in mind

The Virginia guide spends time on property coverage, and two of its points are worth knowing before you hear the phrase "all risk" from a venue. It says the term "all risk" does not mean every loss is covered, and it says the exclusions in an all risk policy are specifically listed as exclusions in the policy.2 It also advises buyers to make sure they know what types of events or perils are not covered by the policy they are considering.2

That gives a planner questions to bring. If a venue says the building is fully covered, ask the venue for the policy's list of exclusions and whether the owner has read it. Which events or perils the policy does not cover, and which parts of a retreat property, such as the lodge, the kitchen and the grounds, it addresses, are questions for a licensed insurance agent.

The guide also separates two ways of valuing property. It describes replacement cost as paying without deducting anything for depreciation, and actual cash value as the amount after deducting for depreciation.2 A planner will not choose between these for the owner. Ask whether the owner can explain the difference in a sentence or two. Short answers are fine.

Ask what the owner did to choose the policy

The SBA page lays out steps for buying coverage, and the first three are to assess your risks, find a reputable licensed agent, and shop around, which it explains as comparing rates, terms and benefits across insurance offers.1 Those steps are a fair set of topics to raise with the venue in conversation. Ask who the owner's agent is, and ask when the policy was last reviewed with that agent.

The Virginia guide gives similar advice from the other side of the desk. It says to deal with someone licensed to sell insurance in Virginia, to ask questions because it is important to understand the coverages you are getting before you have a loss, and to check with your agent periodically as well as whenever new property or operations are added.2 That last point fits retreat centers well, since a venue may add a sauna, a boat dock, a new cabin or a new kind of program over the years. Ask whether the agent was told about each addition. Keep it simple.

Whether the owner has read the policy closely is also worth raising. Decide with your group whether anyone on your side should read the venue's policy, and ask the venue whether that is possible. Ask whether the owner has read it closely, and whether a program like yours was described to the agent in plain terms. A yoga weekend and a ropes course are different activities. Say so.

Bring a short list of questions to the venue

With those terms in hand, a planner can ask short questions that need no insurance training. Does the venue carry general liability and commercial property, or one package policy that combines them? Who is the licensed agent, and can that person answer a question from the group in writing? Has the agent been told about every activity the venue offers, and about any activity your group plans to bring?

The SBA page also says the federal government requires every business with employees to have a list of coverages, and workers' compensation appears on that list.1 Ask the venue whether it has employees and, if it does, whether it can share its workers' compensation documents for the staff who work your event. What that coverage includes is a question for a licensed insurance agent, and whether the federal list applies to a particular venue is a question for a lawyer.

Ask what the venue wants from your group. Start small. Ask whether the venue wants proof of cover from your group. Ask whether that is part of the contract, what the owner wants it to show, and by what date. Decide who on your side would collect the document, since it may have to come from an organization that sits above the planner, such as an employer or a nonprofit board.

Remember that both pages describe coverage in general terms. For your own case, ask the venue for its policy documents, ask a licensed insurance agent what they cover, ask a lawyer what the contract means for your group before you sign it, and keep a written copy of any answer that affects who pays if something goes wrong.

Planner Guide

Plan a Multi-Generation Family Weekend Under One Roof

November 7, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Grandparents, parents and small children under one roof raise three questions fast. Who sleeps where? How does an older guest get around the house? Where does the baby's crib go? Settle each with the venue before anyone books a room.

Count the beds before you count the guests

Start with the sleepers. Write down every person by name, and beside each name put what that person needs at night: a double bed, a crib, or a ground-floor room with a bathroom close by. This list beats a plain total. A house can hold enough beds and still have the wrong ones in the wrong places.

Send the list to the venue and ask for its room inventory in return, then lay the two side by side. Ask how many beds sit on each floor and which bedrooms can be reached without stairs. Suppose the venue says a sofa bed will cover the gap. Ask who it actually suits, whether the guest assigned to it can sleep there comfortably, and whether the shared living room stays quiet after dark.

Guests with the most limited mobility belong on the lowest floor, near a bathroom. Children who handle stairs well can take the upper rooms. Put the plan on paper and share it before the trip, and ask each family to confirm the rooms they were given. Early sharing means no family arrives at a rented house to find the arrangement is news to them.

Stairs, light and the bathroom

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has a page on preventing falls, and it opens with a short statement. It says that "simple actions can help you prevent falling," and it adds that you should "talk to your doctor about the best methods of fall prevention."1 The list that follows covers railings, grab bars and lighting. In a rented house, each of those turns into a question for the venue or a chore on arrival.

Take railings first. The page says to "put railings on both sides of stairs."1 Ask the venue whether every staircase has one, and whether it runs along both sides. Ask about porch steps and the path to the parking area too. The page speaks of stairs in general and names no places, so those questions are the planner's own. A staircase with a railing on one side only is useful to know about early. The family can then decide which bedrooms are better placed elsewhere.

Bathrooms come next. The same page says to "add grab bars inside and outside your tub or shower and next to the toilet."1 It says nothing about rental houses, so the question belongs to the venue. Before booking, ask which bathrooms have grab bars and which have a walk-in shower instead of a tub. Ask whether temporary aids or changes are allowed, and who has to approve them. If the answer is no, a guest who needs support may need another room, or another house. Better to hear that in week one of planning than on the doorstep.

Two more lines from the page apply once the group arrives. It says to "get rid of things you could trip over," and to "make sure your home has lots of light by adding more or brighter light bulbs."1 Walk the house as soon as the first car pulls in. Trace the route from each bedroom to the nearest bathroom. Push loose rugs and cords aside and find the light switches. Check each hallway at the hour guests would actually use it, and have someone try the switches in the dark. A few night-lights, placed where the route bends, cost little to pack.

Medicines on a busy weekend

Ask each older guest whether a weekend away shifts the times they take their medicines. Ask who in the family keeps track of those times during the weekend. The CDC page says to "ask your doctor or pharmacist to review your medicines to see if any might make you dizzy or sleepy."1 That review is a conversation between the guest and a doctor or pharmacist, and a planner cannot hold it for them. A planner can bring it up while the trip is still weeks away.

Decide who in the family knows what each older guest takes, and when. Ask each guest, or the guest's doctor, whether any medicine or device needs special storage or a power outlet near the bed. Then put the fridge and outlet questions to the venue ahead of time. The guest knows what the item needs, and the venue knows what the house can offer.

A safe place for the baby to sleep

A baby in the group gets a plan of its own. The CDC has a page on sleeping safely for babies with a short set of instructions. It says to "place your baby on his or her back for all sleep times," and to "use a firm, flat (not at an angle or inclined) sleep surface."2 It also says to "keep soft bedding out of your baby's sleep area."2

Turn those lines into questions. Does the venue provide a crib or a bassinet? Is the mattress firm and flat? Parents may bring their own crib if the venue has none, and the venue should hear about it early so there is floor space. Ask what bedding comes with the crib or bassinet, since the page says to keep soft bedding out of the sleep area.

The page also advises keeping the baby's sleep area, for example a crib or bassinet, in the same room where you sleep.2 That changes the layout of the house. Decide which room has space for the crib beside the parents' bed, then ask the venue whether it fits. A small room that holds the parents and the crib together may be a better trade than a larger room shared with a snoring uncle.

Two more lines matter in a full house. The page says "do not cover your baby's head or allow your baby to get too hot," and "do not smoke or allow smoking around your baby."2 Ask the venue how each bedroom is heated. Decide the smoking rule before the trip, and tell the whole group when they arrive.

Meals, rest and the rooms in between

Kitchens, quiet rooms and activity schedules are questions for the venue and decisions for the family. How many people does the dining table seat? Count the burners against what the group plans to cook. Ask guests about allergies in the same email that asks about bed needs.

Pick one room that stays calm in the afternoon. Ask each guest, and the parents of any baby, whether they want a rest or an hour alone at some point after lunch. If the house has only one such room, settle in advance who gets it at which hour. Plan outings around the slowest walker. Keep a few shared hours on the schedule and leave plenty of unplanned time around them. Ask the youngest and the oldest guests how much scheduled time suits them.

Before the group arrives, send one message that holds the sleeping plan, the meal plan, the questions you asked and the answers the venue gave. When an answer changes, update the plan the same day and tell the person it affects.

Book a Retreat Venue for a Group of Twelve: Small Properties and First Questions

August 10, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A group of twelve can suit a small property better than a large one. Look for places where your group is the main booking. Ask about access in your first message, and get every answer in writing.

Why a group of twelve needs the right fit

Twelve people can be hard to place. A vacation rental may run out of beds, bathrooms or table space. A large property may be set up for bigger groups, so ask how it would treat yours.

The way through is to look for places where twelve is the whole booking. That might be a lodge with a handful of rooms or a working ranch with one long table for meals. A small inn that hands over the entire building to one group works as well. In each case your group is the main event, and the people who run the place can give it their attention.

Say so in your search. Use words that describe the arrangement you want, such as whole house, exclusive use or private group, and read listings for what they say about sole use. Exclusive use of the whole property for a weekend suits a small group.

Read the listing for what it leaves out

A listing can tell you a lot before you write a word. Look for a room your team can use for working sessions and for a clear statement of how many people the property sleeps. Check whether the listing gives a way to contact a person by email and whether it says what a group booking involves. If no price is shown, expect to ask.

Small inns and ranches may list on their own websites, so widen where you look. Search by region and by type of property. Check the venue's own website and read the pages that describe its rooms and meals in detail. Pay attention to what is missing. A listing that names bedrooms but not bathrooms, or meals but not who cooks them, shows you the questions to put in your first message.

Ask about access before you ask about price

With twelve people there can be a need nobody has mentioned yet. The federal ADA.gov introduction to the Americans with Disabilities Act says that some disabilities are visible and some are not, and its examples range from diabetes to epilepsy.1 It also says that businesses open to the public must provide people with disabilities an equal opportunity to access the goods or services they offer.1

That gives you the reason to ask early. The first email to a small lodge can carry one plain question. Which rooms and shared spaces could a guest who uses a wheelchair reach, and can the venue describe the route in writing? The same goes for the bathrooms and the dining area, and for the meeting room. A small property may be a historic building with steps and narrow doors, or it may have a ground-floor suite and a ramp, and the only way to learn which is to ask.

Ask the question for the whole group and not for one named guest, since a general question lets every room be described at once, and say that you are asking so that you can plan. You do not need to share anyone's private information, and a general request lets the venue answer for every room. If a guest later shares a particular need with you, you can follow up with specifics.

Send one written request for the whole group

The Events Industry Council runs APEX, which it describes as an industry standards resource. It maintains accepted practices operational templates and guidance tools, including a risk management guidebook.2 Those materials are written for event professionals, and you can borrow the idea without them. Write one complete request, because a complete request is easier to compare.

Write one message and send the same message to each property. Include your head count of twelve and your dates, and say how flexible they are. Describe the space your sessions need and your meal needs, then add your access questions and the date by which you need an answer. A single message lets a small property answer once instead of three times, and it shows the venue you are organized and serious.

Today, send that message to two lodges and keep both threads in one folder. Compare the replies side by side. The lodge that answers every line gives you text you can carry into the contract. The lodge that skips lines shows you where to follow up.

Give the venue room to say no. Some small properties cannot host every group, and a plain refusal early on saves both sides time. If a venue says it can host most of your needs but not all, ask which ones it cannot meet and whether it can suggest an alternative nearby. A practical option from the venue is worth noting for later, even when the answer for this trip is no.

Plan the reply deadline with care. Give each venue a few working days and name the date, then follow up once in writing if you hear nothing. Some small properties are run by a few people who also handle guests, so a slow reply may reflect a full week and not a lack of interest. If a venue cannot answer before your own deadline, say so and ask whether it can still hold your dates.

Use the same questions for each property so the replies line up. A simple sheet with one column per venue helps: rooms and beds, bathrooms, meals, access, the dates the venue can hold and the date the final head count is due. When two replies are close, the clearer one is easier to plan around, and you can ask the other venue to fill its blanks before you decide.

Check the details that matter at this size

A few questions matter more for a small group than for a large one. Find out how many bedrooms there are and how many guests the property can sleep in beds and not on sofas. Then ask how many bathrooms the group would share and where they sit. Find out whether the venue can serve meals for twelve at one table, and who cooks them. Find out what else is booked on the property during your dates, and how the venue would keep your group's space private.

Think about what happens if a guest drops out or one more joins. A property that holds twelve comfortably may be tight at thirteen, and a venue that is flexible about small changes is easier to work with than one that is not. Find out when the final head count is due and what the venue needs from you at that point.

Put the answers in your own summary and send it back to the venue to confirm. Keep the summary short enough that a busy owner can read it in a minute and answer with a plain yes or a correction. A short list of agreed points, with the venue's confirmation, is the simplest record of what you were told. It also lets you spot any gap between what the listing promised and what the venue is ready to deliver.

Choose a Retreat Meeting Room That Works on Day Two: Checks for Planners

August 11, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Book the meeting room the group will work in, which is not always the room with the best photos. Check it for glare on screens, sound that carries, chairs people can sit in for hours and a door that closes. Ask the venue to answer each of those in writing before you sign.

Separate the room for photos from the room for work

A retreat venue often has one space that sells the property. It might be a glass atrium, a timber hall with a high ceiling or a terrace over a lake. It is the room on the brochure, and it can be a fine place for a welcome dinner. A working session asks different things of a room.

Think about the second day. People have been sitting since breakfast. Someone is presenting a spreadsheet, two others are on a video call with the office, and the group needs to hear a quiet colleague at the far end of the table. Every flaw in the room is louder by then. Glare that looked like sunshine on the tour now washes out the screen. A high ceiling that felt grand now carries every chair scrape.

So treat the meeting room as its own booking decision. Ask the venue which room it would put your group in for working sessions, and ask for photos and a floor plan of that room in particular. If the answer is the showpiece room, ask what else is available.

Check light and screens

Light is the first thing to test, because it changes through the day. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration, in its guidance on computer workstations, says that if lighting is excessive or causes glare on a monitor screen, people may develop eyestrain or headaches and may have to work in awkward postures to see the screen.1 The same guidance says to arrange the space to cut glare from overhead lights, desk lamps and windows.1

A retreat room with a wall of glass behind the screen can fail that test for part of the day. Ask the venue which way the main windows face and whether they have blinds or curtains that close fully. Ask where the screen or projector sits in relation to those windows. If the venue sends photos, ask for one taken at the time of day your sessions run.

Then ask about the screen itself. Is there a fixed screen or a large display, and does it connect to a laptop without an adapter hunt? Who on site can help when it fails at nine in the morning? A plain answer in writing saves the first session of the trip.

Power and the network come next. Count the laptops you expect in the room, then ask how many power outlets the room has and where they sit. Outlets along one wall leave cables across the floor, which is a trip risk as well as a nuisance. Ask whether the venue can lend power strips and tape the cords down. For the network, ask whether the meeting room has its own wireless signal or shares one with the guest rooms, and how many people have used it at once without trouble. A video call that freezes every few minutes costs more of the morning than a slow coffee service. If the answer is vague, ask whether a wired connection is available for the presenter's laptop.

Check sound for everyone in the room

Sound is the second test. Hard floors, glass and high ceilings bounce voices around, and a large group in a small, echoing room spends the day asking people to repeat themselves. Ask the venue what the floor and ceiling are made of, and whether the room has rugs, curtains or panels that soften sound.

Plan for guests who hear less well, too. The Justice Department's ADA Standards for Accessible Design say that in each assembly area where audible communication is integral to the use of the space, an assistive listening system must be provided, though outside courtrooms one is not required where there is no audio amplification.2 If the venue offers microphones and speakers in the meeting room, ask whether it also has an assistive listening system and how many receivers it can lend. If it has neither, ask what it suggests for a guest who needs help hearing.

Ask what happens next door, as well. A meeting room beside the kitchen, the bar or a busy hallway brings that noise into every session. Ask whether another group will use the rooms on either side during your dates.

Check seats, tables and the door

Chairs matter more than they look in a photo. A banquet chair is fine for an hour and hard going by the fourth. Ask the venue what chairs come with the room, whether they have cushioned seats and backs, and whether better ones are available.

Ask about the tables and how the room can be set. Round tables suit small group work, while a large square or rows of tables suit a presentation day. Ask whether staff can change the layout at lunch, and whether there is a charge or a time limit for that. Get the seated number for your layout in writing, since a room listed for a standing reception holds far more people than it can seat at tables.

Then check the door. A meeting room with an open archway onto a lobby invites interruptions, from other guests to staff setting up for the next event. Ask whether the room has a door that closes, and whether the venue can keep the space for your group alone during sessions. The same ADA standards set a clear width of at least thirty-two inches for doors on an accessible route.2 Ask the venue to confirm that the door to your meeting room meets that width, or measure it on a visit.

Plan breaks, air and the room behind the room

A working room needs a short walk to coffee, water and restrooms. Ask how far those are from the meeting room and whether breaks can be set up just outside the door. A long walk to a single restroom stretches every break, and the minutes add up by the afternoon.

Ask about the air as well. Can the group control the heat and air conditioning in the room, or is it set for the whole building? Do the windows open? A room that grows warm and stuffy by mid-afternoon makes for a slow last session, however good the agenda looks.

Ask for a second room too. Breakout groups need somewhere to go, and a group that splits into three teams cannot all work in one corner of the main hall. A small lounge or a second meeting room close by keeps the day moving.

The showpiece room still has a place on the trip. Use it for the welcome dinner or the closing evening, when the light and the view are the point. Here is a sentence you can paste into your first message to a venue: "Please tell me which room you would use for the working sessions, which way its windows face, what chairs come with it, and whether it has a door that closes."

Choose a Lodge, Cabins or Group Camp for a Family Reunion

October 30, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A family reunion needs lodging that fits the group: beds, bathrooms, a kitchen, a room big enough for everyone, and rooms that suit guests with access needs.

Decide how the family will sleep

Before you email any venue, settle the shape of the stay. A single lodge keeps everyone under one roof and shortens the walk between the bedrooms, the bathrooms and the dining table. A cluster of cabins spreads the group across separate buildings, so ask which relatives would sleep where. A group camp may offer a shared hall and separate sleeping buildings, so ask the venue how it is laid out. Each shape changes how far people walk, so think about who in your group will make that walk.

Then list who needs what. Make a short sheet with each household, the number of people, and anyone who needs a ground-floor room, a bed rather than a bunk, or a space close to a bathroom. That sheet is your yardstick for every venue you consider, and it saves you from rebuilding the comparison each time a new listing arrives.

Count beds, bathrooms and seats

Ask each venue for a written bed count, split by type. A bed, a bunk and a sofa that pulls out are three different things, so ask whether a listed sleeping number counts beds, bunks and pull-out sofas. Ask whether each bunk is a full mattress on a frame or something that needs a pad on the floor. Ask whether extra mattresses are allowed, who supplies them, and whether sheets and towels come with the rooms or have to be brought. If guests must bring linens, work out the quantity for each bedroom so nobody arrives short.

Bathrooms come next, so count them early. Ask for the number of full bathrooms, the number of half baths and the number of showers, and keep those three counts apart. Set the guest count beside the shower count and picture a busy morning. If the number looks tight, ask whether the venue has another building with facilities, and ask what the stay would look like if one bathroom were out of service for a day.

Seating is worth counting too. Pick the main meal or the main evening gathering and ask for the capacity of the largest room as it would be set for your plan, with tables and chairs. A room that holds a crowd standing is a different room from one that seats the same crowd at tables. Ask the venue to confirm the figure for your layout, and write the answer in the booking notes rather than leaving it in an email thread.

Check access before you commit

If anyone in the family uses a wheelchair, a walker or a cane, or has trouble with stairs, put access at the top of the list, not at the bottom. The ADA National Network describes the rule for lodging this way: accessible guest rooms must be held back until all other rooms of that type have been rented.1 It also says the specific accessible guest room reserved must be held for the reserving customer.1 Those statements are about places of lodging. The page does not say how they apply to a private cabin rental or a camp, so ask the venue which of its rooms are accessible and how it holds them for reservations. Whether these rules reach a private cabin rental or a camp is a question for a lawyer.

The same page says places of lodging must identify and describe the accessible features of the property.1 It says customer service staff must be trained to respond to inquiries about features of the facility, including accessible routes to and through the facility, and details about the configuration of accessible guest rooms and bathrooms.1 Use that list as a source of questions. Ask where the step-free route runs from the bedroom to the bathroom and from the bedroom to the dining room. Ask how wide the doorways are and whether there are handrails where a person might need them. Ask about the shower and whether a seat is available.

The page does not tell you how to judge a particular cabin, and it does not give measurements for a family rental. It describes duties of lodging operators. So treat the venue's description as a starting point, and ask for photographs of the route, the bathroom and the entrance. If the main building is accessible and the sleeping cabins are not, ask whether the venue can place the person who needs a ground-floor room in the building that works.

Plan the kitchen and the big meal

A family-size meal is a food safety job as well as a cooking job. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists four steps: clean, separate, cook and chill.2 On cleaning, it says to wash your hands before, during, and after preparing food and before eating.2 The agency does not describe kitchen layouts, so ask what the sink and counter setup looks like and whether there is a second place to wash.

On separating, the agency says raw meat, chicken and other poultry, seafood, and eggs can spread germs to ready-to-eat food unless you keep them separate.2 In a crowded kitchen, think about extra cutting boards, extra plates and a clear spot in the refrigerator for raw items. Ask the venue what it provides, and plan to bring what it does not.

On cooking, the page gives internal temperatures: one hundred forty-five degrees Fahrenheit for whole cuts of beef, veal, lamb and pork, with a three-minute rest before carving or eating; one hundred sixty degrees for ground meats; and one hundred sixty-five degrees for all poultry and for leftovers and casseroles.2 That means somebody needs a food thermometer, and somebody needs to be responsible for using it. Decide who that is before the weekend begins.

On chilling, the page says never to leave perishable food out for more than two hours, or one hour if it is exposed to temperatures above ninety degrees Fahrenheit.2 It says to keep the refrigerator at forty degrees or below and the freezer at zero or below.2 For a reunion, decide who clears and puts away the buffet, and when. It also says to thaw frozen food in the refrigerator, in cold water or in the microwave, and never on the counter.2 If the plan involves a big frozen item, ask how much refrigerator space the kitchen has and whether a second unit is available.

The page does not speak to rental cabins, camp kitchens or catered buffets, so questions about equipment, oven size and who may use the kitchen stay with the venue. Ask whether a caterer may use the space, and whether the venue sets a time by which the kitchen must be cleared.

Ask for the answers in writing

Once you have narrowed the field, send each remaining venue the same list of questions so the replies can be compared side by side. Ask for the number of beds by type, the number of bathrooms and showers, the seating capacity of the largest room for your layout, the accessible route and room details, the kitchen equipment, the refrigerator space and the linen policy. Ask what is included in the price and what costs extra, and ask for the cancellation terms in plain language.

When you choose, ask that the counts and the room assignments be written into the booking agreement. A bed count that lives only in an email thread is easy to lose track of, so put it in the agreement. Share the final plan with the family in one message: who sleeps where, who is bringing linens, who is cooking, who owns the thermometer, and where the accessible route runs. Send it before the weekend and ask each household to reply with corrections.

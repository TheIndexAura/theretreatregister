Family Reunion Organizers: Choose a Lodge, Cabins or a Group Camp

October 30, 2026 · The Retreat Register

When a family reunion lodging plan runs into a house that can’t hold the crowd, the whole schedule can wobble. The first move is to decide whether the group will stay under one roof or split into several cabins, then verify the numbers that matter most.

Ask each candidate by email how many real beds there are, how many bathrooms serve how many guests, whether the kitchen can handle a full‑size feast, and whether one room can seat the whole clan for a toast. Those answers let the organizer match the venue to the family’s size and needs without guessing.

Decide the overall setup

Start by naming the common configurations: a single lodge or a cluster of cabins. A lodge works when the family fits comfortably on one floor and a single dining hall can host everyone. Cabins spread the group across separate structures, providing quiet spaces for older relatives.

A group camp usually offers a central kitchen and a large communal hall, plus multiple sleeping units that can be linked with temporary walkways. The choice hinges on how many people need to sleep together versus how many need privacy. Write down which model feels closest to the family’s rhythm before emailing for details.

Remember that each model brings a different logistical footprint. A lodge may require fewer transport trips, while cabins increase the number of steps between rooms. A camp may need a shuttle service for guests who cannot walk long distances.

Count real beds and bunk capacity

Ask the venue by email for a list of actual beds, not just sleeping mats. Real beds include twin, full, queen, or king sizes that do not need additional mattresses. Bunk rooms should be counted by the number of lower and upper bunks that are safely built.

Write the totals on a sheet and compare them to the number of guests. If the tally falls short, note how many extra air mattresses would be needed and whether the venue permits them. Some places charge extra for supplemental sleeping gear.

When the family spans several generations, consider the comfort level of each age group. Grandparents often prefer a real bed on the ground floor, while teenagers may be fine with a top bunk. Align the bed count with those preferences to avoid last‑minute reshuffling.

Check bathroom ratios

Bathrooms are the next bottleneck after beds. To find the guests per bathroom, divide the guest count by the bathroom count, and keep in mind that the exact ratio depends on how many people use them at the same time.

Ask each property by email how many full bathrooms are available and how many half baths. Also count the showers separately. Count the fixtures, not just the rooms, because a single bathroom may contain two sinks or a separate shower stall.

If the ratio looks tight, ask by email whether the venue can add portable units for the weekend. Some sites allow a temporary restroom trailer as a practical fix for a large gathering.

What the kitchen and common area provide

The kitchen must hold enough counter space and storage for a full family‑size meal plan. Confirm by email whether the stove has at least four burners and the oven can fit a large turkey. Also verify that the fridge has multiple compartments.

In addition to the kitchen, a single dining room or great hall should seat everyone for the main meal. Measure the room’s length and width, then calculate whether a rectangular table for the whole group fits with chairs around it.

If the venue’s kitchen falls short, ask by email whether a catering service is allowed to use a temporary prep area. Some locations provide a separate pantry or a second cooking station that can be unlocked for large events.

Consider accessibility and linens

Older relatives often need step‑free routes from their rooms to the bathroom and dining area. Ask the venue by email about the width of doorways and whether handrails are installed where needed.

Linens are another hidden cost. Find out whether the property supplies sheets and towels, or whether the family must bring them. If the venue requires guests to bring linens, list the exact quantities per bedroom so no one arrives short.

When the venue cannot meet an accessibility need, note the nearest alternative that does. A short shuttle ride to a more accessible building can solve the problem without changing the overall lodging plan.

Rule: If a venue cannot confirm the exact number of usable bathrooms, treat the space as if it has one bathroom fewer than advertised. The extra delay can force guests to wait outside, harming comfort and safety. Apply the rule by subtracting one from the bathroom total and then recompute the guest‑to‑bathroom ratio. If the new ratio looks tight, email a request for a portable unit or look for another site.

On arrival day the parking lot is already half full, the kitchen crew is prepping a three‑hour brunch, and the forecast shows a chance of thunderstorms. Keep the timeline intact and avoid a traffic jam that could strand grandparents in the rain. At this point the organizer must manage vehicle flow, shelter and waste disposal together, because a bottleneck in any one of them ripples through meals, activities and bedtime routines.

To size a shuttle service, start with the goal of matching vehicle capacity to the shortfall in parking spaces. Count the total number of expected cars, multiply that by the average number of people per car, then subtract the number of on‑site spots. The remainder tells how many seats a shuttle must provide. If the result is two hundred seats, divide that by the seats in one vehicle to learn how many trips each round needs. Schedule the first round to depart as soon as the last car parks, and plan a second round before the evening program begins.

Rule: If the site offers fewer parking spaces than half the expected guests, then add a shuttle service; otherwise rely on self‑parking. Reason: Guests with nowhere to park will otherwise block fire lanes, creating a safety hazard and delaying emergency response.

Waste handling often slips the mind until the trash cans overflow and a stray bottle rolls onto the trail. The rule here is simple: allocate enough large bins for the guest count and position a secondary container at each secondary dining area. Reason: Overfilled containers attract wildlife and create slip hazards, and the clean‑up crew ends up working overtime, which eats into the evening program.

Rule: If the venue lacks a designated assembly point for emergencies, then designate a clear, level area near the main entrance and mark it with bright tape; otherwise use the existing fire‑escape stairwell. Reason: In a sudden evacuation a visible, unobstructed spot prevents crowding and keeps the path clear for first‑responders.

Rule: When the sum of confirmed real beds and bunk spots does not meet the total guest count, the planner must add enough sleeping capacity to cover the whole shortfall before the contract is signed.

Reason: Each additional sleeping unit creates a clear, separate space that prevents guests from crowding existing beds. This reduces the risk of nighttime disturbances and keeps the sleeping area organized. Add the required units and note the total before finalizing the contract.

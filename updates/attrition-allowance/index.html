Hotel Attrition Allowance: How Planners Read It Before Signing

October 27, 2026 · The Retreat Register

The hotel attrition allowance is the share of a room block a group may leave unused before the hotel charges for the empty rooms. It sits in the contract as a percentage or a fixed count of rooms that can go dark without penalty.

A planner who skims this line often finds a bill for dozens of unsold nights after the retreat ends. The difference between a per night allowance and a cumulative one decides whether you owe for three empty rooms on the last night or for thirty empty room nights spread across the whole stay.

How the allowance works

The clause sets a threshold. If your block holds twenty rooms for four nights you have eighty room nights in total. A ten percent cumulative allowance lets you leave eight room nights unused across the entire stay. A ten percent per night allowance lets you leave two rooms empty each night without charge.

When the contract says per night the hotel measures shortfall each morning. When it says cumulative the hotel adds up every empty bed at the end. With a cumulative allowance a slow first night can be offset by a full house later.

Read the definition of room night in the same paragraph. Some hotels count a room with one guest as half a room night. Others count any occupied room as full. That distinction changes the math before you even start counting.

Ask for the allowance in writing before you sign. If the sales manager describes it one way and the contract reads another the written version controls.

Reading the clause language

Look for the words attrition allowance or allowable slippage. They mean the same thing. The clause will state a percentage or a flat number of rooms. It will also state whether the measure is per night or cumulative.

Check whether the allowance applies to the original block or to the block after any cutoff date reductions. Some contracts let you shrink the block at a cutoff and then apply the allowance to the smaller number. That reduces your protection.

Watch for a minimum revenue clause tied to the allowance. The hotel may say you can release rooms up to the allowance but only if total revenue stays above a floor. That floor can erase the allowance in practice.

Note any blackout dates or high demand periods excluded from the allowance. A retreat that spans a holiday weekend may find the allowance suspended for those nights.

How to run the numbers

To find your risk start with the total room nights in the block. To find your total room nights, multiply the number of rooms by the number of nights. Then apply the allowance percentage to that total if the clause is cumulative. If the clause is per night multiply the nightly room count by the percentage and then by the number of nights.

Compare the two results. The cumulative method gives a single number of free empty rooms. The per night method gives a nightly cap that resets each morning. With the same percentage, the cumulative method covers at least as many empty rooms as the per night one. It covers more when the empty rooms bunch up, such as a group that shrinks after the first night.

Write the calculation in an email to the hotel contact and ask them to confirm the numbers. Written confirmation creates a record you can reference if the final invoice disagrees.

Pricing in this industry is quote-only by default so the cost of each charged room night will not appear in the contract. Ask for the rate that applies to attrition charges. It is often the group rate but sometimes a higher walk in rate.

Negotiating the terms

Request a cumulative allowance if the contract offers per night. Explain that your attendance pattern is uncertain and a cumulative measure gives fairer protection. A hotel may agree if the overall block is large enough.

Ask for a higher percentage if the retreat has a history of last minute cancellations.

Propose a sliding scale. The allowance grows if you return the block early. For example a ten percent allowance at ninety days out becomes fifteen percent at sixty days. This rewards the hotel for early notice and gives you more room later.

Get every change in an addendum signed by both parties. A verbal agreement on the phone does not override the printed clause.

Edge cases

When a force majeure event cuts attendance the allowance still applies unless the contract carves out an exception. The hotel still holds the rooms and still incurs the cost of turning them over.

If the hotel overbooks and walks your guests the allowance does not increase. Check what the contract says about walked guests before you sign.

When you compare two contracts side by side you will see that one uses cumulative and the other uses per night but both show ten percent. The cumulative version gives you a single bucket of free empty rooms across the stay. The per night version gives you a nightly cap that resets at midnight. If your group tends to shrink after the first night, the cumulative version protects you better, because the bucket can absorb several empty rooms on the same night. If the empty rooms are spread evenly, the two come out the same. A sales manager may not volunteer this difference unless you ask. You will need to read every line twice.

Try it on a smaller block. Ten rooms for three nights gives thirty room nights. A ten percent cumulative allowance gives you three free empty room nights across the stay. A ten percent per night allowance gives you one free empty room each night. Now say two people drop out after the first night, so two rooms sit empty on nights two and three. That is four empty room nights. The cumulative allowance covers three and you pay for one. The per night allowance covers one on each of those two nights and you pay for two. Write the numbers down before you confirm.

You can ask for a sliding scale that rewards early notice. Ten percent at ninety days becomes fifteen percent at sixty days. The hotel gets certainty and you get a larger cushion. A property may agree if you promise a minimum spend on catering. Others may refuse and cite brand policy. The signed addendum is the only proof that matters. Keep a copy in your project folder.

If your block includes multiple room types the allowance applies to the total room night count not to each type separately. The hotel manages inventory as a single pool. A planner who shifts ten king rooms to doubles on the last night may find the allowance consumed by the type change.

When a retreat spans two contracted blocks the allowance does not combine across contracts. Each contract stands alone. You must manage shortfall in each block independently.

Today you can open the contract and circle the attrition allowance line. Write the calculation method in the margin. Send an email to the hotel contact asking them to confirm the method and the rate that applies to any charged rooms.

When the final invoice arrives you will compare the counted empty rooms against the allowance you confirmed in writing. If the numbers match you pay nothing extra. If they do not you have the email trail to dispute the charge.

Planner Guide

Compare booking a retreat through a platform or direct

July 29, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Booking a retreat through a platform or straight with the venue can change the contracting party and the person who answers fee questions. The federal fee rule text defines total price, and a planner can put questions about fees in writing before anyone pays.

Decide which route you are comparing

A retreat planner can reach a property in two ways. One route is a listing site or booking service, where you search dates, pick a property and pay through a screen. The other is a direct inquiry to the venue, perhaps by email to its events contact. Both routes can end with the same rooms and the same weekend, yet they may leave you holding different documents and dealing with different people when something changes.

Before you compare prices, compare the paperwork. Ask each route who the contract is with and who answers if the headcount drops or a session moves. Ask for a named person or an inbox for those changes, and write the answers down next to the quote. Ask each seller for its written terms before you pay, and read them.

Decide what you are buying besides beds. Think through the meeting space and meals you need, then list every need on one page. Hand the same page to each route, so the totals describe the same weekend. If one quote is much lower than the others, check whether it leaves out a room or a meal that the others include.

Read what the federal rule text says

The FTC has a rule on unfair or deceptive fees, published in the Code of Federal Regulations as part four hundred sixty-four of title sixteen. Its definitions section says a covered good or service is live-event tickets or short-term lodging, and it describes short-term lodging as including temporary sleeping accommodations at a hotel, motel, inn, short-term rental, vacation rental, or other place of lodging.1 Rooms for a retreat sit in the lodging half of that list.

The same section says a business is an individual, corporation, partnership, association, or any other entity that offers goods or services, including online, in mobile applications, and in physical locations.1 The text names websites, apps and in-person sales side by side. Whether a particular group booking falls inside the rule is a separate question, taken up below.

The rule section on hidden fees says it is an unfair and deceptive practice for a business to offer, display, or advertise any price of a covered good or service without clearly and conspicuously disclosing the total price.2 The text does not say how large a fee may be. It speaks to disclosure of the total price and of the fees left out of it.

What counts as the total price

The definition is short. Total price means the maximum total of all fees or charges a consumer must pay for any good or service and any mandatory ancillary good or service.1 The text says an ancillary good or service means any additional good or service offered to a consumer as part of the same transaction.1 For a retreat booking, ask the seller to say which charges are mandatory and which are optional, because the definition treats the two differently.

The definition also lists what may be excluded. Government charges, shipping charges, and fees or charges for any optional ancillary good or service may be excluded from the total price.1 Government charges are defined as fees or charges imposed on the transaction by a federal, state, tribal, or local government agency, unit, or department.1 Ask for each of those as its own line, so you can see what sits outside the total.

The hidden fees section adds two duties. In any offer, display, or advertisement that represents any price of a covered good or service, a business must disclose the total price more prominently than any other pricing information.2 The section also says that where the final amount of payment is displayed, that amount must be disclosed more prominently than, or as prominently as, the total price.2

Before the consumer consents to pay, the business must clearly and conspicuously disclose the nature, purpose, and amount of any fee or charge imposed on the transaction that has been excluded from the total price, along with the identity of the good or service for which it is imposed. It must also disclose the final amount of payment.2 The text lists those items and no others. It says nothing about how a negotiated group contract is covered, and the definitions section does not define the word consumer. Ask the venue for its own policy on fees and written totals, and ask a lawyer whether the rule reaches your case.

Questions to ask both routes

Ask about the total. Ask each route for one figure for your dates and headcount that includes every mandatory fee, and ask which lines are taxes and which are optional. If a quote comes back with a base rate and a note that other charges apply, ask for those charges by name and amount. The rule text names the nature and purpose of an excluded fee as well as its amount, so ask the same of any fee line you do not recognize.2

Ask about the contract. Find out whether the agreement names the venue or the booking service as the other party, and read the cancellation terms before you read the nightly rate. If you book through a service, ask whether the venue has its own cancellation terms and ask to see them. If two sets of terms exist, ask a lawyer which one governs when they differ.

Ask about changes after signing. Who accepts a new headcount or a moved session, and how soon do they answer? Ask for the answer in writing, with a name or an inbox. Put the same question to a direct venue and to a booking service, and compare what each one tells you.

Ask about payment. Find out the deposit amount and the date each payment is due, then ask what happens to a deposit if your dates move. Ask whether the final invoice can differ from the quote, and which items could change it. If the answer is vague, ask for it again in an email so that you have it on record.

A comparison you can run this week

Pick two or three options, with at least one from each route if you can. Send the same message to all of them: your dates and how flexible they are, your headcount or a range, the rooms and meals you need, plus the date you need an answer by. If your headcount is not settled, give a range and say so.

When the replies arrive, put them side by side on one sheet. For each option, write the total, the lines that make it up, the taxes and optional items, the deposit, and the cancellation terms, plus the person who handles changes. Mark anything that was missing from a reply, and send a follow-up question for each blank.

Keep every reply with the contract draft. If a later invoice shows a fee that was never mentioned, you have the earlier messages to point to. If the seller and you read a fee line differently, ask the venue what its own policy is. Ask a lawyer whether the federal rule applies to your booking. Bring the sheet to the decision meeting so the group lead chooses with all the totals in view.

Read the first page of the agreement again before anyone signs. Check the party named, the dates and the total, then the list of included rooms and meals, then the cancellation terms. Anything you were told by email that is missing from that page should be added or confirmed in writing before the deposit goes out.

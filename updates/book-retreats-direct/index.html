Booking a retreat through a platform: what it changes and what it does not

July 29, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Pick your booking route by one thing: who you want handling changes after the contract is signed. Platforms, sourcing services and planners each do a different job between you and the venue, and the contract shows which job you are buying. Today, open the draft agreement and read the first page.

Two questions that tell the routes apart

Each route stands between you and the venue for part of the process. What it does there shows up in two plain questions, and you can ask both before you commit to anything.

The first is whose name is on the contract. With some routes the agreement is with the venue. With others it runs through the service you booked with. The party named on the first page is the one you hold to the terms.

The second is who handles a change after signing. A headcount drops, a session moves, the caterer needs a new arrival time. Ask each route in writing who takes those changes and how fast. When a route cannot name a person or an inbox for them, that work is coming to you.

What booking direct gets you

Your contract is with the business that owns the beds, so you know who has to fix a problem on a Friday night. You also reach the people who can change things. Group sales can open the kitchen early or waive a charge, and a quote from them is an opening position that can move on dates and headcount.

You can ask the venue for its insurance certificate yourself. The Texas Department of Insurance describes commercial general liability insurance as covering claims for bodily injury, property damage, and personal and advertising injury, with premises and operations coverage paying for injury or damage on the premises or from the business’s operations.2 That is the coverage that answers a guest slipping in the dining hall. Under the Texas Insurance Code, a certificate may not use terms that would alter, amend, or extend the coverage provided in the policy.2 The certificate reports the policy and cannot add to it, so read the coverages it lists before you sign.

You can also ask for the full price in one number. A federal rule now requires businesses that advertise prices for short-term lodging to show the total price, including mandatory fees, up front.1 The rule bans hiding a mandatory fee from an advertised price. It does not ban charging one, and a privately negotiated group contract with no advertised price sits at the edge of what it covers. Ask for one total either way.

What booking direct leaves to you

Going straight to a venue gets you one venue’s answer. Nobody else is lining up alternatives for your dates, so the comparing is yours to do. If the first venue is full, the search starts again.

Every change after signing is yours too. A new headcount, a moved session or a dietary list goes from your inbox to the venue’s. For a small group with a fixed plan that is light work. For a large group with outside vendors it is a real part of the job, and it is the part a planner takes off your hands.

Writing the first inquiry

Address it to group sales, catering sales or the events office. A general inbox or the front desk is busy with tonight’s guests, and the events team is the one whose job is your event. At a small property that may be the owner, who can agree to things a sales manager cannot.

Put four things in the first email: your dates and how flexible they are, your headcount, whether you need meeting space as well as beds, and the date you need an answer by. Then ask about date flexibility, the buyout threshold, food-and-beverage minimums and service fees. Those four move the real total more than the nightly rate does.

Close the email with one line: “Please send one total for these dates that includes every mandatory fee, tax and service charge.”

When the answers come back

When the contract names the service you booked through, you will want the venue’s own cancellation terms in writing as well. Those still decide what happens at the property.

If your headcount is not firm yet, give a range in the first email. A venue can price a range. It cannot price a number it never heard.

Send the same inquiry to three venues and set the three totals side by side. Ask every follow-up in writing, and keep each reply with the contract draft. The route you book through will matter less than having three answers to the same questions.

Ask Who Runs the Kitchen: Allergen and Inspection Questions for Retreat Planners

August 14, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A retreat venue's menu tells you what it wants to be known for. The person at the stove decides what actually reaches the table. Before you book, ask who runs the kitchen, how a guest's allergy gets from your form to the cook, and which agency inspects the kitchen. Get each answer in writing.

Start with the person, not the menu

Menus are written to be read before a booking. They say braised, foraged, house-made. Then a Saturday arrives with twenty or thirty plates to send out, and the meal is cooked by whoever is working that shift. For a retreat, that difference can matter. Meals are part of every day of the trip, so the kitchen touches every guest. The cook shapes the mood of the whole trip.

So the first question is plain. Who runs the kitchen, and how long have they run it? A kitchen with a clear owner answers in a sentence. A kitchen without one answers in logistics, with talk of a rotating team, an outside caterer or a seasonal hire. Neither answer disqualifies a venue. Each one tells you what you are booking, and it is better to learn that in an email than on the first morning.

One more question belongs in the same message. If the kitchen changes hands or staff between your booking and your dates, who tells you, and when? Someone who has thought about this will have an answer. One that has not may still be honest about it, and you can plan around an honest answer.

Use the federal allergen list as your form

The Food and Drug Administration names nine major food allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame.1 That list is a good skeleton for the dietary form you send to your group. Give each allergen its own tick box, then add an open field for any other allergy and a separate field for medical diets. Ask guests to describe their allergy in their own words, so the kitchen has the detail from the guest.

The same agency lists symptoms that range from hives and an itchy mouth to swelling of the throat, difficulty breathing and loss of consciousness. It calls a severe, life-threatening reaction anaphylaxis, and it says food allergies currently cannot be cured.1 So treat this form as a safety document. A guest who dislikes mushrooms and a guest who reacts to sesame should never share a column.

Keep allergies and preferences apart on the sheet. Vegetarian, no pork and no spice are choices the kitchen can plan around. An allergy is a different kind of instruction, and it should be marked so that nobody on the line mistakes one for the other. Follow up personally with every guest who lists an allergy, because a form answer can be vague and a short conversation can fill the gaps. Then send the complete list to the kitchen in one message, with the guest's first name and the exact words they used.

Ask how an allergy travels to the plate

A list in an inbox does not protect anyone. What matters is the path from that list to the person holding the pan. A few sentences from the venue should describe it. Who reads the list? Is it printed and posted at the station, or does it sit in one person's inbox? Who tells a substitute cook, and what happens on a night when the usual cook is off?

The answers vary a great deal, and the useful ones sound specific. A kitchen that says the chef reads every list the day before, marks the tickets and talks to servers at the start of service says something real. A kitchen that says it can handle anything says very little.

The buffet needs its own question. Shared serving spoons, a salad bar set beside a bowl of nuts and one tray of sauce for everyone are ordinary features of group meals, and each one is a place where food can touch food it should not. If the venue serves family style, ask how a guest with an allergy gets a safe plate.

The FDA uses the term cross-contact for the inadvertent introduction of a major food allergen into a product.1 It is the right word to use in your email, because it names the risk the guest is asking about.

Ask the person who runs the kitchen how cross-contact is prevented on a busy night. Request the steps in plain words, and the name of who follows them. A cook with a routine can describe it in a few lines. A cook without one may talk about the menu instead, and that reply is information too.

Put the question in writing and ask for the answer in writing. You may need the text later, when you brief the group or when a guest asks what you were told. It is also a fair test of how the venue communicates.

Ask which rules the kitchen cooks under

The FDA publishes a model called the Food Code. Local, state, tribal and federal regulators use it as a model, and it covers restaurants, grocery stores and institutions such as nursing homes.2 A model is a starting point for regulators, so find out which rules apply to the venue's own kitchen.

You do not need to read a code to use this. You need two facts. Ask for the name of the agency that inspects the venue's kitchen, and ask for the date of its most recent inspection. See whether the result can be shared with you. If the answer is that the venue is not sure, ask who would know.

An inspection result is one piece of evidence. Read it beside the cook's answers, and ask the venue what has changed since the date of the visit.

A short email you can send

Pull the questions into one message so the venue can answer once. Start with the dates and head count. Then ask who runs the kitchen and how long they have done it. Ask how dietary information moves from your list to the cook and who covers when the usual cook is away. Find out how the kitchen prevents cross-contact. It also helps to ask which agency inspects the kitchen and when it last did. Finish by asking for the replies in writing before you sign.

Compare the answers between venues side by side. A plain sheet with one column per venue works well: who runs the kitchen, how the allergy list reaches the cook, how the kitchen handles cross-contact, which agency inspects it and when it last did. The one that names a person, describes a routine and gives you an agency and a date gives you something to build on. The one that answers every line with reassurance gives you nothing to check. If you are weighing two properties that are otherwise equal, give the clearer kitchen real weight.

Keep the thread. Print or save the replies with your booking papers and share the relevant lines with your group lead. When a guest asks on arrival what has been arranged for them, you will have an answer that came from the kitchen itself.

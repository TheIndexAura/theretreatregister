"Do You Have Wi-Fi?" Is Not Enough for a Company Retreat

August 8, 2026 · The Retreat Register

“Is there Wi-Fi?” It is the simplest question a retreat planner asks, and the answer is almost always yes. A confident nod, a quick affirmation. That yes can still be the villain of your next offsite.

The failure looks the same everywhere. Monday morning, everyone logs on for the working session, and the whole operation grinds to a halt. Buffering screens, dropped calls, and a room full of people watching a spinning circle. The productive morning you paid for is gone by ten.

The problem is not malice. It is a difference in definition. A venue might have Wi-Fi that is genuinely fine for checking email in the main lodge, and no capacity at all for twenty people on separate video calls from separate rooms. Both of those situations are honestly described by the word yes.

What the venue hears, and what you meant

When a sales manager says the property has Wi-Fi, that person is usually answering a guest question. Guests check flights, stream a show, and send photographs to their family. One or two devices, spread across an evening.

You are asking a completely different question. You are asking whether forty laptops, forty phones, a screen share, and a recorded session can all run at once, in the same hour, from rooms spread across a property. Nobody is lying to you. The two of you are talking about two different buildings.

Asking “what happens when our whole group is on video calls at the same time?” changes the conversation from a simple yes to a practical assessment. It also tells you very quickly whether the person on the phone has ever had a group do that.

The number that actually matters

There is a public yardstick you can hold a venue’s answer against, and it comes from the federal government rather than from a router manufacturer.

In March 2024 the Federal Communications Commission raised its benchmark for high-speed fixed broadband to 100 megabits per second down and 20 megabits per second up, which was a four-fold increase on the 25/3 standard it had used since 2015.1 That number describes a household. It is sized for a family, not for a company offsite.

The same FCC report set a separate short-term benchmark for school districts of 1 gigabit per second for every 1,000 students and staff.1 Schools are not retreats, so treat this as a sanity check rather than a rule. Worked through, it comes to roughly 1 megabit per second per person for a building full of people doing ordinary connected work. A forty-person retreat that wants headroom for video is therefore asking about tens of megabits of usable throughput, sustained, at the same moment. If a venue quotes you a number that starts with a small figure and no mention of how many rooms share it, you have learned something.

Two follow-ups turn that into a real answer. Ask what the property’s total internet speed is, and ask whether that connection is shared with guest rooms, the restaurant, and the point-of-sale system. A venue that knows both numbers has thought about this before. A venue that only says “it is fast” has not.

Where you are going matters more than what they say

Retreat venues sit where the scenery is, and the scenery is usually rural. That is the point of the trip, and it is also the risk.

Using its Broadband Data Collection deployment data, the FCC reported that as of December 2022, fixed terrestrial broadband service excluding satellite had not been physically deployed to roughly 24 million Americans, including almost 28% of Americans in rural areas.1 Close to three in ten rural addresses had no wired service at the federal high-speed standard at all.

That is not a reason to avoid rural venues. It is a reason to ask a rural venue a harder version of the question, and to ask it earlier. A lodge on satellite service can run a very good retreat. It cannot run forty simultaneous video calls, and you would rather know that in March than on the Monday morning.

Why this question got sharper

The reason a bad connection now ruins a retreat is that the people in the room have spent the rest of the year working online.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that in 2025, 35% of employed people did some or all of their work at home on days they worked.2 Among employed people with a bachelor’s degree or higher, the figure was 51%, compared with 19% for those with a high school diploma and no college.2 The population most likely to be sent on a company retreat is the population most used to working from a laptop wherever it happens to be.

Those people arrive with expectations built by a year of home broadband. When the connection fails, they do not adjust. They lose the morning.

Six questions to send before you sign

Copy these into an email and send them to the venue’s group contact. The answers are worth more than a site visit.

  1. What is the total download and upload speed of the property’s internet connection?
  2. Is that connection shared with guest rooms, the restaurant, and the office systems, or does the meeting space have its own line?
  3. What is the largest group you have hosted that ran video calls from several rooms at once, and how did it go?
  4. Which buildings and rooms have coverage, and which do not?
  5. Is there a wired connection available in the main meeting room?
  6. Who do we call at 8am on a Monday if the connection is down, and how quickly does that person get there?

What a good answer sounds like

A good answer contains numbers and a name. “The property runs on a 500 down, 100 up fiber circuit. The conference wing is on a separate access point, and there is a wired port at the podium. We had a 60-person insurance group in April running two breakout video sessions, and it held. Our IT contractor is Dale, he is twenty minutes away, and his number is on the sheet in every meeting room.”

What a bad answer sounds like

A bad answer contains reassurance and no measurements. “Yes, we have excellent Wi-Fi throughout the property, and guests never have any problems.” That sentence would be equally true of a venue with a domestic broadband line and one router in the lobby. If you press once and get the same reassurance back, treat the connection as unknown.

The twenty-minute test on a site visit

If you tour the property, run this before you leave. It costs nothing and it settles the argument.

  • Stand in the room where the working session will happen. Run a speed test on your phone. Write the result down with the time of day.
  • Walk to the furthest bedroom, the breakout barn, and the outdoor space someone will inevitably want to use. Run the test again in each one.
  • Start a video call with a colleague and walk the route people will walk between sessions. Note where it drops.
  • Ask the front desk to tell you how many guests are in-house right now. A speed test at 30% occupancy tells you very little about a full house.

What to ask for in writing

Contracts rarely say anything about the connection, which is exactly why asking is useful. You are not asking the venue to promise a speed. You are asking for a written description you can point at.

Ask the venue to add a short paragraph to the agreement stating the internet service being provided, whether the meeting space has dedicated bandwidth, and who to contact for support during the event. A venue that is comfortable writing that down is telling you the connection is real. A venue that will describe it on the phone and not in the contract is telling you something too.

Dead zones are not always a defect

Here is the part planners skip. You do not need coverage everywhere, and the better retreats do not want it.

Decide on purpose which parts of your retreat need a solid connection and which do not. The working session needs it. The recorded all-hands needs it. The walk after lunch, the dinner, and the fire pit do not. A barn with no signal is a problem if you scheduled a breakout in it and an asset if you scheduled the conversation people have been avoiding all year.

Map your agenda against the property’s coverage before you build the schedule rather than after. Connected hubs and quiet corners are both design choices when you pick them, and both accidents when you do not.

What the register checks, and what it does not

The Retreat Register screens for essentials before listing a venue, focusing first on a direct phone number and dedicated meeting or lodging space.3 Wi-Fi capacity is not part of that screening, and there is no published measurement of it for any venue on the list.

That is why the phone number matters here. On July 15, 2026 the register held 2,210 curated retreat venues, and 2,002 of them listed a direct phone number, which is 91%.4 The six questions above are a fifteen-minute phone call, and for nine venues in ten the number to call is already on the listing.

Do not only ask whether there is Wi-Fi. Ask how much, and where, and who fixes it on a Monday morning.

Define What a Corporate Retreat Is Before You Search for Venues

October 4, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Before anyone opens a venue list, a corporate retreat needs a definition that fits the group: a written goal, a length, a guest list and a set of questions for each venue.

Start with a sentence the retreat has to back up

An email arrives. It says plan the company retreat and nothing else. There is no budget and no date. Before you open a single venue listing, you need a working definition of what this retreat is for. Neither the government order nor the university guide defines the term corporate retreat, so the definition has to come from the people who asked for it.

The University of Minnesota Extension has a guide on meeting agendas, and its first step applies here: "First, define results."1 The guide says to write down a set of clear goals.1 The same first question applies to a retreat. Write one sentence that says what the group should leave with, such as a draft plan for the coming year or an agreed way of handing work between two teams.

Send that sentence to the people who gave you the assignment and ask them to confirm it or rewrite it. Keep their answer in writing. It matters later. Without a stated purpose, any venue can look reasonable, so write the purpose first. If the sentence changes later, you will have the earlier version to show what moved.

The same guide adds a warning that is worth taking seriously. It says that if there is not a defined purpose, consider whether there really is a reason to meet.1 For a planner, that is permission to go back to the stakeholders and ask for more before spending anyone's time on a search.

Ask whether the group needs to be in one place

Some retreats are called for a reason that could be handled in a shorter, cheaper way. The General Services Administration has an order on conference and event management for its own offices. It says that attendance should be limited to those necessary to accomplish the purpose of the event.2 It also says its heads of services and staff offices must first consider less expensive methods, such as teleconferences or virtual meetings.2 The order does not say when a virtual meeting should replace an in-person one, so that decision stays with you and your stakeholders.

That order governs one agency. It is no rule for private companies. Still, the questions it raises are useful to any organizer. Could part of the agenda be done in a normal workweek? Does every person on the invitation list need to be in the room for the goal you wrote down? Decide who has to attend and who can receive a summary afterward.

The order also says that, when there is a broad range of subjects requiring extensive group interaction over a period of days, the most effective approach may be to meet in one location.2 That is the condition the order gives, so check your own agenda against it. Read your goal sentence and decide whether the work needs the whole group in one room beyond a short virtual session. If the goal is one decision that two people could make, ask whether a retreat is the right tool at all.

Notice what the order does with the word retreat itself. In its list of definitions, the training entry gives examples, and "Organizational retreats and team-building exercises" is one of them.2 That is how one agency classifies such events in its own order. It is not a general definition, and it does not tell a private company how to classify its own gathering.

Turn the goal into a length and a topic list

Once the sentence is agreed, the Minnesota guide gives a useful order of work. After defining results, it says to decide on the time frame, and it adds that different time frames fit different purposes.1 It says to match the amount of time to the needs and the frequency of the group's gathering.1 Ask how often this group already gathers, and set the length with that answer in hand.

The guide then says to list the topics that need to be covered to accomplish the results, in a logical order, and then to assign realistic time slots to each item.1 For a retreat, decide in advance what happens to the topic that runs long, and pick a person who can say the group will take it up another day.

The guide also says to plan participation strategies for each topic, since different methods of discussion make the best use of what participants offer.1 It asks organizers to be clear about expectations for involving participants, and says that involvement builds trust and authentic action.1 Tell invitees ahead of time whether they will present or only listen, so each person knows what to prepare.

The last two steps in the guide are short. One is to plan time for meeting closure, where you review what needs to happen next and, perhaps, ask for feedback on the meeting.1 The other is a reality check, which asks whether the agenda is really doable in the time available.1 Run both on your draft. Do it before you contact any venue.

Let the goal shape what you ask a venue

With a goal and a topic list in hand, the venue search has something to compare against. The GSA order names several factors for choosing a location, among them removal from workplace distractions, availability of facilities such as space, equipment and supplies, and speaker fees.2 Treat them as a prompt for your own list, and add what your group needs, such as travel time for the people coming furthest. The order is no standard you must meet.

The same order says planners should consider meeting around, rather than during, a site's peak season, if possible, in order to obtain a lower available rate.2 The order is about its own offices, and a venue sets its own rates. If your dates are flexible, ask each venue which weeks it treats as peak, and ask for quotes on more than one set of dates.

Several questions come straight from your goal sentence rather than from any agency. If the goal is planning, ask about wall space and a projector that works without a technician. If the goal is repairing trust after a hard year, ask about small rooms with doors that close and about where people can step outside. If the goal is getting to know new colleagues, ask about shared meals and a room where the whole group can sit together. Put each of these to the venue directly, and ask what the venue has seen work for groups with the same goal. Ask the sales contact to describe the rooms and the grounds in their own words, since the venue answers for its own facts.

Put the goal sentence at the top of every message you send to a venue. Then ask the sales contact to say in writing whether the space suits that goal.

Write the request down before you search

The GSA order lists items for an event request there, among them the purpose or objective of the event and a proposed location with a justification for the event.2 Your company may not require a request form. Even so, a one-page brief with the purpose and the proposed location, plus the dates and the expected costs your stakeholders want to see, lets them answer in writing.

The order also says that the location selected should be justified by an analysis of costs and benefits, to determine that the session will accomplish its purpose at reasonable cost.2 The order states that for government spending. You can borrow the habit without any form. Next to each finalist venue, write the goal sentence and one line on how that venue helps reach it. Then list the quoted costs, so the comparison uses the same terms for each place.

Neither source says anything about the legal or tax treatment of a private company's retreat. If your finance team has questions about how the expense is treated for tax, put them to an accountant who knows your case.

Today, you can open a blank document and write the goal sentence for the next retreat on your calendar. Send it to the person who made the request and keep the venue list closed until a written reply comes back.

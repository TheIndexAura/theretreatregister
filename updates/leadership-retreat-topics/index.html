Choose Leadership Retreat Topics the Whole Team Must Decide

October 24, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Only a leadership team can settle a few topics together, so pick those. State the purpose first and give each topic an outcome. Sort every item as information, discussion or decision, then trim the list until it fits your days.

Start with the purpose, then the list

Hallways collect coats, and leadership retreats collect topics. Picture a budget question going on the list, then a reorganization idea, until the agenda grows long with no reason behind each line. University of Minnesota Extension starts a level higher. To convene a meeting that makes progress, it says, you take time beforehand to clarify its purpose and plan an agenda.1 So ask yourself if the retreat has a purpose you can state. If it has none, ask whether a meeting is needed at all.

Write that purpose as one sentence before any topic gets a line. It should name the decision or direction the group is meant to leave with. Cannot write it? A topic list will not rescue you, and finding that out before anyone books travel is cheap.

The same page puts a second question to every item: which outcome is wanted for each topic.1 It also says to write down a set of clear goals.1 Put an outcome beside each proposed topic, in plain words. "Decide whether to enter the new market" is a topic with an outcome. "Market discussion" names a subject and stops there, so ask what the group should hold in hand when the session ends. Short test, easy to run.

The page adds that when you lead a meeting, planning the agenda is your responsibility.1 If somebody else is convening the retreat, ask those conveners what they want from it before you draft anything. Write their answers next to the purpose sentence.

Sort each topic by what it needs

A second extension source offers a sorting test. It starts from a facilitation agenda that gives clear guidance on the individual items that need attention.2 For each item it asks whether the agenda item needs dissemination, discussion, or decision.2 The article names these the three Ds and says they help determine what type of tool to use.2

Run your list through that test, item by item. Here is one plain reading of the words. Dissemination is information the group only has to receive, so it may never need the whole leadership team in a room; a written update can reach people before anyone travels. Discussion means thinking aloud together. A decision is a choice the group must make and own. Which of the three deserves retreat hours is up to you as the planner, and a reasonable reading for a leadership offsite is to guard the hours for discussion and decision while moving dissemination out of the schedule.

Now ask who has to be there. LeBlanc and Nosik, in their research article on planning and leading meetings, say to limit invitations to participants who have a contribution to make to agenda items.3 Take it as a prompt for your own planning. Is each invitee needed for the whole retreat, or only for certain topics? Settle with the organizer how a part-time attendee would be handled. Maybe a topic needs two of the leaders and a specialist. Then ask if it belongs at the retreat or in a smaller session beforehand.

Vote the list down

Still too long? Let the group cut it. The Minnesota agenda page describes a priority-setting method: list all the ideas and give participants votes, for example, each participant could place three votes among six-to-eight ideas.1 The page gives this as an example, so those numbers are no rule.

Run the vote before the retreat if you can. A short survey sent to the leadership team lets everyone mark the topics they think need the whole group. You then arrive holding a ranking.

A vote shows where attention sits. It does not show who has to decide. After the count, go back through the top items and put the sorting questions again. An interesting topic may have piled up votes and still be information that belongs in a pre-read. An uncomfortable topic may have drawn few votes and be the very one the retreat was meant for. Decide what to do about the gap between popularity and need, and write the reason down so the people whose topic was cut can read it.

Fit the topics to the hours

Minnesota Extension asks how much time is needed to achieve the meeting's purpose.1 It also says to decide on a logical order for discussing the topics or making decisions.1 The Eight Ps article puts a related question: how much time each item needs, and what tools can be used effectively for the allocated time.2

LeBlanc and Nosik say an agenda can list a time estimate for completion of each task, and they describe building one as identifying the items, ordering them and estimating the duration of each.3 One of their examples is a critical decision that requires discussion, which might occur at the beginning of the agenda.3 Another concerns transition time: a one-hour meeting should hold fifty minutes of content at the most.3 They also say the close should represent about ten percent of the meeting time.3 Treat those figures as prompts for your own planning. Leave room at the end of each session for closing. Do not fill every minute with content.

Then do the arithmetic honestly. Count the days at the venue, subtract meals, travel and the time people need to arrive, and divide what is left among the topics that survived your filters. Say a topic wants a long block and only a short one remains. Ask whether to cut that topic, shrink it, or move it to a session before the retreat. Decide before you travel what happens when a discussion runs past its block, and who keeps the group to the schedule.

The Eight Ps article also lists Product, which concerns what is produced as a result of the meeting, and Place, which asks you to think through what type of space the process needs.2 Ask the venue if a room can be set for plenary discussion and for smaller groups, and if the space can stay set across the days.

Send the agenda and plan the follow-up

Minnesota Extension says that sending an agenda before a meeting lets participants know what is to be discussed.1 LeBlanc and Nosik cite a recommendation of sharing an agenda twenty-four hours or more in advance, and they say participants should review any materials or complete any assigned tasks before the meeting.3 A retreat with travel sensibly gets an earlier date, since people need time to read and prepare. Pick the date yourself, and list on the agenda what each person should read.

Plan the end before the start. If a follow-up meeting is required, LeBlanc and Nosik say, you schedule the next meeting before dismissing the current meeting.3 Notes, they add, should be distributed within twenty-four to forty-eight hours of the end of the meeting, and assigned tasks should be linked to a designated person.3

Rehearse the schedule too. The Eight Ps article lists Practice, described as visualizing and rehearsing the meeting and anticipating potential problems.2 Walk through each session in your head and ask what happens if a topic runs long. Decide in advance which session gives up time, and who makes that choice. Then send the agenda to the team.

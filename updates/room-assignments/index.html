Decide Shared Rooms or Singles Before You Shortlist a Venue

September 4, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Decide who shares a room and who gets a single before any venue goes on the shortlist. Write the rule, collect guest needs in writing, and read what the ADA National Network says about accessible lodging.

Write the sleeping rule before you search

Room assignments are easy to leave for last, and a group can end up at a lovely site whose only large rooms are bunk rooms. Settling the rule first avoids that. Decide whether guests share at all, and if they do, who may be paired and who gets a single regardless of the budget. Put the rule in a short note that the planning team can read and agree to.

Start with the categories of guests you actually have. Couples, friends who travel together, colleagues who barely know each other, guests with health needs, and guests who simply sleep badly around other people all raise different questions. For each category, write down whether sharing is allowed, whether the organizer pays for a single, and who decides when two guests disagree. Leave nothing to be settled at the front desk on arrival day.

Then work out what the rule costs. Ask venues for the nightly rate for a single and for a shared room, and multiply the difference by the number of guests the rule says must have a single. If the total is more than the budget allows, change the rule now, while it is still a note and not a contract. It is far easier to adjust a paragraph than to rebook a venue.

Ask guests in writing

Once the rule exists, collect what each guest needs. A short written form works better than a hallway conversation, because answers can be counted and compared later. Ask whether the guest is willing to share, whether they have a preferred roommate, and whether anything about their health or routine makes a shared room hard. Set a reply date and say clearly that the answers are used only for room planning.

Keep the form short and keep sensitive answers apart. Medical details do not belong in the main spreadsheet that the whole planning team opens. Give each guest a way to say that they would rather discuss something privately, and decide in advance who on the team reads those replies. A guest who has to explain a condition to a committee may decide to say nothing, so make the private route easy to find.

From the replies, build two counts: guests who need a single and guests open to sharing. Those two numbers are what you compare against each venue's room list. If a guest is open to sharing only with a named person, add a column for pairings and check that every pair is mutual before you rely on it.

Ask about sleep before you pair anyone

Sleep is one thing room assignments affect, so ask about it directly. Nobody can tell from a registration list who snores, so ask yourself what a guest who is awake half the night next to a loud sleeper would be able to bring to the morning session. Add a few plain questions to the form. Does the guest need a dark room, a cool room, or a quiet one? Does the guest go to bed early or late? Does the guest need a lamp or a device on overnight?

Pair guests by their answers, not by who happens to be listed next to each other. Someone who rises at dawn and someone who stays up late make a poor match even if they get along well at dinner. When two guests give conflicting answers and neither wants to move, the rule you wrote at the start tells you who decides. Use it, and tell both guests the outcome by the date you set.

Ask the venue about the rooms themselves. Is the thermostat set for each room or for the whole floor? Do the walls between rooms carry sound? Does a late bar, a road or a loading area sit under the windows? Are the beds in a shared room a double or two separate beds? These are questions for the venue, and the answers belong in writing next to the quote. Decide whether the agenda leaves guests a reasonable bedtime, since a schedule that runs late and starts early works against every room arrangement.

If the group includes guests who sleep poorly, consider offering a few singles at a cost split that the group has agreed in advance. That is a planning choice, and no outside page can make it for you. Decide it from your own form answers and your own budget.

Accessible rooms and the ADA National Network fact sheet

If any guest needs an accessible room, the rooms and the sharing rule have to be planned together. The ADA National Network's accessible lodging fact sheet explains revised rules from the Department of Justice under Title III of the ADA, which it says covers many types of private businesses, and it says many of the revisions apply to places of lodging.1 It lists hotels, motels and inns as places of lodging, along with other facilities that offer sleeping rooms for short-term stays and meet certain conditions.1 It also lists an exception for small facilities where the proprietor actually resides, and it gives a room limit for that exception, so ask the venue about its room count and whether a resident proprietor lives on the property, and leave the legal question of whether the exception applies to a lawyer.1

For the buildings themselves, the fact sheet has a list under the heading "Minimum requirements" that includes accessible guest rooms with mobility and communication features dispersed among the different categories or classes of guest rooms available in the facility.1 For existing buildings that are not being altered, it says owners still have an obligation to remove barriers to the extent that is readily achievable.1 Ask the venue about the property facts that matter here, such as when it was built and whether it has been altered, and leave the legal classification to a lawyer.

The fact sheet also describes reservations. It says places of lodging must identify and describe the accessible features of the property and the accessible guest rooms in enough detail to enable an individual to decide if the facility will meet his or her needs.1 It says people with disabilities must be able to make reservations for accessible guest rooms during the same hours and in the same ways that other people are able to make reservations.1 Ask each venue for that description in writing, including the layout of the room and the bathroom, and compare it with what your guest told you.

Two more lines from the fact sheet matter for timing. It says accessible guest rooms must be held back until all other rooms of that type have been rented, and that when a reservation is made for an accessible guest room, the specific room reserved must be held for the reserving customer and removed from the reservation system.1 The page does not say how long a venue keeps such a room open or what happens when a group books late, so ask the venue how it handles both. Name the room in your booking and keep the confirmation.

The fact sheet also says customer service staff must be trained to respond to specific inquiries about the features of the facility, including details about the configuration of accessible guest rooms and bathrooms.1 Put that to use. Send the venue a list of exactly what your guest told you about bed arrangement, bathroom layout and any equipment, and ask for answers room by room. The fact sheet does not mention sharing a room or traveling with a companion, so it gives no answer on whether two guests can share an accessible room. The venue answers for its own policy on that, so ask it.

The fact sheet is a summary of regulations, and it does not decide how those regulations apply to your venue and your group. For a question about the law in a particular case, ask a lawyer. For what the property offers and how its booking works, ask the venue.

Match the rule to the room list

Now the search can begin. Send each venue the same request: how many rooms of each type it has, how many beds are in each, which rooms are accessible, and whether any room can be used as a single or a double. Ask for a floor plan or room list in writing, and ask which rooms sit next to each other. Compare the answers with your two counts.

Count honestly. A bunk room with six beds is not six singles. Ask what the rate is when a double room holds one guest, and ask whether the venue holds a few rooms back for late changes. Decide how much slack you want, because guests cancel and guests change their minds about sharing.

When a venue fits, send the sharing rule back to it in writing and ask the venue to confirm that it can meet it. Keep the reply with the quote. Ask the venue whether the named rooms, especially any accessible room, can go into the booking agreement, and ask a lawyer what the agreement does with promises made earlier in email. Then send guests their assignments with a date by which they can ask for a change.

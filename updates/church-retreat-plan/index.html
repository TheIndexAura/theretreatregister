How to Plan a Church Retreat: Protect Minors and Volunteers First

November 10, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Someone is thumbing through a printed schedule while a line of volunteers waits by the reception desk. The planner’s eyes flick between the arrival list and the clock, aware that a delayed lunch will push the first breakout session into the fire‑drill time slot.

Plan a church retreat around the people it must protect: name a coordinator and collect permission forms for minors. Confirm the venue’s insurance in writing. That answer frames how to plan a church retreat with safety at the core.

Name a safety coordinator

Start by assigning one adult who has experience handling emergency procedures.

The coordinator should have a current first‑aid certification.

They should be comfortable speaking with local authorities.

List the coordinator’s name on every printed agenda.

Volunteers then know who to approach if a question arises.

Make a note of the coordinator’s email for all written confirmations.

Give the coordinator a checklist.

It includes the items in the next sections.

The checklist becomes the reference point for the entire planning team.

Keep the checklist in a shared cloud folder.

It can be printed on site.

When the coordinator signs off on a venue, request a copy of the venue’s liability insurance policy.

The request should be phrased as an email asking for a PDF attachment.

Save the response alongside the contract.

This step prevents the surprise of discovering a missing rider after guests have arrived.

The coordinator also collects emergency contact numbers for each participant.

A simple spreadsheet with name, phone, and any medical notes suffices.

Store that spreadsheet in a secure but accessible location.

Use an encrypted drive that the coordinator can open on a tablet during the retreat.

Secure written permissions

Gather signed permission forms for every minor attending the retreat.

The forms must include a release of liability and a medical consent.

A parental contact section is also required.

Send the forms by email as editable PDFs.

Ask parents to return the completed files as PDFs attached to a reply.

Set a deadline that is two weeks before departure.

The deadline gives the coordinator time to review each form for completeness.

It also allows follow‑up on any missing signatures.

Use a polite reminder that mentions the deadline.

Keep a log of received forms.

When a form is missing a signature, forward it to the parent.

Include a brief note asking for the missing piece.

Do not call the parent; keep the exchange in writing for later reference.

Verify venue safety details

Ask the venue for a copy of its emergency‑evacuation plan.

The plan should show exit routes and assembly points.

It must also indicate the location of fire extinguishers.

Compare the plan to the layout of the rooms you have booked.

Note any discrepancies.

Check that the venue’s capacity limits match the number of attendees.

If the venue can host one hundred guests but you have one hundred and ten confirmed, you must reduce the headcount.

Otherwise, find additional space.

This check avoids overcrowding and the need for last‑minute room changes.

Confirm that the venue provides first‑aid supplies.

Make sure a staff member is trained to use them.

If the venue does not meet that standard, arrange for a volunteer with first‑aid training to bring a kit.

By collecting permission forms early, you reduce paperwork later.

Pricing in this industry is quote‑only by default.

Request a written quote that breaks down room rental and catering.

Include any additional service fees.

Keep the quote in the same folder as the insurance document for easy cross‑reference.

Make a note of the nearest hospital and the contact number for the local ambulance service.

Write that information on the back of the agenda so every participant can see it if needed.

Plan transportation and lodging logistics

When arranging buses, request the driver’s license copy and the vehicle’s safety inspection report.

Keep those documents in the transportation subfolder of the shared drive.

Assign sleeping rooms based on gender and age, following any church policy on rooming.

Write the room assignments on a printed sheet that the lodging coordinator can hand out at check‑in.

Calculate the bathroom‑to‑guest ratio by dividing the total number of guests by the number of bathrooms.

Aim for no more than four guests per bathroom to avoid long lines during peak times.

Prepare a short briefing for the bus driver.

Include the drop‑off point and the pick‑up time.

Add any special instructions for participants with mobility needs.

Deliver the briefing in an email that the driver can forward to the assistant.

Ask the venue if they provide wheelchair‑accessible routes and restrooms.

If they do not, note that in the risk assessment.

Consider an alternate location.

Document and confirm everything in writing

After the venue accepts the contract, send a confirmation email.

Repeat the key dates, the total headcount, and the insurance verification.

Keep a copy of that email in the main folder.

Compile all signed permission forms, insurance PDFs, venue quotes, and transportation documents into a single zip file.

Name the file Retreat_Safety_Documents.zip and store it in the cloud drive.

Send a final reminder to all participants two days before departure.

Attach the agenda, the emergency‑evacuation map, and the contact list for the safety coordinator.

The reminder should also note the dress code for any outdoor activities.

This prevents anyone showing up in unsuitable footwear.

On the day of departure, the coordinator should review the zip file on a tablet.

Confirm that every required document is present.

A quick visual scan at the start saves the need for a frantic search later.

After the retreat, archive the safety folder for at least one year.

The archive serves as a reference if the same venue is considered for a future event.

It provides evidence of due diligence should any question arise later.

The planner watches the line of families grow, each child clutching a backpack, each parent checking a watch. A bottleneck forms at the check‑in table, because the volunteer team has only two hands for thirty signatures.

To find the bathroom‑to‑guest ratio, start with the goal of no more than four guests per restroom. Take the total headcount, subtract the number of infants who will use a changing station, then divide that result by the count of available bathrooms. If the final figure exceeds four, add a portable unit or rearrange the sleeping assignments until the ratio drops below the target. Write the calculation on a sticky note and tape it to the coordinator’s tablet, so the number is visible during the final walk‑through. When the coordinator reviews the numbers on the tablet, a quick glance confirms that the ratio meets the guideline without having to reach anyone for clarification. The note becomes a quick reference when the driver asks about restroom breaks.

When the final walk‑through shows a missing portable unit, the planner spots the gap and emails the rental service before the first bus arrives. The extra unit rolls in just as the first group steps off the van, and the line dissolves before anyone feels the pressure of waiting.

When the final bus pulls away and the last guest signs out, the planner feels a quiet relief.

Every safety box was checked.

Today, you can name a coordinator.

Email the permission forms and request the venue’s insurance copy.

The three actions set the foundation for a safe and orderly church retreat.

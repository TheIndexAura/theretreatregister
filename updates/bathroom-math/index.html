Check How Many Bathrooms a Retreat Group Really Needs

September 13, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Morning bathroom lines can sour a retreat before the first session begins. Before shortlisting a venue, ask for separate counts of toilets, showers and sinks, and compare them with your head count.

Why a bed count tells you so little

A listing may state how many people can sleep in a house or lodge without saying how many of them would be waiting outside one shower at seven in the morning. Before you shortlist a venue, ask about that second number and find out what bathroom fixtures sit behind the sleeping capacity.

One published example is a United States workplace rule, and it covers places of employment. A planner can use it as a reference point and decide where the group sits above or below it.

Start with a simple head count of the people who will actually be present. Count staff, facilitators and any guests who arrive for only part of the program. Decide whether the group will fill every bed or only part of the house, and ask the venue for fixture counts that match that number.

What the workplace sanitation rule lists

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration publishes a sanitation standard for places of employment. It says that toilet facilities, in toilet rooms separate for each sex, shall be provided in all places of employment in accordance with its table, and the table ties the minimum number of water closets to the number of employees.1 The table gives a minimum number of water closets for each bracket of employees, and the text adds one fixture for each additional forty employees.1

The same text adds a detail that matters for a lodge. Each water closet shall occupy a separate compartment with a door and walls or partitions between fixtures sufficiently high to assure privacy.1

This is a rule about employees in places of employment. The text also lists an exception for mobile crews and normally unattended work locations. Ask a lawyer whether any rule applies to your event, and ask the venue about its own bathrooms and policies.

Sinks, soap and drying

Toilets are only half of the picture. The OSHA text says lavatories shall be made available in all places of employment.1 It says hand soap or similar cleansing agents shall be provided.1 It also says washing facilities shall be maintained in a sanitary condition.1

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists using the toilet among the times to wash hands, and it also gives a scrub time for washing hands.2 The agency says that washing hands can keep you healthy and prevent the spread of respiratory and diarrheal infections.2

For a retreat that serves meals in a shared house, check that every bathroom has a sink and soap, and think about how the group will dry its hands. When you ask a venue about its bathrooms, ask about the sinks as well, and ask who restocks soap and towels during a multi-day stay. If the venue relies on hand sanitizer in some spaces, the CDC page says that when soap and water are not readily available, an alcohol-based hand sanitizer with a stated minimum alcohol content can be used, and that the product label shows it.2

Questions to put to the venue

Ask for the number of toilets, the number of showers and the number of sinks as three separate figures. A listing that says "three bathrooms" may mean three rooms with everything, or two rooms with everything and one with only a toilet. Ask which of them can be locked from the inside, since a single-occupancy room may suit a morning rush differently than a room with several stalls. Ask how far each bathroom is from each sleeping room, and whether any bathroom sits in a space guests must pass through, such as a kitchen or another group's bedroom.

Ask which bathrooms are on which floor. A house with all its fixtures on one level may leave a group on another level with a long walk at night. Ask whether any bathroom is shared with the host's own quarters, and whether any is reserved for staff. Ask about hot water as well: how many showers can run at once before the water goes cold, and how long the tank takes to recover. Ask what the venue does when the group wants a second round of showers in the same morning.

Accessibility needs its own question. The federal ADA Standards for Accessible Design include sections titled Toilet and Bathing Rooms and Water Closets and Toilet Compartments.3 Ask a lawyer whether and how they apply to a given venue. If any guest uses a wheelchair or a walker, ask the venue to describe the bathroom plainly: the width of the door, the space inside, the shower type and any grab bars. Ask for photographs, and ask whether the accessible bathroom is on the same level as the sleeping room that guest will use.

Turning the answers into a morning plan

Once you have the counts, sort the venues by how crowded the mornings will feel. Compare the number of guests with the number of toilets and the number of showers, and compare each result with the group you are planning for. Ask the group lead how much waiting the attendees would accept, and write that comfort level down so that you can apply it to every venue in the same way.

Build the schedule around the fixtures you have. If showers are few, stagger the first session by group so that not everyone needs the bathroom at the same minute. Offer breakfast in two waves. Put the earliest activity on a day when guests choose how early to rise. Tell guests at the start whether the showers are less busy in the evening, and let people with early flights or early meetings go first.

Ask the venue to walk you through the bathrooms by video or in person before you sign anything. Count the fixtures yourself and compare your count with the listing. Note which doors lock, which sinks have soap, and where the nearest toilet sits to each sleeping room. Write the figures in one shared document that everyone helping you plan can read, so that a second organizer can check them without starting over. Ask the venue whether other groups will stay at the same time and, if so, whether they would share your bathrooms.

Plan breaks long enough for a line to clear. Ask the venue whether a portable unit can be placed outdoors and what the rules are for adding one. Do not count on an extra unit until the venue has agreed to it in writing.

Put the bathroom details into the booking paperwork. Write down the number of toilets, showers and sinks, which of them are accessible, and who supplies soap and towels. If the venue later changes the layout or takes a bathroom out of use for repairs, you will have a written starting point for a conversation. Then send each shortlisted venue a short message asking it to confirm the figures, and keep the replies with your other venue notes. If a reply differs from the listing, ask the venue which one is correct and have the answer written down before you compare venues again.

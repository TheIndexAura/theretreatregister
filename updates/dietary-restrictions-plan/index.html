Collect Dietary Restrictions for a Retreat: A Form and Kitchen List for Planners

September 6, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Collect every guest's dietary needs once, in writing, at registration, and keep allergies apart from preferences on the form. Then send the venue kitchen a single list with each name, the food that person must avoid and how serious it is. Send it early enough for the chef to change the menu and the food order.

Start from the nine allergens on the label

Dietary trouble at a retreat can start with information that arrives in pieces. One guest mentions an allergy in a reply to the whole group. Another tells a colleague and assumes the venue already knows. The kitchen gets half a list on the morning of arrival, after the chef has already bought the food.

One form with the same questions for everyone closes that gap. Build it around the list the Food and Drug Administration uses. The agency names nine major food allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame.1 Sesame is the most recent addition.2

Put those nine on the form as checkboxes, with a free line under them for anything else. People react to foods outside the list as well, and the free line catches those. The checkboxes help for a plainer reason. They use the same words as food labels and suppliers' sheets, so a chef can match a guest's answer to an ingredient list without a round of follow-up questions.

The FDA describes reactions that range from mild symptoms such as hives and lip swelling to a severe, life-threatening reaction called anaphylaxis.1 A form cannot tell you which kind a guest has. Let the guest tell you, in their own words.

Build a form that keeps allergies apart from preferences

Several kinds of answer arrive under the heading dietary needs, and a kitchen treats them very differently. An allergy can make someone ill from a trace. A medical diet, such as the one for celiac disease, has strict rules of its own. A preference, such as vegetarian or no pork, matters to the guest without carrying the same risk.

Give each kind its own section. Under allergies, ask for the food, the reaction the guest has had before, and whether a trace on a shared board or pan is a problem for them. The FDA's word for that trace is cross-contact, meaning a major food allergen gets into a food that was not meant to contain it.1 Under preferences, a short list of choices is enough.

Add one more question for guests with serious allergies. Do they carry their own emergency medicine, and would they like one organizer to know where it is kept? Keep that answer private. Share it only with the person who leads on safety during the trip.

Set a deadline for the form a few weeks before the event and treat registration as unfinished until it comes back. A blank answer and an answer of none mean different things, so require one or the other. Chase the blanks by email the week the deadline passes.

Make it easy to answer honestly. Some people leave an allergy off a form because they do not want a fuss made of them at dinner. Say on the form who sees the answers, and that the kitchen gets names only where it needs them to plate a meal. A guest who trusts the process is more likely to tell you the whole story.

Send the kitchen one list, and get a written reply

Turn the answers into a single sheet. Sort it with allergies first, then medical diets, then preferences. Give each guest one row with their name, what they avoid and any note about cross-contact. Leave out anything the kitchen has no use for, such as the reason a guest eats no meat.

Email the sheet to the person who runs the kitchen as well as the sales contact who booked the event. Ask for a reply in writing that confirms they have it and can prepare every line. If one line is a problem, you want to hear it while there is still time to change the menu or bring a dish in from elsewhere.

Ask how the kitchen keeps allergen meals apart. Some cook them first and some keep separate pans and boards. Others plate and label each one by name. Ask which method this kitchen uses for your group, and get the answer in writing before the first meal is served.

Send a short reminder a week before arrival with the final sheet attached and any late changes marked. Ask the kitchen to confirm again. Between the first sheet and arrival day, people add allergies and sign up late. The sheet the chef holds should match the guests who walk in.

Ask whether written allergen notice applies where you go

The FDA writes a model Food Code that state, local, tribal and territorial governments can adopt. Under the edition that added sesame as a major allergen, unpackaged food served or sold in a retail food establishment has to come with written notice of major food allergens. The same edition says staff food safety training has to include allergy awareness of the nine major allergens.2

Two limits apply. The rule covers the places that adopt that edition, and adoption varies. A private retreat house that brings in a cook is also a different setting from a restaurant. So ask the venue directly whether it labels allergens on buffet dishes. If it does not, ask it to label them for your group. Small printed cards beside each dish spare guests from having to ask a server about every tray.

Labels help at breakfast most of all. It is the meal where people serve themselves, half awake, from a long table. A card that says the muffins contain tree nuts does more than any reminder sent the week before.

The meeting room needs the list too. Coffee breaks bring pastries, nut bars and seeded crackers to the table, sometimes set out by staff who have not seen the dinner menu. Ask the venue to label break snacks the same way as meals, and to keep one tray made without any of the foods on your list.

Plan the meals that happen away from the venue

A retreat may include a meal off site, a packed lunch for a hike or snacks from a local shop. Each one is a place where the list can get lost. Send the same sheet to every restaurant and caterer involved, and ask each of them for the same written reply.

For outings, pack the allergy-safe food separately and label it with the guest's name. Bring more of it than you think you need. A late bus or a long trail can turn one snack into two, and the guest with the allergy should not be the one who goes without.

On arrival day, walk the chef through the list in person if there is time, and introduce guests with serious allergies to the server who looks after their table. After the retreat, delete the medical answers you collected or store them under the privacy rules your organization uses for health details. Keep the blank form, though. The next event starts from it.

The sentence to send the kitchen with the sheet: "Please confirm in writing that you can prepare each meal on this list as described, including the cross-contact notes, and tell us now about any line you cannot meet."

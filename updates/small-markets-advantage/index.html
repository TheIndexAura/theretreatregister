Choose a Retreat Location Outside a Big City: Five Checks for Planners

July 17, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A small town can hold a good retreat if five checks come back clean before anyone signs: the way in, internet at the venue's own address, the nearest hospital, what lodging costs in that county, and what else is booked in town that week. Each check has a public tool behind it, and you can run all of them from a desk in one afternoon.

Read the lodging table before you read a single venue page

The General Services Administration sets the per diem rates federal agencies pay employees for lodging and meals on official travel. Its own answer to how those rates work is plain: a standard rate covers about eighty-five percent of counties in the continental United States, and the places with their own higher rate are the ones federal travelers visit often, with rates set from the average daily room rates of local lodging properties.1 Standard counties, in the agency's words, are less frequently traveled by the federal community.

The table says something about how often federal travelers go to a place. If the town on your shortlist sits in a county that carries its own rate, federal travelers go there often enough that the table gives it a rate of its own. If the county falls under the standard rate, it is one of the places the federal table treats as ordinary. Neither answer tells you what a venue will quote your group, and the table is not a price list for retreats.

Use it for one job. Look up the county and note whether it has its own rate. Put that note next to each town on your shortlist. Then, when two towns look alike on every other count, you have one more honest line to compare them on, and it came from a federal record.

Check the way in, door to door

The first question a small town has to answer is how the group gets there and how long it takes. Find the nearest airport with regular commercial flights, then map the drive from that airport to the venue's street address, and do it for the hour your group will actually land. A town that looks close on a map can sit at the end of a two-lane road that slows to a crawl behind a farm truck.

Then picture the last mile. Ask the venue in writing whether a coach or a large passenger van can reach the door, whether there is room to turn around, and where the cars would park if half the group drives. Ask whether the road is paved the whole way and whether anything about it changes after heavy rain or snow. These are easy answers for a venue that hosts groups, and a slow or vague answer tells you something too.

Arrival time deserves its own line in the plan. If the group lands late in the day, a long drive on unfamiliar roads after dark is the part of the trip people remember, and not fondly. Moving arrival to the morning, or adding a first night near the airport, can cost less than the tired first session that follows a bad drive.

Check the internet at the exact address

If the agenda needs a shared screen or a video call to the people who stayed home, the connection matters. The National Broadband Map, run by the FCC, the federal agency that oversees phone and internet service, displays where internet services are and are not available across the United States, as reported by the providers themselves.2 Type in the venue's street address, not the town name, and you will see which providers report service there and the top speeds they advertise.

The FCC says the map shows internet availability and does not show network performance, affordability, or adoption.2 A provider listed at the address tells you service can be bought there. It does not tell you how the connection holds up in a meeting room with every laptop in the group open on it.

So the map is where the question starts. Write to the venue and ask for a speed test taken in the meeting room at the hour your sessions would run. Ask too whether guests share that connection with the meeting space, and who on site can reset the equipment on a weekend when it drops. If the answers are thin, plan the agenda so that nothing important depends on a live connection, and print what you would otherwise put on a screen.

Check the nearest hospital before you need it

Nobody plans a retreat around an emergency. Still, a group that spends a day on trails or on the water is a long way from a city, and in a small town the nearest emergency room can be a long drive away.

Medicare's Care Compare tool lets you search by street, ZIP code or city to find hospitals near a location, and it covers more than four thousand Medicare-certified hospitals across the country.3 Search from the venue's address, note the closest hospital and the drive time to it, and put both on the run sheet that you and one other organizer carry. If the program includes an outing, look up the hospital nearest to that spot as well.

Ask the venue in writing who on its staff holds first aid training and where the supplies are kept. Ask as well how an ambulance would find the building at night. The point is a calm answer ready on paper, so that no one has to search for an address while someone waits.

Check what else is happening in town that week

A small town has a calendar of its own, and your dates have to fit inside it. A wedding or a county fair can take the overflow rooms you were counting on and fill every table at the one restaurant that seats a group. Ask the venue in writing what else is booked in town for your dates, and look at the town or county events page for the same week.

Ask the same question about the season. Some rural places are busy in the months when visitors come for the leaves or the snow, and quiet the rest of the year. The quiet months can mean easier dates and a host with more time for your group. They can also mean the café closes early and the hardware store is shut on Sunday, so plan meals and supplies with that in mind.

Then ask about the small things that a city hides. Where is the nearest pharmacy, and how late does it stay open? Can a grocery order reach the venue? Does a ride service operate in the area at all, or will the group need its own vehicles for every trip off the property? Each answer changes the plan, and none of them is on a venue's photo page.

Put every answer in one shared document, with the venue's written replies pasted in. If the town passes all five checks, it has earned its place on the shortlist. If it fails one, you will know which one, and you can decide whether the quiet is worth the workaround. Write to each venue with the same sentence, so the replies line up side by side: "Please tell us how a group this size reaches you, what the connection is like in the meeting room, and what else is booked in town that week."

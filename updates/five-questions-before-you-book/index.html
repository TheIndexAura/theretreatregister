Five questions to ask a retreat venue before you sign anything

July 13, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A pretty picture and a tempting nightly rate. It is enough to make any retreat planner swoon. You have found the perfect spot, you think, ready to take your team somewhere they will actually get something done.

Then the quote lands in your inbox and it is a different story. The numbers do not add up to what you expected.

The nightly rate is often the least useful number on the page. It is the figure the venue can publish without committing to anything, because the rest of your bill is decided by four things that are not printed anywhere: your flexibility on dates, the buyout threshold, the food-and-beverage minimum, and the service fees. Those four decide the real number.

This industry runs on quotes by default. On July 12, 2026, 133 of the register’s 1,374 curated venues published a starting price anywhere on their own website, which is 9.7%.2 By July 19, 2026, with 2,152 venues on file, 184 published one.3 The share has not been above one in ten in any dated reading the register has taken.

So the price is a conversation. Here are the five questions that shape it, what a useful answer sounds like, and what an answer that should worry you sounds like.

1. What is your flexibility on dates?

Why it decides the number. A venue’s price is mostly a function of what else it could sell that weekend. Move a Thursday-to-Saturday retreat to a Sunday-to-Tuesday and you are often buying the same building for a different figure, because the venue was not going to sell those nights anyway.

Ask it like this: “We are looking at the second week of October, and we have some flexibility. Which weeks in October are softest for you, and what does that change about the rate?”

A good answer: a named week, a named reason, and a number. “The second week is full with a wedding block. If you can take the third week, Sunday in and Wednesday out, I can hold the whole property at a rate I could not offer you on a weekend.”

A worrying answer: “Our rates are the same year round.” Very few real properties price that way. It usually means the person you are talking to cannot move on price and will not say so.

2. Is there a buyout threshold, and what is it?

Why it decides the number. A buyout means your group has the entire property. Most venues have a headcount or a spend level at which a buyout becomes available, and often it becomes cheaper per head than booking rooms individually, because the venue stops having to manage two audiences.

Ask it like this: “At what group size or total spend does a full buyout become available, and what is included when it is?”

A good answer: a threshold and a list. “Thirty-two rooms, or $46,000 in total spend. A buyout includes both meeting rooms, the barn, exclusive use of the dining room, and a 10pm noise curfew instead of 9pm.”

A worrying answer: “We can talk about that later.” The buyout threshold is the single biggest lever on your cost. A venue that will not name it early is choosing when you learn it.

3. What are the food-and-beverage minimums?

Why it decides the number. The food-and-beverage minimum is a floor on what you must spend on catering, separate from the rooms. It is where a cheap-looking venue becomes an expensive one. A property can advertise a modest nightly rate and attach a minimum that doubles the bill.

Ask it like this: “What is the food-and-beverage minimum for a group of our size over three days, is it per day or for the whole stay, and does service charge and tax count towards it?”

That last clause is the one planners forget. If the minimum is $12,000 and the service charge does not count towards it, you need to buy $12,000 of food before the fees start.

A good answer: a figure, a period, and a clear statement about what counts.

A worrying answer: a figure with no period attached. “The minimum is $12,000” means something very different per day than it does per stay.

A federal yardstick you can hold the answer against

There is a public number you can measure a catering quote against, and it is not a marketing figure. The U.S. General Services Administration sets the ceiling for what a federal employee may be reimbursed for lodging and meals on official travel. For fiscal year 2026 the standard lodging rate is $110 a night and the standard meals and incidental expenses rate is $68 a day.1

That is not a market price and it is not a group rate, so do not use it as one. Use it as a floor for your own sense of scale. If a venue quotes $190 a head a day for food, you now know it is running at almost three times the federal daily meals ceiling, and you can decide whether the menu justifies that.

The same bulletin contains the more interesting admission. The government allows its own travelers to be reimbursed above the ceiling when the ceiling will not do: “If per diem reimbursement rates are insufficient to meet expenses, the FTR permits actual expense reimbursement up to 300% of the applicable maximum per diem rate.”1 The federal government publishes a national lodging number and then writes down, in the same document, that it may need to be tripled depending on where and when you go. That is the plainest available explanation of why almost no venue will print one group price.

4. What service fees and taxes will appear on the final invoice?

Why it decides the number. Service charge, administrative fee, resort fee, gratuity, state sales tax, county occupancy tax. Each is a percentage, several compound on top of the others, and together they routinely add a fifth to a third to the subtotal.

Ask it like this: “Please list every fee and tax that will appear on the final invoice, with the percentage for each, and tell me which ones are applied on top of the others.”

A good answer: a list you can add up. “22% service charge on food and beverage, 8.25% state sales tax on the whole bill, 3% county occupancy tax on rooms only. The service charge is taxed.”

A worrying answer: “There is a standard service charge.” Ask for the number. If it does not arrive in writing before you sign, it will arrive on the invoice.

5. Who answers group inquiries, and how quickly?

Why it decides your experience rather than your cost. The person who replies to your first email is usually the person who will be reachable at 7am on the morning your bus is late. Response speed before the contract is the best available signal of response speed after it.

Ask it like this: “Who will be my contact for this booking, is that the same person on site during the event, and who covers if they are away?”

A good answer: a name, a mobile number, and a named deputy.

A worrying answer: a shared inbox with no name attached, or a four-day gap before the reply arrives.

You can test this one before you ask anything. On July 13, 2026 the register held 2,082 curated venues, and 1,883 of them listed a direct phone number, which is 90%.4 Reading across the register’s dated readings from July 12 to July 18, 2026, that share stayed between 90.4% and 90.8% while the register itself grew from 1,374 venues to 2,281 and was then cut back to 2,125.4 A ratio that holds that steady while the sample nearly doubles is telling you something about the industry rather than about the sample.

Pick up the phone. How the call goes is the answer to question five.

The email you can send today

Subject: Group inquiry, [dates], [headcount] people

Hello,

I am planning a company retreat for [headcount] people, arriving [date] and departing [date]. We would need [number] guest rooms and a meeting space for the full group, plus two breakout spaces.

Before we go further, could you answer these five things:

  1. Which dates in that window are softest for you, and what does that change about the rate?
  2. At what group size or total spend does a full buyout become available, and what is included?
  3. What is the food-and-beverage minimum for a group this size, is it per day or for the stay, and do service charge and tax count towards it?
  4. Please list every fee and tax that will appear on the final invoice, with the percentage for each.
  5. Who is my contact for this booking, and who covers when they are away?

A rough all-in figure is more useful to me than a nightly rate. Thank you.

[Name, title, phone]

Send that to three venues on the same day. The three replies, side by side, are worth more than any published rate.

Do not let a glossy photograph and a low room rate lead you astray. Ask those five questions and you will be comparing whole numbers rather than pretty pixels, which is the only comparison that survives contact with the invoice.

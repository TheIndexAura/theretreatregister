Planner Guide

Read the date-based charges in a retreat cancellation clause

September 28, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Does your cancellation clause change its charges by date? Read every date-based charge before you sign, put each one in plain words, and ask the venue your questions in writing.

Find the cancellation section before anything else

It is easy to read the dates and the deposit line and miss the cancellation section, and a change of plans may be priced there. Open the contract and find every sentence that mentions cancelling, reducing the group or moving the dates. The cancellation terms may be in one place or spread across an addendum and a page of general terms.

The Wisconsin Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection puts the basic advice in one line: read through the entire contract, even the fine print, before signing.1 Its handout also says that most of the time if you cancel your contract, you will lose your deposit.1 It adds that the loss can be more, depending on the minimum requirements of the contract.1 That handout is general advice for consumers, and it does not describe retreat contracts or any particular charge scale.

Print the contract if you can. Use a highlighter and a pen. The goal is to mark each place where a date changes what you owe. If a clause refers to another document, ask the venue to send that document before you sign, and read it too.

Turn each date into a plain sentence

A date-based clause may read like a table or a run of paragraphs. Each row or paragraph may name a point in time before arrival and say what the group owes if it cancels after that point. Whatever form the charge takes, copy it into your own words on a separate sheet.

Write one sentence per date. For example, you might write, "If the group cancels after this date, the venue keeps the deposit and also charges for the guest rooms." The sentence should say which date starts the charge, what the charge is made of, and what the contract uses as the starting point for working it out. If you cannot finish that sentence from the contract text alone, you have found a question for the venue. Write it down.

Pay attention to the wording around the dates. Does the clause count from the day you send notice, or from the day the venue receives it? Does it say how notice must be given, and to whom? Does a smaller group count as a cancellation of part of the booking, and if so, which row applies? Check each of these points in your own contract. The answers have to come from your own document.

Put the dates on a calendar with the sentences beside them. Add a reminder ahead of each date, so a decision about attendance or program changes arrives before a higher charge begins. A planner who has the whole schedule in front of them can compare the cost of waiting with the cost of deciding early.

Ask what the charges are made of and when they are shown

Federal rules about hidden fees may be worth a look. The hidden fees rule in the federal regulations, for a covered good or service, at section four hundred sixty-four point two of title sixteen of the Code of Federal Regulations, says a business must disclose clearly and conspicuously, before the consumer consents to pay, the nature, purpose, and amount of any fee or charge imposed on the transaction that has been excluded from total price.2 It also calls for disclosure of the final amount of payment.2

The rule applies to what it calls a covered good or service. The definitions page lists two kinds: live-event tickets, and short-term lodging, including temporary sleeping accommodations at a hotel, motel, inn, short-term rental, vacation rental, or other place of lodging.3 The rule pages do not say whether a group contract for a retreat falls inside those definitions, and they do not mention cancellation charges at all. Treat the rule as background, and have a lawyer say whether it applies to your booking.

Whatever the rule covers, the question is practical. Ask for a written list of every charge that could appear on the final bill, including any that apply only after cancelling. Ask which of those charges the contract already states, and which depend on a rate card or a policy kept somewhere else. If a charge sits in a separate document, ask to see that document before you sign.

Get the answers in writing, and ask about waiting periods

Date-based charges raise questions the contract may leave open, so write them down before you sign. How much advance notice does each row assume, and does the venue count days or nights? Is there a waiting period before a change takes effect? Does the venue treat a smaller group as a cancellation of part of the booking? Put each of these to the venue in an email.

Keep the answers in writing. The Wisconsin handout says if the seller makes a verbal promise, make sure the written contract states the promise.1 It adds its own statement that if there is a dispute about a written contract or a verbal promise, the written contract prevails.1 That is one state agency's consumer handout, and what it means for your booking, in the state where the venue sits, is a question for a lawyer. Meanwhile, if a sales manager says the dates are flexible, ask whether that promise is written into the contract, and keep the answer in writing.

So when a venue contact offers a friendlier arrangement, ask for the sentence to be added to the contract itself. If the venue prefers an email, keep it with the contract and ask the venue to confirm that the email applies to the booking. Then check the contract for a sentence saying that statements outside the document are not recognized. The Wisconsin handout warns that many contracts have provisions, often in fine print, that say so.1

Change what you can before you sign, then hand it to a lawyer

The Wisconsin handout tells readers to seek legal or other advice if they do not understand any part of the contract.1 A retreat contract with date-based charges deserves that review, because a lawyer can tell you what the terms mean for the person who would sign. Only a lawyer who has read your actual contract and knows the law where the venue sits can say what its terms mean for you. Ask the lawyer to look at the cancellation section, the notice rules, and anything that refers to another document.

Before the review, write down what you want changed. You might ask for a longer period before the first charge begins, or for a clause that lets you reduce the group size by a stated amount without it counting as a cancellation. You might ask what happens if the venue resells the dates, and whether the contract says anything about that. The venue may say no to any of these. Ask anyway.

The Wisconsin handout also says never to sign a contract where something can be added later, and to get a copy of the signed contract and keep it.1 Before signing, ask your lawyer what to do about any clause that does not apply to your group or does not match what the venue told you. Store the signed copy, your plain-word sheet and every written answer from the venue in one folder. When the group size or the dates shift, open that folder first and find the row that applies today.

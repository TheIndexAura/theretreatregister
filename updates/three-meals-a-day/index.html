Protect meal time: plan the table, pacing and allergens

August 14, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Many agendas time the workshop to the minute and give dinner one line.

Then the group goes home, and the planner wonders why it never quite connected.

Connection tends to show up when the schedule loosens, and the table is where it loosens most. The moments people remember from a retreat often come at meals, when the talk runs past the planned hour. Shared stories get told there.

Plan the dining room as carefully as a session. One long table tends to pull a group into a single conversation, while a room of scattered four-tops splits it into small ones. A dinner with no clock on it can also let people go deeper than a quick, efficient meal does. Put the venue's default meal setup on your selection list, next to a listed phone number and a dedicated meeting or lodging space.

When you email venues, ask how they usually set the dining room and whether they can do one long table. Ask how flexible they are on meal pacing for groups, and leave unhurried time in your own agenda.

What the research on shared meals shows

Cornell researchers studied firefighter platoons in a large city fire department with more than 50 firehouses. Over 15 months they asked 395 supervisors to rate their platoon from zero to 10 and to say how often it ate together. Platoons that ate together most often earned higher ratings. The lead author called eating together "a kind of social glue" (Cornell Chronicle, Groups that eat together perform better together).

Read the limits. The ratings came from supervisors, so the result is an association. It does not show that meals caused better work, and a firehouse crew on shift is not a retreat group. Use it as a reason to protect meal time (Cornell Chronicle on the 2015 firefighter study).

Allergens belong in the first email

The FDA names nine major food allergens, and sesame became the ninth under a law signed on April 23, 2021. It describes reactions that run from hives and lip swelling to severe, life-threatening symptoms, often called anaphylaxis (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Food allergies).

The FDA material covers product labels. A plated dinner has no label. So collect dietary needs on your registration form and send the list to the kitchen in writing, with a request for a written reply. Then ask who prepares the food, since that person holds the answers (FDA page on food allergen labeling and the major allergens).

Plan a Colorado Mountain Retreat: Altitude, Weather and Forest Questions

July 29, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Altitude and mountain weather are two facts about a Colorado venue that the listing may not mention. Find out the elevation, how the venue handles a guest who feels unwell and what it does when the weather turns.

Start with the height of the property

A Colorado listing may lead with scenery. The number that matters for a group is the elevation, because some guests will arrive from sea level and spend their first night well above it. MedlinePlus describes acute mountain sickness as an illness that can affect climbers, hikers, skiers or travelers at high altitudes, usually above eight thousand feet.1 A venue can sit below that line and still have a trailhead, a ski area or an excursion above it.

So the first email can be short. Give the venue your dates and ask for the elevation of the lodging, the meeting room and any place where the group will spend an afternoon. A plain number from the venue is easier to plan around than a description such as high mountain setting. If the venue has hosted groups from lower ground before, ask how those guests did and what the staff suggest.

Add a line about your own group. Someone planning a first retreat in the mountains may not know who has been at altitude before. A short note to guests that names the elevation and asks them to tell you if they have had trouble in the past gives you something to work with, and it does not require anyone to share more than they choose.

Share the elevation with guests early. Each one can then plan their own travel and rest.

Build the schedule around the way the body adjusts

MedlinePlus puts the best way to prevent altitude illness in one sentence: ascend gradually.1 It also advises a day or two of rest after each two thousand feet of climb above eight thousand feet, and it recommends drinking plenty of fluids.1 Those lines were written for people climbing a mountain, and a retreat group is not doing that. Still, the idea carries over to the agenda.

Put a light first day on the schedule. Arrival, an easy dinner and an early night leave room for guests who feel tired. Keep the hardest physical activity, such as a long hike, for a later day. Offer water at every session, and tell the kitchen you want plenty of it on the tables. If the group is flying in the same morning, a late start on day one costs very little and may save an afternoon.

Ask the venue how it handles a guest who does not feel right. Is there a quiet room? Does a staff member check on guests overnight? Where is the nearest clinic, and how long does the drive take in winter? These are the same questions a planner would ask anywhere, and they matter more at height, where a short trip can take longer than the map suggests.

Know what to do if someone gets ill

MedlinePlus is plain about the response. For a climber with symptoms, the advice is to climb down right away and as safely as possible, and it names symptoms that need emergency help through the local emergency number.1 The page introduces one list of symptoms for the mild-to-moderate illness and another for the signs that need emergency help. Read both yourself before the trip.

Put the plan on one sheet that the group lead and the venue both have. The sheet names who contacts emergency help, who arranges transport, which clinic is nearest and who stays with the guest. Keep it short. In an emergency nobody reads a long document, and a one-page plan is easier to find than a section of a long contract.

Do not use the plan to diagnose anyone. Your job as a planner is to know the route to help and to make it easy for a guest to use it. A guest who feels unwell should be able to say so without feeling that they are spoiling the retreat. A sentence in your welcome note that invites guests to speak up early does a lot of that work.

Plan for weather and for the forest around the venue

If the venue sits in or near a national forest, the Forest Service has a page for visitors. It tells people to be prepared by monitoring weather conditions before they set out for the day, and it warns that the weather can change suddenly and dramatically.2 Mountain weather is a good reason to keep a second plan for every outdoor session. If the group is meant to hike on the second morning, decide the night before whether the forecast allows it, and name who decides.

The same page says some sites require a modest recreation fee, and that visitors must remain vigilant for potentially dangerous situations.2 For a group, the fee question belongs in your budget conversation with the venue. If the retreat includes a trip to a forest site, find out who pays any fee and whether the venue arranges it.

The Forest Service also reminds visitors that animals in the forest are not pets, and that people should keep a safe distance and be careful not to disturb their habitat.2 A short line in the welcome note covers this. Guests who are not used to the mountains may want to feed an animal or walk close to take a photo, and a reminder from the organizer is easier to hear than a warning from a stranger.

Think about the return trip as well. Guests who go home the day after a high-altitude stay may feel the change again when they travel, and a group that flies out from a mountain airport can face a long drive on the last morning. Leave a buffer on departure day, and tell guests the time the shuttle leaves well ahead of the date. A calm last morning is worth more than one extra session.

Plan the meals with the height in mind. Drinks matter at altitude, so ask the kitchen for water, tea and juice on every table, and keep the evening menu light on the first night. A kitchen used to mountain groups will have its own habits, and its answers show how much experience the venue has with guests who are new to the area.

Questions to put in one message

Pull the points above into a single email to each venue you are comparing. Give your dates and head count. Ask for the elevation of the lodging and the meeting room. Ask what the venue suggests for guests who are not used to altitude, and where the nearest clinic is. Ask how it handles a sudden change in the forecast, and who decides about outdoor sessions. Ask whether any forest or park fees apply to the activities you have in mind.

Compare the replies side by side. A venue that answers with specifics, such as a number for the elevation and a named clinic, gives you something to plan with. A venue that answers with reassurance alone leaves you to find the facts yourself. Share the key lines with your group lead, and send the welcome note to guests before they travel, so that the height is no surprise on arrival.

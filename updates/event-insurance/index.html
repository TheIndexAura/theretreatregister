Retreat Event Insurance for Planners: Protect the Group and Deposit

September 12, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Retreat event insurance is the organizer's own policy for the event. It has two parts. Liability coverage handles claims of injury or property damage caused by the group. Cancellation coverage is for the money at risk, such as deposits, if the event is called off. The venue's policy covers the venue, not the group's activities.

The two parts of the policy

The Oklahoma Insurance Department describes special event insurance in two pieces. Liability insurance “provides coverage for injury or property damage related to your event.” Cancellation insurance “covers expenses related to delays, rescheduling or cancellations,” and the department names weather, illness or injury, and vendor failure as examples.1 Insurers sell the two parts on their own or together, so the first thing to check on any quote is which of them it includes.

Liability coverage answers claims from other people. A guest trips on a trail the group arranged, or a rented kayak comes back cracked. The Texas Department of Insurance describes the premises and operations part of a commercial general liability policy as paying “for bodily injury or property damage that occurs on your premises” or “as a result of your business operations.”2 At a retreat, those operations are the sessions and activities the organizer puts on the schedule.

Cancellation coverage answers a different loss: money already paid, or already owed, when the event cannot go ahead or has to move. For most retreats that means the venue deposit, any nonrefundable venue fees, and deposits paid to outside vendors.

Why the venue’s policy does not cover the group

The Oklahoma department notes that “event sites may have their own liability insurance,” and that relying on it can leave gaps.1 A venue buys its policy to protect the venue and its own staff. A claim that grows out of something the group brought in, such as an outside facilitator or a guided hike, can land on the organizer instead. The venue contract decides how that split works, so the insurance clause and the indemnification clause are worth reading side by side.

Alcohol needs its own question. The department tells organizers to be clear on whether the event liability policy includes host liquor liability.1 If the retreat serves drinks, get that answer in writing before you buy.

When each part is worth buying

Liability coverage is worth buying when the venue contract requires the organizer to carry it, or when the schedule holds activities the venue does not run itself. A venue contract may ask for a certificate of insurance before arrival, with the venue named on it. A group that only meets in a room the venue staffs carries less exposure than a group that brings its own guides and boats.

Cancellation coverage is worth pricing when the money at risk is large and the deposit terms are strict. Start with the venue’s cancellation schedule. Add up what the group would owe on each date in that schedule, then add the vendor deposits. That total is the amount a cancellation policy would need to cover, and it grows as the event date gets closer. If the contract lets the group move its dates without a fee, or returns most of the deposit, the case for the policy is weaker.

One exclusion matters more than the rest. The Oklahoma department says reimbursement for event costs is “not typically covered if the event is called off voluntarily.”1 A policy pays for the causes it names, such as severe weather or a vendor that fails to appear. A decision by the organizer to call the event off is a different thing. Read the list of covered causes before you compare quotes.

Questions to send before you buy

Send these in one email to the insurer or broker, and keep the written answers with the venue contract.

  • Is the company licensed to sell insurance in the state where the retreat takes place?
  • Does the quote cover liability and cancellation, or only one of them?
  • Which causes of cancellation does the policy cover, and which does it exclude?
  • Is there a deadline to buy cancellation coverage after the first deposit is paid?
  • Does the liability policy include host liquor liability?
  • Does the liability coverage reach activities run by outside vendors the group hires?
  • Can the venue be named on the certificate, and how soon can the certificate be issued?
  • What limit does the venue contract require, and does the quote meet it?

Arranging it in writing

Price the policy while the venue contract is being settled. By then the deposit amounts and the required limits are known, and the first large payment has not yet gone out. Ask the insurer to send the certificate straight to the venue contact named in the contract, and keep a copy with the contract and the policy.

Before the final schedule goes to the group, set each activity on it beside the policy’s exclusions. If an activity is excluded, there are three ways out: move it, drop it, or get written confirmation that the vendor who runs it carries its own coverage. The venue side has its own check: ask the venue for its certificate of insurance before you sign.

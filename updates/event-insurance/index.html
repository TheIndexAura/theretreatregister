Check event insurance terms before a retreat deposit goes out

September 12, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Event insurance has several parts, such as cancellation and liability, and each part answers a different question. The Oklahoma Insurance Department describes both of those, and the Texas Department of Insurance explains commercial general liability. Bring plain coverage questions to a licensed insurance agent and questions about the venue's own policy to the venue before any deposit goes out.

Sort out what the words mean

Quotes from different sellers get easier to compare once you pin the terms down. In a consumer page about special events, the Oklahoma Insurance Department lists event cancellation, liability, personal injury, property damage and liquor liability among the coverage areas event insurance can include.1 It names liability insurance and cancellation insurance as two types of event insurance.1 So when a quote lands in your inbox, look for those two words first.

Write down which of them show up and which do not. Say a quote lists liability and stays quiet on cancellation. Ask the insurance agent whether cancellation cover comes with it or is sold separately. Look for the limits and exclusions in the quote as well. You do not need insurance training for any of this, just patience and a pen. Mark every line you couldn't explain to a colleague in one sentence.

The Oklahoma page is a consumer page about special events.1 The Texas page is written for business owners.2 A licensed insurance agent who has read your actual policy is a different thing, and neither page can stand in for one. The venue answers for its own policy. If you want to know what a contract clause means in law, ask a lawyer who knows the state where the retreat takes place.

Liability coverage and the venue's own policy

Oklahoma's department says liability insurance provides coverage for injury or property damage related to your event.1 Picture a guest who is injured in a fall on a stair during the welcome dinner. That is bodily injury. A rented projector that is damaged when it is dropped is property damage. Whether a particular claim is covered comes down to the policy wording, so put examples like these to the insurance agent and ask for the answer in writing.

The Texas Department of Insurance explains a related product, commercial general liability insurance, in a page written for business owners. It says premises and operations coverage pays for bodily injury or property damage that occurs on your premises or as a result of your business operations.2 That is one state's page, written for business owners. Treat it as a source of vocabulary, then ask the licensed insurance agent which product the quote is for and whether it fits a single retreat.

Event sites may have their own liability insurance, the Oklahoma page says, and you may want to consider your own liability coverage to protect yourself from gaps in their coverage.1 It does not say what those gaps look like at any given venue. A licensed insurance agent can answer that once they see the venue's policy. So ask the venue for a copy of its policy, or at least its certificate, and for the names listed on it. Hand whatever the venue gives you to the insurance agent. Then ask whether the venue's coverage reaches activities your group brings in, such as an outside facilitator, a hired guide or a vendor running an evening event.

One more line from the same page: be clear on whether your event liability policy includes host liquor liability.1 What that coverage pays for isn't explained there, so the insurance agent has to do it. Say wine is poured at dinner. Put the questions in writing. Ask the venue who is serving the alcohol and whether the venue holds its own coverage for it. Ask the insurance agent whether your policy adds anything on top. Keep the written answers with the rest of the event paperwork.

Cancellation coverage and the money at risk

The Oklahoma department says cancellation insurance covers expenses related to delays, rescheduling or cancellations due to unforeseen covered events.1 The page gives three scenarios. If the event is postponed due to weather, cancellation coverage can reimburse you for rescheduling costs. If the event is rescheduled because a contracted vendor got sick or hurt, rescheduling costs can be reimbursed too. If a vendor fails to deliver service, the page says cancellation insurance may cover the loss of the deposit, in addition to expenses for replacement vendors hired on short notice.1

Read the phrase "covered events" closely. The page does not list which events a given policy counts, so ask the insurance agent for the policy's list and for the events it leaves out. It does give one example of what a policy may not pay: reimbursement for event costs may not be covered if the event is called off voluntarily.1 Might your group cancel on its own, over budget or the calendar? Ask the insurance agent how the policy treats that case before you buy.

Before shopping, work out how much money is exposed. Take the venue's cancellation schedule and write down what you would owe on each date in it. Then add deposits paid to caterers, guides, transport companies and any other vendor. The total moves with the dates in your contracts. Ask the insurance agent how much of it a cancellation policy would reimburse and what limit to request. Ask, too, whether a contract that lets you move dates without a fee, or returns part of a deposit, changes the cover worth buying.

Timing deserves a question of its own. Ask whether cancellation coverage must be bought within a certain window after the first deposit, and whether a cause that is already known when you buy, such as a storm that forecasters have already named, is treated differently. The Oklahoma page lists no purchase window, so the policy documents are the place to look.

The Oklahoma page also lists extra options to ask about, including coverage for rented property. It says that if you are renting furniture or equipment for the event, additional coverage can help pay for repair or replacement costs should the rented property be damaged or destroyed.1 If the retreat rents sound equipment, tables or a projector, ask whether the quote includes that option.

Contract language that shifts liability

The Texas page lists examples of exclusions in commercial general liability policies, and it says that coverage varies by insurer and includes additional exclusions beyond its examples.2 One example is that such policies exclude coverage for property damage to your work.2 Another is that they do not cover property damage to your product arising out of the product or any part of it.2 Those examples speak of "your work" and "your product," so ask the insurance agent whether either one touches a retreat.

The page also describes contractual liability. Policies exclude coverage for bodily injury or property damage that you are obligated to pay because you assumed liability in a contract or agreement, and the page lists two exceptions.2 One exception is for liability you would have had without the contract. The other is for liability assumed in a contract the policy defines as an insured contract, if the injury or damage occurs after the contract is executed. An exception to an exclusion does not by itself promise coverage, so the policy wording decides. The page says a policy may provide coverage to indemnify another party, depending on the wording of the indemnity agreement.2

Neither source describes venue contracts, so treat this part as questions to ask, not as findings. Read the venue contract for any clause that asks your group to take on responsibility for claims, and for any insurance clause. If you find one, ask the licensed insurance agent whether your policy would respond to that promise, and whether the policy defines the contract as an insured contract. A lawyer can say what the clause means under your state's law. The planner's job is to spot the clause early, before signing.

The Texas page adds that a pollution exclusion eliminates coverage for injuries or damages to a third party resulting from a pollution event arising from your business operations.2 It also says total pollution exclusions eliminate all coverage, including coverage for premises and operations and for products and completed operations.2 The page does not mention retreats. Ask the insurance agent whether the quote carries any pollution exclusion and what it removes, and ask the venue whether it stores fuel, chemicals or boats on site.

The Texas page also describes excess liability insurance, which pays for covered losses that exceed your policy's dollar limit.2 Ask the venue whether its contract states a required limit. If it does, compare that number against the limit on the quote before you pay for anything, and ask the venue whether it accepts the quote as written or wants a higher limit.

Questions to send to a licensed insurance agent and to the venue

The Oklahoma page lists questions to ask when shopping for event insurance. Among them: whether the insurance company is licensed to do business in Oklahoma, what is and is not covered by the policy, whether other policies you hold already provide coverage and whether it is adequate for your event, and what additional event coverage options exist.1 It also says you can verify an agent or licensee on the department's website under Consumer Assistance.1 That lookup belongs to Oklahoma, so ask the insurance department in the state where the retreat is held what it offers.

Add questions that fit a retreat. Which causes of cancellation does the policy list, and which does it leave out? Does the liability coverage reach activities run by outside vendors you hire? Is the quote written for a set number of guests, and what happens if the group grows? Can the venue be named on a certificate of insurance, and how quickly can the certificate be issued? Does the quote meet the limit the venue contract asks for?

Send the venue its own set. Ask for a copy of its certificate before you sign. Ask what insurance the venue's contract expects your group to carry, then give that answer to the insurance agent and ask which gaps remain. Ask whether it requires a particular wording on your certificate, and who at the venue receives it. Ask what happens to your deposit if the venue itself has to cancel, since that is a different situation from a cancellation by the group.

Put every question in one email, send it while the contract is still open for changes, and file the answers beside the signed contract. Keep a copy of the policy where the whole planning team can find it the night before arrival. If the answers from the insurance agent and the venue disagree, resolve that before the first large payment goes out.

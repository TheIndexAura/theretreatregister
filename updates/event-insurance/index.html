Retreat Event Insurance for Planners: Protect the Group and Deposit

September 12, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Retreat event insurance is the organizer's own policy for the event. It has two parts. Liability coverage handles claims of injury or property damage caused by the group. Cancellation coverage is for the money at risk, such as deposits, if the event is called off. The venue's policy covers the venue, not the group's activities.

What each part of the policy answers

The Oklahoma Insurance Department splits special event insurance into two parts. Liability insurance “provides coverage for injury or property damage related to your event,” and cancellation insurance “covers expenses related to delays, rescheduling or cancellations,” with weather, illness or injury, and vendor failure as the department’s examples.1 Insurers sell the parts on their own or together. Check which parts a quote includes before you set two quotes side by side.

Liability coverage answers claims from other people. A guest turns an ankle on a trail you arranged, or a rented kayak comes back cracked. The Texas Department of Insurance says the premises and operations part of a commercial general liability policy pays “for bodily injury or property damage that occurs on your premises” or “as a result of your business operations.”2 At a retreat, your operations are the sessions and outings on your schedule.

Cancellation coverage deals with money. It covers what you have already paid, or already owe, when the event cannot go ahead or has to move. For most retreats that means the venue deposit, any fees the contract makes nonrefundable, and deposits paid to outside vendors.

Where the venue’s policy stops

The Oklahoma department notes that event sites “may have their own liability insurance.”1 That policy is bought for the venue and its own staff. A claim that grows out of something you brought in, such as an outside facilitator or a guided hike, can land on you instead. Your contract sets the split, so read its insurance clause next to its indemnification clause.

Ask about alcohol by name. The department tells organizers to be clear on whether the event liability policy includes host liquor liability,1 the part that answers claims tied to drinks served at your event. If anyone pours wine at dinner, get that answer in writing.

When each part is worth buying

Buy liability coverage if the venue contract requires it, or if your schedule holds activities the venue does not run. A contract may ask for a certificate of insurance before arrival, with the venue named on it. A group that meets in a room the venue staffs carries less exposure than one that brings its own guides and boats.

Price cancellation coverage if the money at risk is large and the deposit terms are strict. Take the venue’s cancellation schedule and write down what you would owe on each date in it, then add the vendor deposits. That total is what a cancellation policy would need to cover. It grows as the event gets closer. If the contract lets you move dates without a fee, or returns most of the deposit, you need the policy less.

Before you buy

Put these questions in one email to the insurer or broker, and file the answers with the venue contract.

  • Is the company licensed to sell insurance in the state where the retreat takes place?
  • Does the quote cover liability and cancellation, or only one of them?
  • Which causes of cancellation does the policy cover, and which does it exclude?
  • Is there a deadline to buy cancellation coverage after the first deposit is paid?
  • Does the liability policy include host liquor liability?
  • Does the liability coverage reach activities run by outside vendors you hire?
  • Can the venue be named on the certificate, and how soon can the certificate be issued?
  • What limit does the venue contract require, and does the quote meet it?

Price the policy while the venue contract is still being settled, when the deposit amounts and the required limits are known and the first large payment has not gone out. Ask the insurer to send the certificate straight to the venue contact named in the contract, and keep your copy in one folder with the contract and the policy. Know where that folder is the night before arrival. Then check the other side of the table: ask the venue for its certificate before you sign.

Rules for the awkward cases

If you decide to call the event off yourself, the policy will not treat that as bad luck. The Oklahoma department says reimbursement for event costs is “not typically covered if the event is called off voluntarily,”1 and a policy pays for the causes it names, such as severe weather or a vendor who never shows. A change of heart is not on the list.

If an activity on your final schedule shows up in the policy’s exclusions, you have four ways out, and they run from easy to tedious: move the activity, drop it, ask the insurer to add it, and failing that, get written proof that the vendor who runs it carries its own coverage. Pick one before the schedule goes to the group. After that it is a negotiation with everyone who signed up.

If the group grows after you buy, ask the insurer whether the policy was written for a set number of guests. If it was, the bigger group needs a new answer in writing.

Planner Guide

Choose Team Retreat Activities by Goal and Venue Fit

October 11, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Pick team retreat activities by the goal each one serves, then rate the effort each asks of people and test it against what the venue can host and who can take part.

Start with the goal, then pick the activity

Planners can start with a list of ideas and look for the ones that sound fun. Ask whether that order serves the team, or only fills the schedule. Consider reversing it. Write down what the group needs from the retreat, then choose activities that serve each need. Ask the group lead whether the team needs time to talk or rest more than a program.

It helps to sort every idea into one of four types: a conversation starter, a joint task with a finished product, quiet time, or a lesson in a new skill. Then ask the venue what each type would need. For a conversation starter, ask about a room with movable chairs. For a joint task, ask about a floor, tables and a clear space for the finished product. For quiet time, ask about a calm spot and nearby restrooms. For a lesson, ask about power outlets, a screen or tools.

Rate the effort each activity asks of people

A team may include people who jog before breakfast and people who have not exercised in years. Before you book a hike, a relay or a ropes course, judge how hard it will feel to the least fit person in the room. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention gives descriptions of moderate and vigorous effort.1

Its page for adults says that moderate effort is the kind where "you'll be able to talk, but not sing the words to your favorite song." For vigorous effort it says "you won't be able to say more than a few words without pausing for a breath."1 Among its examples of moderate effort it lists walking fast and water aerobics. Among vigorous examples it lists jogging or running, swimming laps and playing basketball.1 Walk through your agenda with those descriptions and label each block as talking pace or gasping pace.

The labels let you sequence the day. If a gasping-pace block comes right before a long strategy meeting, allow recovery time between them. Leave time between them for water and a change of clothes, and check that the meeting does not start before that time ends. Ask the venue whether showers or changing spaces exist and where they are. Also allow time for the walk between buildings.

Check the weather and the body

Outdoor activities add a second question: what will the heat do to the people taking part? The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention page called About Heat and Your Health says that hot days can affect anyone.2 It says that if your body gets too hot, you can get sick, and it lists symptoms of overheating that include muscle cramping, dizziness and nausea.2

The same page says some people are at greater risk. Among the groups it names are people sixty-five years of age or older, people who are working or exercising outside in the heat, and infants and young children.2 Build the plan so nobody has to explain a health condition to join the group.

For staying cool outside, the page lists items for when its HeatRisk level is red or above: to stay in the shade as much as possible, take breaks when you can, and do outdoor activities during the coolest parts of the day or evening, if possible. It also says to drink plenty of fluids.2 Turn each of those measures into a venue question. Is cool drinking water available at the activity site, or must the group carry it? Is there shade near the field, the trail or the dock, and how many people does it hold? Is there an indoor space close by where people can cool down? If the answer is no, schedule outdoor blocks for the coolest part of the day you can, and keep each one short.

The page does not give a temperature at which an activity should be cancelled, so that decision belongs to your group. Pick a person with the authority to shorten or move a session, and tell the facilitators who that person is. Settle the rule before the retreat, when nobody is hot and annoyed, and write it in the facilitator notes.

Ask who can join, and how

Decide before booking whether every person on the team can take part in each activity. The ADA National Network lists a recorded webinar called Best Practices for Inclusive Recreation Programs. Its description speaks of making recreation programs inclusive and accessible to individuals with a range of disabilities and ages.3 The topics it lists include advertising, registration and the accommodation process, along with issues such as one-to-one support, behavior policies, medication and personal care.3

That page is a description of a training session. It does not state what a planner must provide, and it does not say which rules apply to a private retreat. For the legal side, ask a lawyer about your own case. Ask the venue about its property rules and policies. Use its list of topics as a prompt for conversations with your group and the venue.

Start with the group. Send a short note before the retreat that describes the activities in plain terms, including how far people will walk, how long they will stand and what the ground is like. Ask attendees to tell you what they need, and give them a person to tell, so they do not have to address the whole team. Ask about medication timing and about anyone who may need a support person at an activity.

Then ask the venue. Which routes between the sleeping rooms, the meeting rooms and the activity sites can someone use with a wheelchair, a walker or a cane? Are the restrooms near the activity site reachable by those routes? Can the outdoor activity move to a different spot if the first one is hard to reach? If an activity has no version that everyone can join, replace it with one that does.

Put the venue answers in writing

When the shortlist is ready, build a simple table. Put the activity and its type in the left column and the venue answers that matter in the right column. For a group puzzle, the right column might note the table size, the number of chairs and whether the room is quiet. For a hike, it might note the route length, the shade, the water and the nearest restroom. Scan for any row with a no in a critical field. Replace that activity or change the venue.

Send the venue an email that repeats the shortlist and asks for a written answer to each point. Ask about equipment the venue owns and equipment you would need to bring. Ask whether outside vendors, such as a facilitator or a cooking instructor, may work on the property. Ask what insurance the venue expects from outside vendors and whether it expects proof. Ask a lawyer and your insurer how a vendor contract would affect your own event.

Label each reply with the activity it supports and compare the replies with your table. Any mismatch is a sign to change the agenda before it is final. Hold a weather backup for each outdoor block, and write the name of the indoor room next to it. Share the finished table with the group lead for approval, and give a copy to every facilitator.

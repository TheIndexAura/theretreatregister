Check Walls, Noise and Sleep Before Booking a Couples Retreat Venue

July 20, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Before booking a couples retreat venue, ask what is on the other side of the wall, how the entrance is arranged, what sounds carry near the unit, and what the CDC and NIDCD list about sleep and loud sounds.

Ask what sits on the other side of the wall

A listing can say private cabin or secluded suite, and neither phrase tells you what is next door. Privacy is a question about construction and layout, so ask about construction and layout. Ask whether the unit shares a wall, a floor or a ceiling with another guest unit. Ask what the nearest building is, and what is above and below the bathroom.

Ask for a site plan or property map, since one may not appear on the website. Ask the venue to mark your unit on it. A map may show whether the units sit in a row or are scattered, how far each is from the main building, where the parking is and where any event lawn sits. It takes a minute to read.

Entrances need the same care. A door that opens straight outside is one arrangement, and a door onto a shared porch is another. Ask whether the entrance is shared, and whether the outdoor seat belongs to your unit or sits in view of the next one. Ask how far the unit is from dining and from the front desk, whether the path is lit and surfaced, and whether a guest with a mobility need can use it.

Photographs deserve a second look. Look at exterior shots as well as interior ones, and count the doors in the frame. A cabin photographed from one narrow angle may be one end of a longer structure.

Learn what the sound numbers mean

Noise is one part of privacy, and it helps to have a yardstick. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders lists average decibel ratings of some familiar sounds. It gives normal conversation as sixty to seventy A-weighted decibels, a movie theater as seventy-four to one hundred four, and a fireworks show as one hundred forty to one hundred sixty.1

The same page says sounds at or below seventy A-weighted decibels, even after long exposure, are unlikely to cause hearing loss.1 It also says long or repeated exposure to sounds at or above eighty-five A-weighted decibels can cause hearing loss.1 Those statements are about hearing health. The page does not say how loud is too loud for a quiet weekend, and it does not rate hotel rooms or cabins.

So treat the numbers as a vocabulary and not as a pass mark. Nobody needs to arrive with a meter. The useful move is to turn the numbers into questions for the venue. Ask what produces sound near your unit, such as a road, a kitchen or an event space, and whether a pool or pump house sits close. Ask which hours the amplified music is allowed, and whether any event is booked on your dates.

If the venue holds weddings, ask for its event calendar and read it before you commit to dates. Ask whether a wedding or other event would use the lawn or the staff on your dates, and whether its sound system could be heard from your unit. Ask whether the property is ever sold whole to one group, because a full buyout changes who your neighbors are.

Check the room against the CDC sleep tips

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists keeping your bedroom quiet, relaxing, and at a cool temperature.2 That gives you a short list to check against the unit. Ask how loud the heating and cooling equipment is, whether the guest can set the temperature in the room, and whether the window faces a path, a parking area or a pool.

The CDC page gives amounts of sleep by age group. It lists seven or more hours for ages eighteen to sixty, seven to nine hours for ages sixty-one to sixty-four, and seven to eight hours for ages sixty-five and older.2 Ask the venue whether it can offer a unit away from early kitchen noise and morning deliveries, and what the unit is like at six in the morning and at ten at night, and which side of the property stays quietest, since the venue knows its own grounds.

The same page lists going to bed and getting up at the same time every day, and turning off electronic devices thirty minutes or more before bedtime.2 A retreat is a break from routine, so decide with your couple which tips suit them and which they would skip. Since the page lists turning off electronic devices, ask whether the unit has a clock radio or a television, and whether either can be switched off. Ask whether the curtains close fully and whether the lamps dim.

Shape food and the schedule around two

The CDC page also lists avoiding large meals and alcohol before bedtime, and avoiding caffeine in the afternoon or evening.2 That is a fair reason to look at how dinner works. Ask whether the kitchen serves a fixed menu at a fixed hour, and whether a guest can request a different dish. Ask whether meals are served at set times or across a window, and whether food can be brought to the unit.

Ask whether the unit has a kitchenette and a way to make coffee without getting dressed. Ask whether the dining room has tables for two or only shared seating. Ask which nights the kitchen is closed, and what is available to eat when it is. A couple who want a late, slow dinner need to know before they arrive that the kitchen shuts at a set hour.

Activities follow the same logic. Decide whether your pair wants group sessions or a mixer. Ask which activities are bookable for a single party, which are group only, and which can be skipped without losing a fee. Ask who else is on the property at those times, and whether the spa or pool has quiet hours posted.

Put the answers in writing

Words like adults-focused can mean different things at different places. Ask the venue for its actual policy on children and for the dates when children are on site. Ask for the pool and hot tub rules, and whether a hot tub is private to your unit or shared behind the main building with posted hours.

Once you have the answers, ask for them in an email or in the booking agreement. Name the specific unit, the arrival and departure times, the meal arrangements and any quiet hours the venue has promised. A promise made in a chat or in a brochure is easy to lose. A line in the agreement can be pointed to later by whoever is at the front desk.

Keep a record of the questions you asked and the replies. If two venues give different answers about the same wall or the same lawn, that difference is useful when you choose. If a venue will not say what is next to the unit, or whether an event is booked, note that, and weigh it when you compare. Compare the replies side by side before you pay a deposit, and decide in advance which of them would change your choice.

Plan Breakout Rooms: Count Groups, Set Rules, Check Exits

September 5, 2026 · The Retreat Register

If sessions run in parallel, ask how many rooms the agenda calls for. Count the most groups meeting at the same hour, decide what each room must hold, and ask the venue how its doors and exits are arranged for the rooms you would use.

Count the rooms the agenda needs

A main hall can look impressive on a tour and still be the wrong answer for a day that splits into small groups. Start with the agenda, not the building. List every block where participants divide into separate cohorts, write the start and end time beside each one, and lay the blocks over each other on a single timeline. Take the hour with the most groups meeting at once as your starting number of rooms, and give that number to the venue first. Ask the venue whether groups could share a larger space with a divider, and what it says about sound between them.

Then decide what a room has to hold for each group. A discussion circle of eight needs chairs and a clear floor. A design workshop needs a table, and perhaps a wall for paper. A group that will pass around laptops needs outlets within reach. Write the needs down per session, because a venue that says yes to rooms for ten people may mean ten people in rows of chairs facing a screen, which is a different thing from ten people around a table with notebooks.

Add a few spare seats to every count for late arrivals and for the facilitator, and decide whether the facilitator sits with the group or stands. Then decide how rooms are assigned. You can give each session a room for the whole retreat, so signs and materials stay in place. You can also rotate groups, which may reduce the number of rooms you need but adds walking time between sessions. Either choice is fine, and either one belongs in the first message you send the venue.

Set working agreements before the groups split

Once participants disperse, decide how the habits that keep a session useful will reach every room. Extension staff at the University of Minnesota write about this in a page on working agreements for productive discussions. It says that in smaller groups, creating expectations together as part of the meeting is a helpful first step.1 It also says groups can create working agreements in advance of the meeting, or with the group at the meeting.1

That gives a planner two workable options for breakouts. You can draft a short list ahead of time and bring it to each room, or you can give each group a few minutes at the start of its session to write its own. Choose one before the retreat and tell the facilitators which it is. If participants move between sessions, decide whether every room should follow the same routine.

The same page says that having nine or fewer working agreements helps the group more easily remember and support them.1 Decide where the list goes up in each room, since a facilitator may want it in view. It adds that once working agreements are clarified, leaders should confirm with the group that these practices will guide the discussion.1 Plan for that confirmation as a spoken moment with a show of hands, and give it a place in the first five minutes of each session.

Treat group size and room choice as your own decisions, based on the activity and on how much talking each person should get.

Ask about doors and exits

Ask the venue how each room you plan to use is arranged for getting out. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration publishes rules on exit routes for workplaces. They are written about employees and workplaces. Whether they apply to your event is a question for a lawyer, and how the doors and exits are arranged is a question for the venue.

The rule on the number of routes says two or more exit routes must be available in a workplace to permit prompt evacuation of employees and other building occupants during an emergency.2 The same page says a single exit route is permitted where all employees would be able to evacuate safely during an emergency.2 For a breakout room, ask the venue this: how does a person in this room get outside, and is there more than one way? Ask about each small room, including any at the end of a corridor.

The page also covers doors. It says an exit door must be unlocked, and it says employees must be able to open an exit route door from the inside at all times without keys, tools, or special knowledge.2 Ask the venue about the doors on the exit paths from your rooms, especially if the building is locked at certain hours or if one of the spaces is a converted storage room. Ask who holds keys and when the doors are secured.

Door swing comes up as well. The page says a door connecting a room to an exit route must swing out in the direction of exit travel if the room is designed to be occupied by more than fifty people or if the room is a high hazard area.2 Your breakout rooms may be well under that size, so the figure may not apply to your plan. It does mean the venue should know how its doors are hung, and you can ask.

Keep the exit path clear and plan the hardware

A second page from the same agency covers the upkeep of exit routes. It says exit routes must be free and unobstructed, and that no materials or equipment may be placed, either permanently or temporarily, within the exit route.3 This is the sentence a breakout planner should keep in mind while setting up. Think about where flip charts, easels, boxes of supplies, a rolling cart of markers and extra chairs will sit.

Decide where each group stores its materials, and tell the facilitators before the first session. Walk each room the day before, or on arrival, and stand where the door is. Look at the path from every seat to that door and move anything that sits in it. If a room is too small to hold the group and its equipment while keeping the way out clear, treat that as a reason to switch rooms or trim the equipment.

The page also lists lighting and signs. Each exit route must be adequately lighted so that an employee with normal vision can see along it, and each exit must be clearly visible and marked by a sign reading Exit.3 It adds that each exit route door must be free of decorations or signs that obscure the visibility of the door.3 That last point matters for organizers who like to label rooms. A paper sign with the name of a session can be taped to a wall or an easel instead of the door that leads out.

Ask the venue about sound and cost

Sound between rooms is a matter to raise with the venue. Ask what the walls between the breakout rooms are made of and whether they reach the ceiling. Ask whether the doors close fully and whether any room opens onto a shared hallway, a kitchen or a lobby where staff are working. Ask what else is booked next door at the hours your sessions run, since a quiet room beside a wedding rehearsal is a different thing from the same room beside an empty office.

Visit the rooms with two people if you can, and have one of them talk at a normal volume in one room while the other listens from the next. It takes a few minutes. If the sound carries, ask whether sessions that need quiet can be placed in rooms that do not share a wall, and whether louder activities can be placed together.

Ask for a quote that lists each space and each service on its own line, so you can see what a room costs by itself and what extras are added for equipment, cleaning or staff time. Ask whether a room is yours alone for the dates, or whether it may be used by someone else between your sessions. Finish with a written summary that lists the number of rooms, the layout for each, who holds the keys and where each group will store its materials. Send it to the venue and the facilitators.

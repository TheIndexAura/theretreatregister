Plan a Texas Hill Country Retreat: What Planners Check Before Booking

August 15, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Before a group books a ranch or lodge in the Texas Hill Country, run four checks that a venue's photos do not answer: the roads in and the creeks they cross, the local alcohol rules, any county burn ban, and the heat on your dates. Each one changes the plan, and each one belongs in writing before you sign.

Check the road in, and every creek it crosses

Look at the last stretch of road between the highway and the property. If it dips through a creek bed, that dip is a low-water crossing, and on a dry afternoon it looks like nothing at all. After a hard rain the same dip can carry fast water across the road.

The National Weather Service is blunt about what that water can do. It says more deaths occur from flooding each year than from any other thunderstorm hazard, and it reports, from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, that over half of flood-related drownings happen when a vehicle is driven into flood water.1 It adds that about a foot of rushing water can carry away most cars, and that a flooded road may have collapsed under the water you can see.1

So the route belongs in the plan from the first email. Ask the venue in writing whether the road from the highway crosses any low-water crossings, and whether there is a second way in that stays high when the first one floods. Ask what the venue does when a flood watch is posted for the county, and whether guests have ever been asked to arrive early or stay put. A venue that hosts groups often should have a written answer, and you want it before the deposit goes out.

Then build the rule into the run sheet. If a flood watch or warning is posted for the area on travel day, ask the venue in writing how the road looks before anyone leaves, and give the shuttle driver the second route. Nobody drives around a barrier to save a few minutes. Say it to the drivers out loud, and then put it on the sheet they carry.

Give every driver the street address and the second route the day before the trip. If the group arrives in several cars, set one meeting point in town where everyone can wait together if the road closes. Cell signal on a ranch road can come and go, so print the route.

Check the alcohol rules where the venue actually sits

Texas does not have one rule for alcohol sales everywhere. TABC, the state agency that regulates alcohol sales, explains that voters hold local option elections to decide what types of alcoholic beverages can be sold in their communities, and that those elections are held by counties, by cities, or by individual justice of the peace precincts.2 A ranch outside town and a hotel inside it can fall under different answers.

TABC also says that only the city or county clerk's office can officially declare a location wet or dry.2 It publishes a wet and dry map as a starting point, and the clerk's office is where the final word lives.

TABC says most counties and cities allow some types of alcohol sales, and as of March 2025 it counted sixty completely wet counties and three completely dry ones.2 The gaps sit in between, where one kind of drink is allowed and another is not. Read the rule for the precinct where the venue sits.

This matters the moment the agenda says wine with dinner. Ask the venue in writing whether it can serve alcohol to your group and whether guests may bring their own. Ask which permit covers it. If the venue says the caterer handles it, ask the caterer the same question in writing and keep the reply. Then plan the evening so that it still works if the answer turns out to be no, with good food and a long table worth staying at.

One more question belongs here. If the group will stop in town on the way in to buy supplies, check the rules for that town as well, since the store on the highway and the venue on the ranch road can sit in different precincts with different answers.

Check for a burn ban before you promise a campfire

If the program ends around a fire pit, that is the first part of the weekend a dry season can take away. The Texas A&M Forest Service explains that when drought conditions exist, a county judge or the court that governs the county can put a burn ban in place that prohibits or restricts outdoor burning for public safety.3 The forest service keeps a burn ban map, so you can see which counties have one in force.

A ban is set county by county, and it can start or end close to your dates. Check the map when you book. Check it again the week before, and once more on the morning of the evening session. Ask the venue in writing whether its fire pit or outdoor grill falls under a county ban, and what it offers instead when one is in place.

Plan the evening so the fire is a bonus. If the closing session is a talk around the fire, write a version that works on a covered porch with lanterns and chairs pulled close.

Plan the schedule around the heat on your dates

If your dates fall in the warm months, plan the day by the clock. Put the hike and any outdoor working session early in the morning, and move the long indoor sessions into the hottest part of the afternoon.

Ask the venue in writing which meeting rooms are air conditioned, how far they are from the sleeping cabins, and whether there is shade along the path between them. Ask where guests can refill water bottles without walking to the main house, and whether a covered outdoor space exists for any session that has to be outside. If a session moves outdoors, keep water at the spot before the group arrives and give people permission to step out when they need to. Build real breaks into every outdoor block. Short ones count. Say in the welcome note which hours are kept indoors, so nobody books a long walk or a horseback ride for the middle of the afternoon.

Spring and fall dates change this part of the plan. Whatever the season, ask what else is booked on the property that weekend, since another group may share the paths and the dining room.

Put every answer in one place

By the time a venue makes your shortlist, you should hold four written answers from it: how the group gets in when the creeks are up, what can be served at dinner, whether the fire pit is allowed this month, and how the cabins and meeting rooms handle the heat. Paste the replies into one shared document, with the date each one arrived.

A venue that answers all four quickly and in plain words has told you something about how it runs a weekend.

Send each venue on your list the same message, so the replies line up side by side: "Before booking, please tell us how guests reach you after heavy rain and what you do when the county has a burn ban."

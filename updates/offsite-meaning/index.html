Plan a Company Offsite: Purpose, Site, Agenda and Records

October 27, 2026 · The Retreat Register

An offsite is a work meeting held away from the usual office. Ask first whether the group has a clear purpose for it, then look at the agenda, the site, the contract questions and the records, and check each step against published guidance.

What an offsite is

An offsite is a work meeting held somewhere other than the usual office, whether for one day or several. Planning guides for meetings in general still apply once the room is across town or across the country, and a planner can use them as a starting point.

The foundation guide says the meeting organizer has a clear purpose for holding a meeting.1 A planner can answer these questions in writing before looking at any venue. Who is on the guest list? How many days does the group need? What budget is available? Do participants handle their own long-distance transportation? A short written answer to each one gives you something to hand to a venue, to finance and to your own leaders.

Start with the purpose and the agenda

University of Minnesota Extension gives a sequence of steps for planning an agenda. The first step is to define results, and the page says that if there is not a defined purpose, consider whether there really is a reason to meet.2 That question is worth asking about an offsite in particular. Decide whether the travel and venue costs of an offsite are worth it. If the purpose is a status update, ask whether a regular meeting would do the job.

The remaining steps follow in order: decide the time frame, list the topics, allot time for each topic, plan how people will take part in each one, plan time for closure, and do a reality check.2 The closure step reviews what needs to happen next and, perhaps, asks for feedback. For the reality check, the page says to scale back expectations or schedule another meeting to address some of the topics. A group with remote guests needs its own plan for them.

The foundation guide adds a rule of thumb for building the schedule. It says to double the time for each session and cut back the items on the agenda.1 It also says experienced trainers indicate that the maximum amount of time people can focus is about five to six hours.1 For questions, it gives one rule of thumb, one minute of questions for each minute of presentation.1 Check each of these against your own group before you adopt it. Decide how much time your own group needs for introductions and how much for the hardest topic.

The guide also matches group size to the goal. For decision-making it names fifteen people as the top size, for think tanks thirty unless attendees are broken up into smaller groups, and for skill building twenty.1 Those are the guide's figures for meetings in general. If your guest list is larger than the figure for your goal, decide in advance whether you will cut the list or split the group.

Choose a site that fits the purpose

The foundation guide says think tanks and brainstorming are thought to benefit from more relaxed and unusual settings, and it advises considering a resort only if the attendees will have a chance to use the facilities.1 If your offsite is all work, ask yourself whether guests will have any free hours to use what a resort offers. Ask each venue what kind of group it is set up to host and how it feeds and houses a group of your size.

The guide also tells the planner to develop a site checklist tailored to the meeting.1 It says to visit each meeting room, check for adjustable lighting, and test to see that sound-proofing is adequate.1 Add your own items to the checklist. Ask whether the room has windows and whether guests can take a break outdoors, since you may care about both. Ask whether every meeting room is reachable without stairs, and what transportation is available to and from the site for guests who need it. Those are questions for the venue, and the guide does not give the answers.

On timing, the guide says that for popular locations and times of the year, it may be necessary to select a site a year or more in advance.1 Its planning timeline puts site selection six to twelve months out.1 It puts informing attendees at four to six months out, and it advises them to self-identify special needs, including dietary needs and food allergies.1 Ask the venue how far ahead it books your dates, and ask whether the dates you want are already held.

Compare venues and read the contract

For the contract, the foundation guide says to try to have two or three hotel options.1 It says one complimentary guest room night usually comes with a certain number of room nights booked.1 Your venue may offer a different ratio, so ask what it offers for your group size. Consider asking each option for a written quote, and decide in advance how you will compare the quotes side by side.

The same guide says to include a requirement that the contractor must meet the ADA guidelines for facility accessibility.1 The guide does not say how to word that clause. Ask the venue which accessible rooms and features it holds for your dates, and ask it to put the answers in writing. For what the accessibility rules require of a given building, ask a lawyer.

The guide is an older document, so check current accessibility rules with a lawyer. Questions about food and beverage spending, parking and internet speed belong in your own list for the venue. A venue answers for its own policies, so write the replies into your venue comparison.

Plan the days and the money

The foundation guide includes advice about the first evening. It says to begin think tank meetings with an evening meal or a social event so introductions take place before the meetings.1 The guide does not list formats for the rest of the day. When you build the agenda, decide how much of the time is presentation, how much is discussion, and whether some of it should happen in smaller groups.

Money questions belong with finance early. The Internal Revenue Service article on travel expenses lists lodging and non-entertainment-related meals among deductible travel expenses while away from home, and it says you can't deduct expenses that are for personal purposes.3 It says the deduction for business meals is generally limited to fifty percent of the unreimbursed cost. It also says that instead of keeping records of meal expenses and deducting the actual cost, you can generally use a standard meal allowance.3 It also says that generally your tax home is the entire city or general area where your main place of business or work is located.3 That article is about travel for the person taking the trip. It does not describe a company offsite as such, so ask your accountant how it applies to your event.

The article stresses good records, and it refers readers to Topic Number three hundred five.3 Ask your accountant which records to keep for a trip. Decide who keeps the receipts, the contract, the agenda and the attendee list for the offsite, and agree on where they are stored before anyone travels.

Before the meeting ends, use the closure step. The extension page says to plan for a few minutes at the end of the agenda to review what needs to happen next and, perhaps, to ask for feedback.2 The foundation guide asks planners to allow time to fill out evaluation forms before the end of the meeting, since otherwise people leave without completing them.1 Write down who owns each action item.

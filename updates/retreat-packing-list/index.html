Retreat Packing List: What Guests Bring and What Stays Home

November 11, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Pack for the weather and the walking, check what the venue supplies, and keep a phone within reach for emergencies. A retreat packing checklist starts with those three steps and builds from there.

The list changes when the retreat asks for silence or when the venue provides bedding.

How to read the venue list

Ask the organizer for the venue supply list in writing or by email. That list tells you whether sheets and towels appear in the room or whether you bring your own. If the list does not mention bedding then you pack a sleeping bag rated for the expected low temperature. If the list does mention bedding then you leave the sleeping bag at home.

Read the list for kitchen access. Some venues stock a shared pantry and others run every meal through a dining hall. When the venue provides meals you write your allergies and any cross-contact questions in an email to the organizer who forwards them to the kitchen. The kitchen should explain how it keeps your food separate from other dishes.

Check the list for laundry. A weekend retreat rarely needs it. A week-long stay may offer a washer and dryer or a line outside. Pack enough underwear and socks for each day plus one spare set. Do not pack extra shirts hoping to wash them unless the list confirms machines.

Look for a fire extinguisher note. Guests never bring a fire extinguisher. You ask the organizer where the venue keeps the nearest one and you make a mental note of the location when you arrive.

Clothing for weather and walking

Start with the forecast for the dates of the retreat. Pack a base layer that wicks sweat, a mid layer that traps heat, and a shell that blocks wind and rain. That combination covers cold mornings and warm afternoons without bulky coats.

Footwear comes next. Bring broken-in hiking shoes or trail runners for uneven ground. Add camp shoes or sandals for indoor use if the venue asks you to remove outdoor shoes at the door. Pack wool or synthetic socks equal to the number of days plus one pair. Cotton socks stay damp and cause blisters.

Headwear matters more than people expect. A brimmed hat shades the face on long walks. A wool beanie retains heat at night when temperatures drop. Gloves follow the same logic. Light liner gloves work for cool mornings. Insulated gloves go in the pack if the forecast shows freezing lows.

Clothing for silent retreats follows the same rules but avoids bright logos and loud fabrics. Neutral colors reduce visual distraction for everyone. Avoid zippers that clatter and Velcro that rips. Test each garment at home by moving your arms and sitting cross-legged. If it binds or makes noise you leave it out.

Sleep gear and toiletries

When the venue supplies bedding you pack only a pillowcase if you prefer your own fabric. When the venue does not supply bedding you pack a sleeping bag rated for the expected low temperature and a sleeping pad if the floor is hard. A compressible pillow stuff sack filled with clothes saves space.

Toiletries stay in a waterproof bag that fits on a shared bathroom shelf. Toothbrush, toothpaste, floss, deodorant, and any prescribed skin care. Skip scented products when the retreat asks for a fragrance-free environment. The request usually appears in the welcome email.

Towels depend on the venue list. If the list says towels provided you bring a quick-dry hand towel for personal use. If the list says bring towels you pack one bath towel and one hand towel per person. Microfiber dries faster than cotton and takes less room.

Earplugs and an eye mask belong in every kit. Shared rooms and early light disrupt sleep. Silicone earplugs block more sound than foam. A contoured eye mask presses less on the eyelids than a flat one.

Medications and food needs

Prescriptions stay in the original bottle with the pharmacy label visible. Count the doses for each day of the retreat plus two extra days in case travel changes. Place the bottles in a clear zip bag at the top of your pack so you can show them if asked.

Over-the-counter items follow the same rule. Pain reliever, antihistamine, anti-diarrheal, and any supplement you take daily. Write the generic name on a piece of paper inside the bag in case the label wears off.

If you carry an auto-injector or inhaler you keep it on your person at all times. A waist pack or jacket pocket works. Do not bury it in a suitcase. Tell one other guest or the organizer where you keep it and how to use it.

Special food items that the kitchen cannot provide go in a sealed container labeled with your name. Email the organizer a list of those items before you pack so the kitchen can plan storage. Do not assume refrigeration exists unless the venue list confirms it.

Electronics and quiet rules

A phone must stay reachable for emergencies. Silence the ringer and turn off notifications. If the retreat asks for quiet you keep the phone in a pocket or bag on vibrate. You do not leave it in the car or a locker. The phone itself remains a safety tool.

Bring a charger and a cable that matches your device. A small power bank helps if outlets are scarce. Label the cable with tape and your initials. Do not bring a tablet or laptop unless the organizer confirms a work session or study period.

Headphones stay in the pack unless the schedule includes guided audio. If you use a meditation app you download the tracks before you arrive. Streaming requires signal that many retreat sites lack.

Flashlights or headlamps replace phone lights at night. A headlamp with a red mode preserves night vision and does not disturb roommates. Pack spare batteries. Test the light before you leave home.

Lay every item on the floor the night before departure. Compare the pile to the retreat packing checklist the organizer sent. Cross off each line with a pen. Cross off each line by hand and any gaps show.

Weigh the packed bag. If it exceeds the airline limit or the vehicle capacity you remove non-essentials. Extra clothing goes first. Extra toiletries go second. The sleeping bag stays if the venue does not supply bedding.

Email the organizer a one-line confirmation that you have packed according to the list and that you understand the quiet policy and the meal arrangements. That email creates a written record for both sides. You carry a printed copy in your bag.

Place your identification and insurance card in an outer pocket. Leave the itinerary off the luggage tag. A name and phone number suffice. The bag is ready when it closes and the email sends.

The list works when you treat it as a contract between you and the venue. You check the venue supply list in writing, you pack for the forecast, you keep medications labeled and reachable, and you keep the phone on your person.

When you arrive you know where the fire extinguisher hangs and you have already asked the kitchen how it protects your food. The bag is light enough to carry up a hill. The organizer has your confirmation email. You arrive with the list and the confirmation email in hand.

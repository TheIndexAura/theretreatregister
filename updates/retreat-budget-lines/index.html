Planner Guide

Plan the Retreat Budget Rows First-Time Planners Forget

September 4, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A quote fills the lodging and meals rows and leaves the rest of the sheet empty. Rows for transport, supplies, insurance and deposits arrive later. The IRS separates tips from service charges, and a federal rule addresses total price disclosure for some goods and services. Get the sheet approved in writing before signing.

Build the sheet around categories, not quotes

A first retreat budget can start with two rows, lodging and meals. The venue quote fills both, the total looks reasonable, and the planner moves on. A quote describes what the venue sells. It does not describe what the group needs around the venue, and every need left off the sheet is a cost nobody has looked at yet.

Start the sheet with a row for every category of spending, even the ones you expect to be empty. Give each row three columns: what you expect to pay, what you actually paid, and notes. The notes column holds the vendor contact and the contract reference. Where you have a written quote, copy it in. Where you do not, write that a quote was requested and the date you asked.

Never leave a cell blank. A row with no cost gets the word none, so that nobody opens the sheet in a month and wonders whether the row was forgotten or truly free. This small habit also gives the person who approves spending something concrete to read, because every category is named and every cell holds an answer.

Give gratuities and service charges their own row

Food and drink need a row that separates two things a contract can contain: a charge the venue adds, and a tip the group decides to give. The IRS draws that line on its tip recordkeeping page, which covers how tips and service charges are treated for federal tax purposes. It says that extra amounts charged to a bill, such as mandatory service charges, are not tips.1 It also says a payment is a tip only if the customer voluntarily decides to pay it and determines the amount.1

The same page gives examples of service charges. One is an automatic charge added to a bill, and the example it uses is a mandatory charge for large parties. Another is a required gratuity included in a contract or invoice.1 The page adds that a business or worker describing a payment as a tip is not determinative.1 For your sheet, that means a contract line labeled gratuity deserves a closer read. Ask the venue whether the contract makes it required, and if it does, enter it as a fixed charge instead of a tip.

The page does not say what any particular venue charges or who receives the money. Those are questions for the venue, since the venue answers for its own policy. Ask in writing whether a service charge applies, which food and drink it covers, and whether it replaces tipping for the staff who serve the group. Then decide whether the group will add anything beyond it, and put that decision in the row.

Read the total price before you trust a quote

Fees that sit outside the headline rate are the other place a budget can lose track of money. A federal rule on hidden fees, found at section four hundred sixty-four point two of title sixteen of the Code of Federal Regulations, speaks to this for the goods and services it covers. It says it is an unfair and deceptive practice for a business to offer, display or advertise any price of a covered good or service without clearly and conspicuously disclosing the total price.2 It also says that before the consumer consents to pay, the business must disclose the nature, purpose and amount of any fee or charge excluded from the total price, along with the final amount of payment.2

The definitions in the neighboring section describe total price as the maximum total of all fees or charges a consumer must pay for goods or services, plus any mandatory ancillary good or service.3 They name two covered goods or services only: live-event tickets, and short-term lodging, including temporary sleeping accommodations at a hotel, motel, inn, short-term rental, vacation rental or other place of lodging.3 The definitions do not mention catering or the other budget lines on your sheet, and whether the rule reaches any of them is a question for a lawyer. The definitions also say that government charges, shipping charges and fees for an optional ancillary good or service may be excluded from the total price.3

The definitions do not say whether a group booking for a business retreat counts, and the rule speaks of consumers. A lawyer is the person to ask whether the rule applies to your contract. The venue is the person to ask about its own fees, and the habit works either way. Ask the venue for a written list of every fee and charge that applies to your dates, and compare it to the sheet row by row. If a charge appears that the quote did not mention, ask why before signing.

Add transport, supplies, insurance and deposits

Ground transport needs its own row. Someone has to move the group from the airport or station to the venue and back, so ask whether the venue includes a shuttle in its quote, and add a note on how people are arriving. Session supplies come next. Ask each facilitator whether they bring handouts, flip charts, markers or props, and who pays for the printing and shipping. One email to the facilitator settles that row before the contract is signed.

Insurance gets a row of its own. Ask the venue what coverage it carries, and ask an insurance professional whether that coverage reaches your group and whether the group needs separate cover. Put each answer in the notes column, along with the name of the person who gave it, so the sheet records where the advice came from. Damage deposits belong on the sheet too. Ask whether the deposit is refundable and when it comes back, because the money leaves your account before the event and the budget has to carry it until it returns.

Coffee and snacks outside meal times need a row as well. Ask whether breaks are included in the food and beverage package or charged separately, and whether refills or extra pots of coffee carry their own charge when a session runs long. Ask about the fee questions that decide the venue rows before you fill in lodging and meals, since the nightly rate may leave out charges that the venue adds at the end.

Get the sheet approved and keep it current

Add one row labeled contingency. Its size is a decision for whoever signs off on the budget, and it is easier to agree on a figure before anything goes wrong than after. Put the agreed figure in the expected column. Then send the whole sheet to the people who approve spending and ask for approval in writing, with one plain line: please confirm you approve this budget, including the contingency row, before I sign the venue contract.

From that point the approved sheet is the budget. A later change goes in as a new line with a short reason beside it, and you check the running total against the approved one each time an invoice arrives. When the final invoice comes, copy each amount into the actual column. If the caterer has added a late coffee service that nobody listed, it goes in as a new line under coffee and snacks, with the invoice reference in the notes.

The gap between the two columns is the first place to look when a total runs over. If the total stays inside the contingency, record that in the notes. If it does not, write down why and ask for approval of the extra amount before the next payment goes out. Keep a printed copy of the approved sheet clipped to the front of the contract folder, so the answer to what was agreed is always one page away.

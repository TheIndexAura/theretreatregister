Group Glamping: The Questions to Ask Before You Book for Twelve

September 14, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A planner stares at a glamping website built for two people and tries to imagine twenty. The photos show a fire pit and a king bed. They also show a string of lights. The description promises romance and stargazing. Nowhere does it say how many tents sit on the property or whether the bathhouse has six stalls or one.

The stakes are simple. A group glamping booking that skips the venue questions becomes a scramble when the forecast turns wet or the dinner plan collapses. You need to treat the site like a venue, not a room, and you need the answers before you sign.

How to count units and measure distance between them

Start with the unit count. Ask the host for the exact number of sleeping structures on the property and the maximum occupancy for each one. Write the request in an email so the answer is documented. A host who says ten tents but lists eight on the calendar has already given you a discrepancy to resolve.

Ask for the walking distance between the farthest units. Measure it in minutes on foot, not in yards. A cluster that takes five minutes to cross works for a morning meeting. A spread that takes fifteen minutes fractures the group before coffee. If the host cannot give you a time, ask for a map with the units marked and measure it yourself.

Check whether the units share walls or sit on separate pads. Shared walls mean noise travels. Separate pads mean guests carry gear farther. Both facts change how you assign rooms. Write the layout into your planning notes so the assignment sheet matches the ground.

How to find indoor space for the whole group at once

Every retreat needs a room that holds everyone at the same time. Ask the host for the dimensions of the largest covered space on site. A dining tent that seats twelve does not work for twenty people doing a workshop. A barn that seats thirty gives you margin for chairs and a projector.

Ask whether that space has heat and power outlets. Also ask whether the floor stays dry when the ground is wet. A canvas pavilion with a dirt floor fails all three. A converted garage with a concrete slab and a circuit panel passes. Record the specifications in your venue comparison sheet.

Confirm access hours. Some hosts lock the common building at ten in the evening. Others allow twenty-four hour use. If your schedule runs late, the lock time becomes a hard stop. Get the policy in writing before you commit.

How to clarify meal arrangements

Food is the second essential that decides whether a retreat venue works for a group. Ask the host whether meals are provided. Also ask whether a kitchen is available for self‑catering or whether you must bring a caterer. Each path carries different logistics and costs.

If the host provides meals, request a sample menu and the maximum headcount the kitchen can serve at one sitting. A kitchen built for eight guests cannot plate dinner for twenty without a second shift. If you bring a caterer, ask for the kitchen equipment list and the delivery access route. A narrow gravel drive blocks a refrigerated truck.

Ask about dietary restriction protocols. Some hosts handle allergies in house. Others require the caterer to manage them. Know which system applies so you can brief your attendees accurately. Document the agreement in your email thread.

How to get a rain plan in writing

Rain turns a group glamping weekend into a test of infrastructure. Ask the host for the specific plan when storms arrive. A vague assurance that guests can move indoors is not a plan. You need to know which building serves as the backup and how many people it holds. Also determine whether the move requires carrying bedding across mud.

Request photos of the indoor backup space taken during wet weather. A sunny photo of a barn tells you nothing about drainage. A photo showing dry floor boards after a downpour tells you the space works. Save those photos in your venue folder.

Ask whether the host provides extra tarps and floor coverings. Also ask whether portable heaters are available for the common area. If the answer is no, add those items to your packing list and budget. A written rain plan that includes equipment responsibilities prevents arguments when the sky opens.

Edge cases that change the calculation

The rule is to verify every capacity number against the physical space. The reason is that a legal pad taped to the refrigerator holds more truth than a website badge. A site listed for twelve may have two bathrooms and a single shower. That ratio fails a group of ten.

The rule is to confirm pricing in writing before you collect deposits. The reason is that pricing in this industry is quote-only by default and the first number you see is rarely the final one. A host who quotes per tent then adds a cleaning fee per person changes the budget by hundreds of dollars.

The rule is to require a direct contact number and a dedicated meeting or lodging area in the contract. The reason is that a direct phone number and a dedicated meeting or lodging space are the two essentials that most often decide whether a retreat venue works for a group. Without them you are guessing.

Ask the host whether each sleeping structure includes a lockable storage box for personal gear. A missing box forces guests to leave bags in a common area where they can be misplaced. Write the answer into the same email thread so the detail sits beside the unit count. One sentence.

Confirm that the largest covered space has adjustable lighting and a sound system that reaches the far corner. A dim room with a single bulb makes a workshop hard to follow. Note the wattage and speaker placement in the venue comparison sheet. One sentence.

Ask how waste and recycling are handled after each meal. A site that leaves trash bags beside the fire pit creates a cleanup scramble on the last morning. Record the pickup schedule and any extra fees in the email thread. One sentence.

Verify that the host provides a reliable way to reach staff after dark, such as a two‑way radio or a designated phone line. When a storm cuts power, a silent office leaves the group without direction. Add the contact method to the rain plan document. One sentence.

The rule is to treat the legal pad taped to the refrigerator as the final authority on capacity. The reason is that a handwritten note survives a website redesign and a host memory lapse. When the pad says twelve and the site says twenty, you follow the pad and adjust the headcount. Trust the paper.

Your task today is to send one email to each glamping host on your shortlist. Ask for unit count and walking times, the largest indoor space with specs, the meal structure and kitchen access, and the written rain plan with photos. Paste every reply into a single document so you can compare side by side.

When the document is complete you know which sites can actually host your group and which ones only look good in pictures. That knowledge lets you book with confidence and sleep through the night before the retreat starts.

Behind the Register

Why venue photos lie

July 14, 2026 · The Retreat Register

You are planning a retreat. You scroll through photographs of a venue, picturing your team there. It looks perfect, right down to the sun-drenched meeting room and the sparkling pool. Then you arrive, and the reality is different.

This is not just bad luck. It is a common headache for planners, and it comes from recycled, outdated or borrowed venue photographs. What you see online is not always what you get in person, and that is a problem for everyone involved.

Four ways a venue photograph stops being true

The photograph is old. A resort renovated its function room in 2019 and never reshot it. Nothing dishonest happened. The picture simply stopped describing the building.

The photograph is of the good half. Every property has a hero angle. A lodge with one beautiful lakeside deck and eleven ordinary bedrooms will show you the deck eleven times.

The photograph was taken in the right season, once. The lawn in the picture is the lawn in June. Your offsite is in February, and the outdoor breakout space you budgeted for is under snow.

The photograph belongs to somebody else. This is the one that matters, and it is usually not the venue’s doing. Listing platforms pull a general image because a general image is cheap, and a prettier picture converts better than a truthful one. It saves the platform effort and it costs you trust.

What The Retreat Register does about it, stated plainly

The register’s published rule is one sentence long. A listing never shows a different property. Until an owner claims their listing and uploads their own photos, we match the venue with a close stand-in image.1

That second sentence is the honest half, so read it slowly.

A stand-in image is not a photograph of that venue. It is a representative image of the same kind of property, and it is there so that a listing is not a blank grey rectangle while the register waits for an owner to claim the page and upload their own. What it is never doing is showing you a photograph of one hotel while the listing describes another.

That distinction is the whole policy. A picture that stands in for a property is a placeholder. A picture of a different property presented as this one is a lie. The register runs the first and rejects the second.

Here is the part a directory would normally leave out. The register has never published a count of how many listings carry an owner’s own photographs and how many carry a stand-in. That figure has not been measured, so no proportion is claimed on this page, in either direction. What is described above is a policy, not a measurement, and it is being labelled as one.

Why the front door is the least reliable page on a venue’s website

The register learned this the hard way, and it cost it six hundred venues.

Its early checks read a venue’s homepage and asked whether the place hosts groups. That looked reasonable until somebody looked at how upscale properties actually build websites. The homepage sells the dream, meaning the lake at dusk, the spa, the restaurant. The group business lives on an inner page at /meetings or /groups or /events that the front door barely mentions.2

When the register re-judged its entire rejection pile against a rebuilt check that reads those inner pages, 606 properties that had been thrown out were restored, and 2,193 stayed out because there was no group evidence anywhere on their site.2

For your purposes, that finding transfers directly to photographs. The homepage gallery is the marketing surface. It is built to make you feel something. The meetings page is where a venue puts the floor plan, the room dimensions and the unglamorous photograph of forty chairs in rows, because that page is written for someone who has to book, not someone who has to dream.

If you only look at one set of pictures on a venue’s website, look at the ones on the meetings page.

Where the register stood on this date

As of July 14, 2026, The Retreat Register held 2,200 curated retreat venues, and 118 venues had passed checks since the previous update.3 That reconciles exactly against the 2,082 published the day before.4

On the same date, 1,993 of those 2,200 venues listed a direct phone number.3 That is 90.6%, which the page rounds to 91%. Only 185 published a starting price, which is 8.4%.

Those two numbers are the reason the photograph problem matters so much. In an industry where fewer than one venue in ten will print a price, the picture is doing an enormous amount of the selling. It is carrying weight that a price, a floor plan and a capacity table would normally share.

The one place this has already become a legal question

Photographs and reviews are the same trust surface wearing different clothes, and one of the two is now regulated in the United States.

The Federal Trade Commission’s final rule on consumer reviews and testimonials “addresses reviews and testimonials that misrepresent that they are by someone who does not exist, such as AI-generated fake reviews,” and it took effect on October 21, 2024.5 Fabricating praise for your own business became a federal violation on that date.

The register has never run on reviews. It is worth saying why that is relevant here rather than a boast: the same pressure that produced fake reviews produces borrowed photographs, and the picture has not yet had its October 21 moment.

What a planner can actually do about it

Run a reverse image search on the photographs that look too good. Save the image, drop it into an image search, and see where else it appears on the internet. If the same lobby turns up on four properties in three states, you have your answer in about ninety seconds.

Cross-reference against the venue’s own website, not the listing. A directory listing is a copy. The venue’s own site is the source. If the angles, the furniture or the carpet do not match, ask why.

Ask for a photograph of the specific room in the specific layout you need. Not the ballroom. The room where thirty of your people will sit in a U-shape on a Tuesday morning. A venue that has hosted groups like yours can send that in an afternoon. A venue that cannot has just told you something useful.

Ask when the photographs were taken, and ask for a walkthrough video. A phone video shot this month settles every question a gallery raises, and a venue with nothing to hide will usually shrug and send one.

Call and ask. On July 14, 2026, roughly nine in ten venues in the register published a direct phone number, so this is usually available to you.3 Two minutes on the phone will tell you more about a property than an hour of scrolling.

Or start your search in a register where a listing never shows a different property, and spend the two minutes on something else.

The honest limits of this page

The photograph rule described here is a stated policy. There is no published count behind it, and this page does not imply one.

Every register figure above is dated July 14, 2026, and the last exact total the register published was 2,152 curated venues on July 19, 2026. Nothing on the site re-checks a venue’s photographs on a schedule, and no such claim is being made.

The Federal Trade Commission rule covers reviews and testimonials. It is cited here for what it actually says about fake reviews and their effective date, and not stretched into a claim about photographs, which it does not cover.

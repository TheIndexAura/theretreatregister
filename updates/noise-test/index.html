Test a retreat venue for noise in one silent minute

August 12, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Photos are silent. They show the calm lawn and the tidy meeting room, and they leave out the drone of distant traffic and the chimes from the event barn next door. Nothing in the gallery warns you that a group trying to think will hear all of it.

For a lot of retreats, quiet is the thing you are really buying. Check it the way you check the beds and the projector.

On a site visit, step outside the main gathering space and stand still for one full minute. Say nothing. Just listen. You may catch the hum of a road you could not see from the lobby, or the rhythm of daily work somewhere nearby.

Then send questions in writing, tied to your dates. Is a wedding booked next door that weekend? When do the crews mow? Does hunting season or a local event reach the property while you are there? A written answer beats a casual promise that it is usually quiet.

Put the silent minute on your site visit checklist for every venue you tour. When you get home, email each venue about your group's need for quiet.

Why quiet is worth testing

Quiet is a real draw. The National Park Service says surveys show the American public comes to parks for natural quiet and the sounds of nature, and it points to research linking those sounds with lowered stress and improved mood, in its Why Sounds Matter page. A group that came to think may notice noise that a casual guest would miss.

What the guidance says about sleep

Sleep is one place where noise can cost a retreat. The World Health Organization's Europe office recommends less than 30 A-weighted decibels in bedrooms at night for good sleep, and less than 40 outside bedrooms, in its noise fact sheet. That page dates from 2011 and covers community noise. Treat it as a yardstick for your questions. You cannot measure decibels by ear, but you can ask what sits near the sleeping rooms, such as a road or a kitchen vent.

Machines on the grounds

The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders lists lawnmowers and leaf blowers among common sources of loud noise around a home, in its page on noise-induced hearing loss. Those are the machines behind a day of grounds work. Your checklist already asks about mowing. Add leaf blowers, then ask for the crew's usual hours in writing and compare them with your session times.

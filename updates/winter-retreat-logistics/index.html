Winter Retreats: Settle the Snow Questions Before You Book

September 16, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A planner sits at a kitchen table in October scrolling through a gallery of a lodge in the mountains. The photos show a lawn rolling to a lake, a deck wrapped in string lights, a dining hall with beams the color of honey. The calendar shows three open weeks in January. The price fits the budget. The deposit button waits.

What the gallery does not show is the access road after a foot of snow, the outbuilding where the heat fails at midnight, the contract clause that keeps your money when the highway closes. Winter retreat planning turns on those missing details. The stakes are simple: a group arrives safe and warm, or they do not.

How the Summer Gallery Misleads

Summer photos are taken when the light is long and the ground is dry. The photographer stands on a paved loop that disappears under ice in December. The deck chairs are staged on grass that becomes a sheet of slick mud. The dining hall glows with late afternoon sun that sets at four in the afternoon come January. You see the property at its most generous. You book it at its most stingy.

A planner who books on summer images alone will find the parking lot unplowed on arrival day. The van slides into a ditch. The luggage stays in the back while the group walks the last quarter mile in boots that were not packed for snow. The first meeting starts late and cold. The mood settles into frustration before the agenda begins.

The fix is to treat every summer gallery as a partial truth. You ask for photos taken in January or February. You ask for video of the access road after a storm. You ask for the name of the plow contractor and the response time in writing. A venue that cannot provide those has not earned a winter booking.

Run the Winter Screen

The winter screen is a short list of written questions you send before you sign. The goal is to learn whether the property functions when the temperature drops and the snow piles up. You send the questions by email so the answers are documented. You keep the thread for reference when the contract arrives.

Start with the access road. Ask who plows it and how fast after a storm ends. Ask whether a standard passenger vehicle can reach the parking lot in six inches of snow. Ask whether four wheel drive is required and whether chains are ever mandated. The answers tell you what vehicles to rent and whether attendees need to coordinate rides.

Move to the buildings. Ask which structures have central heat and which rely on space heaters or wood stoves. Ask for the lowest overnight temperature recorded inside each sleeping space last winter. Ask whether the water lines are insulated and whether the septic system handles frozen ground. An outbuilding that looks charming in July can become a liability in January.

End with the contract. Ask for the force majeure clause in plain language. Ask what happens to the deposit if the county closes the highway. Ask whether the venue refunds or credits when the group cannot arrive. Ask who decides the event is cancelled. The venue decides unless the contract says otherwise.

Check the Access Road and Plowing

The access road is the first filter. A paved road maintained by the county is the best case. A gravel road maintained by the venue is common. A dirt road maintained by no one is a risk you calculate. You need the plow contractor's name and phone number. You need the service level agreement that states the maximum hours between snowfall and cleared pavement.

Ask whether the plow clears to the parking lot or stops at the main gate. Ask whether the venue staff shovels the walkways to the buildings. Ask whether ice melt is applied and who supplies it. A venue that expects the group to shovel is a venue that shifts labor onto guests. That labor is not in the budget.

If the road requires four wheel drive, you note that in the arrival instructions. You rent vehicles that meet the requirement. You tell attendees who drive their own cars to check their tires. You build an extra hour into the travel window for slow conditions. The road does not care about your schedule.

Verify Heat in Every Building

Heat is the second filter. The main lodge may have a boiler that runs on propane delivered by a truck that cannot climb an icy hill. The cabins may have electric baseboards that trip the breaker when three units run at once. The meeting yurt may have a wood stove that requires a cord of wood stacked under a tarp that blew away in November.

You ask for the heating source in each building used by the group. You ask for the fuel delivery schedule and whether the venue has a backup supplier. You ask whether the electrical panel supports the load of every heater running at once. You ask for the temperature log from the coldest night last year. A venue that does not keep logs is guessing.

If an outbuilding is unheated, you decide whether to use it. You may move the breakout sessions to the heated dining hall. You may rent a portable heater rated for the square footage and the electrical capacity. You may tell attendees to bring layers. The decision is yours, but the data comes from the venue.

Read the Contract for Weather

The contract is the third filter. Most venues use a standard agreement that protects the venue. The force majeure clause often lists acts of god but does not define them. A snowstorm may not qualify if the venue remains open. The venue keeps the deposit. The group loses the money and the retreat.

You read the clause and you mark the gaps. You ask for an addendum that defines weather cancellation as the closure of the access road by the county or the inability of the plow contractor to clear the road within twelve hours of snowfall ending. You ask for a refund or a credit valid for twelve months. You ask for the decision to rest with the planner based on the road report, not the venue based on occupancy.

Pricing in this industry is quote only by default. The quote does not include the addendum. You negotiate the addendum before you sign. You confirm the changes in writing. You save the signed version. The contract is the only document that matters when the storm hits.

Today you open a new email thread to every venue on your winter shortlist. You paste the winter screen questions. You send them. You wait for written answers. You compare the answers side by side. You eliminate the venues that dodge or guess.

The planner who skips this screen arrives at a locked gate with a van full of people and a deposit the venue will not return. The planner who runs the screen arrives at a cleared lot with a heated building and a contract that protects the budget. The difference is the work you do in October.

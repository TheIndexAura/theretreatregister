The capacity number that is missing

July 19, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Let us talk about capacity. It is a word that sounds simple, and in the world of corporate retreats it is the slipperiest eel in the venue-finding ocean. Planners know the drill. You ask for capacity, you get a number, and the number tells you almost nothing.

The truth is that capacity is not a single fixed figure. It shifts with the layout, the furniture, the exits and the fire code. That ambiguity is not just annoying. It can derail your planning before you have started.

One room, four answers

Think about what you are actually asking when you ask how many people a room holds.

Theater style puts out rows of chairs facing a stage, with no tables. It is the densest layout, and it is the number a venue quotes when it wants the headline figure to be large. It is also useless for a working offsite, because nobody can write anything down.

Classroom style adds a narrow table in front of each row of chairs. Everyone can take notes and open a laptop. The same room typically holds roughly half the people it held in theater style.

Banquet rounds are circular tables of eight or ten. This is the dinner layout, and it is the one people picture when they imagine the evening. Round tables waste floor space, so the number falls again.

U-shape and boardroom put everyone around one continuous table so that every person can see every other person. It is the right layout for the twenty-person leadership session that is the actual reason for the whole trip, and it is the least dense arrangement in the building. A room that seats two hundred in theater style might seat thirty in a U.

That is one room and four different true answers. A venue that publishes a single capacity number has picked whichever one flatters it.

Then there is the other capacity

The second question is completely separate from the first, and it is the one that ends more retreat plans than any other.

How many people can sleep on site?

A conference lodge with a two-hundred-seat function room and forty-two bedrooms cannot host your eighty-person offsite as a residential retreat. It can host it as a day event with everyone driving in, which is a different trip with a different budget and a different outcome. The meeting-room number and the bed number are not related, and one of them is usually much smaller.

Ask for both, separately, in writing.

While you are at it, ask a third thing: can the venue give you the meeting room and the bedrooms at the same time, on your dates, without another group in the building. That is the buyout question, and it is where a venue’s real capacity for your retreat turns out to live.

Where the register stands on this

As of July 19, 2026, The Retreat Register holds 2,152 curated retreat venues, and this capacity conundrum is a common thread among them.1 Twenty-seven new venues cleared checks since the last update, which reconciles exactly against the 2,125 published the day before.2

You will also notice that only 184 of those venues publish a starting price,1 which tells you something about how much information in this industry is left to interpretation. That is 8.6% of the register. Fewer than one venue in ten will name a number before it names your group size, and one of the main reasons is that your group size is the number that changes everything.

Even in the deepest coverage areas, California with 162 venues, New York with 98 and Texas with 83 on that date,1 the ambiguity is the same. It is not a small-market problem or a big-market problem. It is an industry-wide habit.

What the register does not publish, and why

Here is the part most directories would leave out.

The Retreat Register has never published a capacity figure for its venues. There is no count of how many hold fifty people, no average, no median, and no breakdown by layout. The register’s published quality bar asks whether a property has “real retreat lodging or dedicated meeting space, not just rooms, but somewhere a group can actually work and stay.”3 That is a yes-or-no question, not a measurement.

The register screens for essentials before listing a venue, focusing first on a direct phone number and dedicated meeting or lodging space. Screening for the existence of dedicated space is a different job from counting the seats in it, and the second one has not been done.

Saying so is the point of this page. A directory that quietly generated a capacity number for every listing would be publishing exactly the confident single figure this article is warning you about.

What the missing number costs you in money

Capacity is not only a logistics question. It is the number your whole budget hangs off, which is why the vagueness is expensive.

There is one genuinely public yardstick in the United States, and almost nobody in this industry uses it. The federal government publishes what it will reimburse for a night of lodging and a day of meals. For the 2026 fiscal year, “the standard lodging rate also remains unchanged at $110,” and “the standard M&IE rate remains at $68.”4

Hold a venue quote against that. Two nights and two days of meals for one person at the federal standard is $356. For a group of forty, that is $14,240 before you have booked a meeting room, hired a projector or fed anybody a coffee break.

Now read the sentence in the same federal bulletin that nobody quotes: “If per diem reimbursement rates are insufficient to meet expenses, the FTR permits actual expense reimbursement up to 300% of the applicable maximum per diem rate.”4

The federal government, setting its own ceiling, wrote into the regulation that its ceiling is often not enough, and allowed three times as much. That is the plainest available evidence that lodging and meeting cost varies too much by place, date and group size to be published as one number. It is also why a venue will not quote you before it knows how many people are coming and which layout they need.

The capacity number and the price number are the same problem wearing two hats.

The fix, and it is not complicated

Send this list, in writing, before you take a call.

  1. What is the capacity of the main meeting room in theater, classroom, banquet and U-shape layouts? Four numbers, please.
  2. How many bedrooms, and how many beds, are on site? Those are different, and a room with two queens is not the same offer as a room with one king.
  3. Can we hold the meeting space and the bedrooms on the same dates without another group in the building, and what group size triggers a full buyout?
  4. What are the breakout rooms, how many, and what do they seat?
  5. Which of those numbers changes if the weather moves an outdoor session inside?

Treat any single all-encompassing capacity claim with a healthy dose of suspicion. A venue that answers all five in one email is a venue that has hosted groups like yours before, and that is worth more than the numbers themselves.

For venue owners, the same list is a checklist. Publishing those five answers on your own website is the cheapest advantage available to you, because almost nobody else does it. It is clarity, not just a number.

The honest limits of this page

Every figure above carries the date it was measured, and July 19, 2026 is the last date the register published an exact total. Later pages give a rounded figure of more than 2,000 venues, and pages published from July 22, 2026 onward carry no count at all. There is no newer number here to give you, and this page will not imply one.

The federal per diem figures are reimbursement ceilings for federal travellers. They are not market rates and they are not group rates. They are a public yardstick to hold a quote against, and that is all this page uses them for.

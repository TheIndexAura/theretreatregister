Plan a Board Retreat: Set the Objectives, Then Pick the Room

October 26, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Write the board's goals down before you book the room. Set the objectives with the chair and the chief executive, turn each into a session, and only then match sessions to spaces.

Write the objectives before you look at rooms

Picture the planner a week out. The agenda is half drafted, the chair has sent three different ideas about what the day is for, and the venue field in the spreadsheet still says to be decided. Write down first what the board should leave the room having done. Start there, then ask of each objective how long its session should run, who should lead it and what kind of room it calls for.

Ask the board chair and the chief executive, separately at first, to finish this sentence: by the end of the retreat, what should the board have done? Collect the answers and write each one as a short verb and noun, such as approve the strategy, review the budget or agree on recruitment goals. Keep the list short. Decide how many real decisions one day can hold, and ask the chair whether some items belong at a regular meeting instead.

Then put the list in front of both people together and settle it. Keep the final wording in one shared document with the year in the file name, and send a short message to the chair, the chief executive and the board secretary that repeats the list word for word. Decide that any later change goes out in writing to the same three people, so that everyone can see what was agreed.

Who may change the list, and by when? Decide whether new objectives are accepted the week before the retreat, and what comes off the agenda if they are. Pick a date after which additions wait for the next regular meeting, and say so in the message that carries the list.

Decide what kind of retreat this is

Decide what this retreat is for, since the purpose shapes the room. Purdue Extension describes a board program that covers the phases of the Board Development Cycle, focusing on board member recruitment, engagement, and renewal.1 The same page says Purdue Extension can sometimes assist with facilitating visioning, goal-setting, and strategic planning.1 A planner who is unsure what to put on the list can read those descriptions as topics to consider, and a board does not have to adopt any of them.

The page calls its workshop both a three-hour, in-person workshop and a daylong workshop. It does not mention retreats at all. That gap is worth noting. A planner should not treat that page as a template for a retreat, and should treat the topics only as ideas to raise with the chair.

Sort the objectives into two kinds. Some need a decision, such as a vote. Others need discussion, such as a conversation about where the organization should be in a few years. Ask what a decision session needs, such as a table, papers and a way to record the vote, and what a discussion session needs, such as space to talk, movable chairs and something to write on. Mark each objective with its kind now, before you start comparing rooms.

Think about who else should be present. A retreat for the board alone is planned differently from one that includes staff or invited guests. Ask the chair who must be there for each objective, and write the answer next to it. Someone needed for one session only can be scheduled for that block rather than for the whole day.

Turn each objective into a session

For every objective, ask what the board needs in hand to act. A vote to approve a strategy needs the draft strategy sent ahead of time. A review of the budget needs the figures in a form the board can read quickly. Write the needed material next to the session, and set a date for sending it. Decide whether people should read before they arrive or during the session.

Name a facilitator for each block. It can be a board member, the chief executive or an outside person, but whoever it is needs a briefing on the decision, the time available and what a finished session looks like. Put the facilitator's name next to the session title on the draft agenda, so the plan shows who keeps the time.

Give the biggest objectives the longest blocks and leave a margin before and after them for questions. Short items can run back to back, and the smallest can share a closing block. Build in breaks and a meal, and decide whether any block should happen with the staff in the room. Put the draft in a table with the session title, facilitator, decision or purpose, and time, and send it to the chair and the chief executive for comments.

Plan how decisions get written down. The New York Attorney General says a charitable corporation must keep correct and complete minutes of all meetings of its members, board, and executive committee.2 That is a New York rule for charitable corporations. The page says nothing about other states, so a planner elsewhere should ask a lawyer which rules apply to the organization. Whatever the rule, the practical step is the same: decide who takes the notes, and make sure that person sits where they can hear every session.

If the retreat agenda includes a vote on officer pay, read what the same page says. For charitable corporations in New York, it says the board must approve the compensation of officers by the affirmative vote of a majority of the entire board, and that bylaws may instead allow a committee to approve it.2 If anyone joins by video, ask the board secretary how their votes and comments are recorded. Ask the board secretary whether a vote of that kind belongs on a retreat agenda at all, or whether it is better left to a regular meeting where the full board is sure to be present.

Match the sessions to a space

Now turn each session into a room requirement. A decision session wants a long table or a horseshoe of tables, good light and a screen if the board looks at figures together. A discussion session wants chairs that move and a wall to write on. Someone joining remotely adds a video link and a dependable way to hear the room. Jot these needs beside each agenda item. Your search then starts from the plan, not from a list of available locations.

Send every candidate venue the objective list and the draft agenda. Then ask the board-specific questions. Can the board have a private room for the whole day, with no other group walking through? One person should answer questions before and during the event, so ask who that is. Ask whether the room can be set up in more than one layout and how long a change takes. Food matters too: can it be served in the room or nearby, and who clears it?

Get a written quote and keep it. Hold each quote against the budget and read what it lists and what it leaves out. Room, equipment, setup and food may be priced together or apart, so ask. Put every reply in one folder. When someone on the board asks why this venue won, you can show them.

Size the room from the venue's own figures. Ask for the count for each layout you want, rows and tables with space for papers included, and set each against your headcount. Then leave space to move. A facilitator needs to walk between tables.

Check the small things too. Ask how many restrooms serve the meeting space and whether the room has windows and its own heating and cooling controls. Find out where people can step away for a private word. Ask where guests park and how accessible the building and the room are. Before you sign anything, send someone to walk the route from the entrance to the room.

Close the loop and prepare for changes

Pick the venue and write down why. A short summary is enough: the room, the layout for each session, the equipment, the quote and the agreed contact. Attach it to the objective sheet and send both to the chair and the chief executive for sign-off. Does the board have a bylaw or policy on spending or contracts? Then ask the secretary who may sign the venue agreement.

Keep a ranked shortlist in case the first choice falls through. A venue might cancel after the agenda is set. Go to the next one on the list, ask the same questions in the same order, and check whether the agenda still fits its rooms. Print the shortlist so the planner and the chair can both find it.

Before you sign, ask a lawyer whether the venue contract needs a clause about privacy. Decide with the chair which matters the board wants kept inside the room. A planner should not guess here. Put the same worry to the venue: who can enter the room during the day, and what happens to flip charts and papers left behind?

Send the final agenda, the room plan and the objective sheet to everyone invited. Add a plain note on what the board is meant to decide and which material to read first. Decide who sends word of a late change, and keep a list of how to reach each participant.

After the retreat, send the chair and the chief executive a summary of decisions, with a name and a date beside each action. Put the first check of those actions on the agenda of the next regular board meeting. File the original objective sheet with the summary. Laid side by side, the two show what the day delivered.

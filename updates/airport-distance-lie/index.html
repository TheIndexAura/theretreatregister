The airport distance lie

August 6, 2026 · The Retreat Register

The itinerary said ninety minutes. It is hour three, the van smells like airport sandwiches, and the first session was supposed to start twenty minutes ago.

Nobody lied, exactly. Somebody measured that drive once, alone, at six in the morning on a Tuesday, in a sedan with no luggage and no reason to stop. Then that number went on a web page and stayed there for four years.

Here is how to get the real one before it wrecks your first day.

What the published number leaves out

“Ninety minutes from the airport” is a measurement of one thing: how long a car takes to travel between two points under ideal conditions. Your group is not a car, and your day is not ideal conditions.

The parts that never appear in that number:

  • Wheels down to the curb. Taxiing, deplaning a full aircraft, the walk from a regional gate, and baggage claim. For a group with checked bags, thirty-five to fifty minutes is ordinary, and it is longer at large hubs where the rental and rideshare pickup sits in a separate structure.
  • Assembling people. Fourteen adults arriving on three different flights do not become one group in five minutes. Somebody is in the restroom, somebody’s bag came out last, somebody went to find coffee.
  • Loading. Luggage into a van takes ten minutes, and a second van doubles the coordination rather than halving the time.
  • The stop. There is always one stop. Bathroom, snacks, the person who needs to make a call before the signal disappears. Budget for it, because pretending it will not happen does not prevent it.
  • The last useful moment. Arriving at the property is not the same as being ready to work. Check-in, room keys, dropping bags, and finding the meeting room is another twenty to thirty minutes for a group.

The published drive time is a component of your travel day. It is not the travel day.

The map test, done properly

Two minutes, free, and it settles most of this.

  1. Open your maps app and enter the airport as the start and the venue’s street address as the destination. Use the address, not the venue name, because the pin on a large property often sits on the entrance gate rather than on the building where you are sleeping.
  2. Set the departure or arrival time to the actual day and the actual hour your group lands. A Friday at 4pm and a Tuesday at 6am are different roads.
  3. Read the range, not the headline. Most apps will show something like “1 hr 40 min, typically 1 hr 25 to 2 hr 15”. The top of that range is your planning number.
  4. Zoom into the last ten miles and look at the road. Two lanes with no shoulder through a canyon behaves nothing like an interstate, and it is the part that a winter forecast or a fallen tree closes first.
  5. Check the road in the season you are traveling. Mountain passes and unpaved access roads have closures that a summer map view does not show you.

Then apply the multiplier. For a loaded van that stops once, add a third to the drive time and add an hour on the front for the airport itself. If the app says ninety minutes, plan the day as though wheels down to first session is three and a half hours. That is not pessimism, it is arithmetic on the list above.

Two questions to ask the venue

The people at the property have watched a thousand groups arrive. They know the answer and they are rarely asked.

“What time does a group flying into [AIRPORT] on a [DAY] usually get to you?” Note that this asks about groups, not about drivers, and it asks for what usually happens rather than what is possible.

“Which airport do most of your groups actually use, and why?” A venue will often name a smaller regional field two hours closer than the international hub your booking tool is pushing. Cheaper flights into a distant airport can cost more once you price ground transport and lose an afternoon.

Calling is the fast way to get both. On July 18, 2026 the register held 2,125 curated retreat venues, and 1,928 of them, or 91%, listed a direct phone number.1 These are two questions that take one call.

The ground transport questions nobody asks until it is too late

  • Does the venue run a shuttle, and what does it cost per person or per run?
  • Is there a minimum number of passengers, and a latest pickup time?
  • What happens to somebody who lands after that cutoff?
  • Is there rideshare coverage at the airport late in the evening, and at the venue for the return?
  • Does the road have mobile signal the whole way, or is there a stretch where a delayed driver cannot call ahead?

That last one catches people. A rural property with an excellent shuttle and a fifteen-mile dead zone means your late arrival has no way to tell anyone they are late.

Build the day around the real number

Once you have an honest arrival time, the fix is a schedule change rather than a venue change.

  • Do not put content in the first block. A session that starts within an hour of arrival is a session half the room misses.
  • Let the drive be the decompression. A two-hour van ride with the right seating is a decent start to an offsite. A two-hour van ride that is making everybody late is a bad one. The road is the same road; the difference is what the agenda promised.
  • Open with food. Dinner is the most forgiving opening event ever invented. It absorbs stragglers, it does not need a projector, and nobody has to have arrived on time for it to work.
  • Put the first real session after breakfast on day two, and stop treating travel day as a working day. You will get more out of two clean days than out of two and a half messy ones.
  • Plan the departure with the same honesty. Work backwards from the last flight, add the same multiplier, and accept that the final morning session is shorter than you want it to be.

About the flights themselves

Booking a group into a single arrival window is harder than booking one person, and it is worth knowing the size of the system you are working inside. The Federal Aviation Administration states that “Every day, FAA’s Air Traffic Organization (ATO) provides service to more than 44,000 flights and more than 3 million airline passengers across more than 29 million square miles of airspace”, based on its 2024 fiscal year figures.2

That figure proves scale and nothing else. It says nothing about how often flights are late, and this page does not claim that it does. Grouping arrivals into a two-hour window and holding the shuttle for that window is a scheduling decision, not a prediction about delays.

What the register does here

The Retreat Register screens for essentials before listing a venue, focusing first on a direct phone number and dedicated meeting or lodging space.

Drive times are not part of that. The register does not measure or publish the distance from any airport to any property, and a listing will not tell you what a loaded van does on a Friday afternoon. That measurement is yours, it takes two minutes, and it is the cheapest hour you will ever save.

Never build a schedule on a distance you did not measure yourself.

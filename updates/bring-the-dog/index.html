Plan for Dogs at a Retreat: Service Animal Rules and Pet Policy Questions

August 12, 2026 · The Retreat Register

For plenty of people a dog is family, and a trip that leaves it home is a trip they skip. Service dogs and pets are two separate questions under federal rules. Sort your group's needs into those two lists first, then ask each venue for its pet policy and its service animal practice in writing.

Why one dog can shrink a gathering

Someone you want at the retreat says no, and the reason is their dog. They have no sitter they trust and no kennel they like, so the dog comes or they stay home. That one detail can reduce a group before the invitations are even answered. A clear policy that welcomes dogs can turn that no into a yes, and a policy that forbids them is better learned now than on arrival.

Start by asking what each guest needs. One guest wants a pet along. Another relies on a service dog, which is a different matter in law and in practice. Keep the two lists apart from the first message, because the rules for each are different and the venue will answer each one differently.

Think about timing, too. Send the question to the venue before you open registration, so the answer can go into the invitation. A guest deciding whether to come wants to know the rule, the room and the limit before they commit. Find out whether the venue caps the number of dogs it accepts for one group, and whether any breeds, sizes or ages are excluded. With the answers in hand, you write fewer one-off emails later.

Service dogs and pets are two different questions

The Department of Justice defines a service animal under the Americans with Disabilities Act as a dog that has been individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability.1 Its requirements page puts the idea in a short line: service animals are working animals, not pets.2 The department's frequently asked questions say that emotional support animals do not qualify as service animals under the ADA, though some state or local laws may still allow them in public places.1

So you can sort your group. A guest with a trained service dog is covered by federal rules about access. A guest with an emotional support dog or a pet is covered by the venue's own policy and by any local law that applies. The same dog can fall into the second group at one place and be welcome at another, so the answer changes by venue and by location.

The same page adds that a business with a no-pets policy generally must modify it to allow service animals into its facilities.1 A covered venue that bans pets may therefore still have to admit a service dog. These rules apply to businesses the ADA covers, and the room and fee points are written about hotels, so a private retreat property may or may not be covered. The venue can say how it applies them. If a guest who uses a service dog agrees, raise the question early, and leave the guest unnamed unless they want to be named.

What the rules say about rooms and charges

The guidance speaks to hotels directly. A hotel may not limit a guest with a service animal to pet-friendly rooms, and that guest must get the same chance to book any available room as other guests.1 Hotels are not permitted to charge a guest for cleaning the hair or dander shed by a service animal. If the animal damages a room, the hotel may charge the same damage fees it charges other guests.1

The handler stays responsible. The guidance says the handler is responsible for caring for and supervising the service animal, and that a business is not required to look after it.1 A service animal must be under the control of its handler at all times. A dog that is out of control, where the handler does not take effective action, may be removed from the premises, and a dog that is not housebroken may be excluded.1 The requirements page adds that service animals must be harnessed, leashed or tethered, unless the person's disability prevents it or the devices would interfere with the animal's safe, effective work. In that case the person must keep control through voice, signal or other effective means.2

Staff who are unsure whether a dog is a service animal may ask only two questions. They may ask whether the dog is a service animal required because of a disability, and what work or task it has been trained to perform.2 The FAQ page adds that a covered business may not require documentation, such as proof that the animal has been certified, as a condition for entry.1 Share a guest's details with a venue only with the guest's consent, and only what the venue needs to plan. The guest can also write to the venue directly. Do not ask for papers yourself.

Read the guidance itself for the exact wording. If a venue pushes back on any of this, a lawyer who handles access law is the right person to ask.

Write to each guest plainly. Say which list they are on, what the venue has said and what it expects of them as the person responsible for the animal. Guests who bring a pet should know that the venue's rules, its charges and its limits apply to them, and that they are answerable for the animal's behavior during the stay.

What to ask a venue about pets in writing

A pet policy is the venue's own rule, so the details vary from place to place. Find out which rooms accept dogs and whether those rooms can be held for your group. Find out what the pet charge covers, who pays for damage and how the venue handles two dogs in one room. It also helps to ask where dogs may go once inside the property, for example the dining room, the meeting room and the outdoor paths. Check whether dogs may be left alone in a room, and what the venue expects if a dog barks.

Look past the fee. A pet charge says little about how welcoming a venue is. Ground-floor rooms with direct access to the outdoors, or a fenced area where a dog can run, say more, because those details show that someone thought about a guest traveling with an animal. Look into walking routes, waste bags and where guests can exercise a dog without crossing a path used by others.

Other guests matter too. Some people are allergic to dogs or afraid of them, and a group that includes both needs a plan. See whether the venue has rooms that pets never enter. Service animal access is a separate question, so do not set a rule that would keep a service dog out of a room.

Keep the two lists apart in your planning

Write two short lists for your own planning. The first names guests who have told you they rely on a service dog. The second names guests who would like to bring a pet, with the size and number of animals. Give the venue the pet list and your general questions about service animals. Pass on a guest's service dog details only with their consent, or let the guest write to the venue.

A guest with a service dog should never be sent to a pet room by mistake. Ask the venue to confirm, in writing, that its staff know the difference and that your guest may book any available room. Keep the reply with your booking papers and share the relevant lines with the guest, so they know what has been arranged before they travel.

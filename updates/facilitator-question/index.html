Choose Your Own Facilitator or the Venue's Before You Book

September 9, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Decide who leads the sessions before you compare venues. The choice is between your own facilitator and the venue's, and each choice sends different questions to a site.

Name the model before you look at rooms

Compare three models for the working sessions: led by your own group, led by an outside facilitator, or a program the venue provides. Someone from the group can lead them, an outside professional can be brought in, or the venue can supply its own staff and program. Each choice changes what you ask a site, so it helps to settle it first and write the choice at the top of the planning sheet in plain words: group-led, outside facilitator, or venue-provided.

The choice also changes what the room has to do. A group-led day needs wall space, tables that move and a way to split into small clusters. With an outside facilitator, ask the site about a separate room for preparation, a place to store materials and who decides the layout. A venue-provided program comes with its own schedule and its own idea of the room, so compare that idea with the plan you made. If you do not know which approach you are choosing, every reply you collect is hard to compare. Settle it first.

Keep the label visible while you research. When a site answers with a package, check it against the label before you check it against anything else. Ask each site whether the package assumes its own staff lead the sessions or whether it simply rents the space, because those are different offers.

Know what a facilitator is for

Before you pick a person, be clear about the job. The Penn State Extension engagement toolbox says that facilitators serve a number of roles that help groups accomplish tasks and maintain relationships.1 Its list of roles that help groups accomplish tasks includes idea initiator, information seeker, information provider, problem clarifier and consensus tester. Its list of roles that help groups maintain relationships includes harmonizer, gatekeeper, supporter and standards monitor.1 Decide which of these jobs you want the person running the sessions to hold, and which ones the group can cover for itself.

The same page treats the agenda as a working tool. It says agendas are an important management tool that effective facilitators use to plan for and conduct productive meetings.1 It also says an agenda should include topics for discussion, a presenter or discussion leader for each topic, and a time allotment for each topic.1 Whoever leads your sessions, ask who writes the agenda and when the group sees it. If the venue's staff would lead, ask to see their version before you book.

The page also says ground rules are especially useful when the group is working on a contentious issue or if conflict is likely, and that ground rules should be shared with the group at the start of every meeting.1 It offers a number of statements as a starting point, including only one person speaks at a time and respect time boundaries.1 Ask any candidate how they set ground rules and whether the group can edit them. Listen to how specific the answer is.

Think about a neutral person

The same page says to consider inviting a neutral person to facilitate sensitive meetings.1 It does not say what counts as neutral, so the definition is yours to make. One test you could use is whether the person has a stake in the outcome. Ask yourself whether a manager who has to live with the decisions, or a team lead whose own work is on the agenda, could guide the conversation and also take a side in it.

Use that test against the topic. If the sessions deal with something touchy, such as a reorganization, a budget cut or a strained team, a person from outside the group may fit the test better. If the sessions are routine planning or a creative workshop, you may decide a group member is enough. For the venue's staff, ask whether they have any connection to either side of the topic, and ask to see how they run a session. Write down the reason for your choice so the group can see why you made it.

If your group does choose a leader from inside, consider separating the roles. Pick someone to run the process and let the people with the opinions speak as participants. Say this to the group in the welcome note so nobody is surprised when the person running the room declines to weigh in.

Check how an outside facilitator is engaged

Hiring someone from outside raises a question about the working relationship, and it is worth a few minutes of your time. The Internal Revenue Service says that facts providing evidence of the degree of control and independence fall into three categories: behavioral, financial, and type of relationship.2 For the behavioral category it asks whether the company controls or has the right to control what the worker does and how the worker does his or her job.2 For the financial category it asks whether the business aspects of the worker's job are controlled by the payer, and it says these include how the worker is paid, whether expenses are reimbursed and who provides tools and supplies.2

For the type of relationship category, the IRS asks whether there are written contracts or employee type benefits, and whether the relationship will continue and the work performed is a key aspect of the business.2 The page says that no one factor stands alone in making this determination, and that the keys are to look at the entire relationship and consider the extent of the right to direct and control the worker.2 The Small Business Administration says an independent contractor operates under a separate business name from your company, and that independent contractors can sometimes qualify as employees in a legal sense.3 It adds that if a contractor is discovered to meet the legal definition of employee, you may need to pay back taxes and penalties, provide benefits, and reimburse for wages stipulated under the Fair Labor Standards Act.3

Neither page mentions retreats or facilitators, so they cannot tell you how your own arrangement would be treated. Take your plan to a lawyer, and say who would pay the facilitator, who would set the methods and who would bring the materials. Decide what you are offering before you write the agreement, then put it on paper.

Send each model its own questions

For a group-led retreat, ask the venue about movable tables, wall space for flip charts and whether a large room can be split into smaller zones. Ask what the room is like when other guests are using the space next door, and whether sound carries through the walls.

For an outside facilitator, ask whether the venue lets a guest bring materials in and use the room early for setup. Ask for a quiet space for preparation, and ask who in the building controls the screen, the lights and the temperature. Tell the facilitator the room layout in writing. Nobody should guess on the first morning.

For a venue-provided program, ask who will lead, how that person is chosen and what they have run before. Ask to see a sample agenda with the time allotment for each topic, and ask whether the group can change it. Ask what happens if the group wants to swap a session or add one, and who pays for the change. Find out whether the program can be adjusted to your goals or whether it comes as a fixed set. Get that in writing.

In every case, put the answers in one grid so the sites sit side by side. Write the model label across the top, the rooms down the side and the facilitator arrangement in each box. Then send a short confirmation to the site you pick, repeating the label and the space details. Keep that note with your booking papers.

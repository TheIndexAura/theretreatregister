Faith-based retreat centers: a core retreat use, judged on the same bar

July 18, 2026 · The Retreat Register

The register holds 2,125 curated retreat venues as of July 18, 2026. Faith-based retreat centres tend to get one of two treatments on a venue list. They are left off entirely, or they are dumped in with no check at all, and a planner cannot tell which list they are reading.

Both treatments miss the same thing. A congregation retreat is one of the oldest and most common uses of a retreat property in the United States, and it has requirements that a corporate offsite does not.

The size of the audience, stated carefully

Pew Research Center’s 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study found that “37% of U.S. adults say they, personally, are members of a church, synagogue, mosque or other house of worship, including 54% of Christians”.1

The same study reports that 40% of U.S. adults take part in religious services at least once a month, and it is worth reading how that number is built before using it. Pew counts online and television participation in the 40%, and breaks it out: 16% take part both ways, 17% in person only, and 8% online or on television only.1 A retreat is a thing people physically travel to, so membership at 37% is the sounder figure for this purpose, and “40% of Americans are in a building every month” is not what the study says.

That is a large, measurable population, organised into groups that already meet weekly and already have someone whose job includes planning the annual weekend away.

What a congregation needs that a company does not

One room where everyone faces the same way. Corporate venues are built around round tables and breakout pods. A congregation usually needs a single space that seats the whole group in rows or a semicircle, with a front. Ask for the seated capacity in theatre style rather than banquet style, because the two numbers differ by a lot and the banquet number is the one on the brochure.

Sound, and who owns it. Ask whether there is a fixed sound system, how many microphone inputs it has, whether a music team can plug in, and whether there is a technician or a locked cupboard. Ask whether the room has a piano and whether it is tuned. These are unglamorous questions and they decide the weekend.

A sleeping mix, not a room type. A congregation group is usually multi-generational in one booking: youth in bunks, couples in doubles, older members who need a ground floor and a lift. Ask for the actual inventory by type, ask how many rooms are on the ground floor, and ask what the walk is between the sleeping buildings and the main room in the dark and in the rain.

Food, and whether your volunteers may touch it. Many congregations bring their own cooks, and many venues cannot allow it. This is often a health-department question rather than a preference, so ask it as a fact question: may our team cook in your kitchen, and if not, is there a servery or a hall where we can handle food we bring? Ask about allergies and about the vegetarian and alcohol-free arrangement in practice.

A written alcohol policy. Ask whether alcohol is served on site, whether it is available to other guests, and whether the venue can be dry for a private booking. A venue that says “we can accommodate that” is not the same as a venue that does not have a bar.

Whether outside groups are actually welcome. Some retreat centres serve their own denomination and take other bookings only in gaps. That is a legitimate way to run a property, and it is worth knowing before your youth pastor spends a week planning around it. Ask directly whether you would be a guest group or a member group, and what differs between the two.

Who else is on site. Ask whether the property will be exclusively yours, and if not, where the other group will be and what they are doing.

The accessibility question is genuinely different here, and almost nobody says so

This is the part of the page most worth reading twice.

The Americans with Disabilities Act sets accessibility requirements for places of public accommodation, and the Department of Justice’s regulation defines a place of lodging to include an inn, hotel or motel and facilities providing guest rooms for short-term stays.2 A commercially run conference lodge sits inside that definition.

The same regulation then carves out an exemption. Under 28 CFR 36.102(e): “This part does not apply to any private club (except to the extent that the facilities of the private club are made available to customers or patrons of a place of public accommodation), or to any religious entity or public entity.”2 The regulation defines the term plainly: “Religious entity means a religious organization, including a place of worship.”2

So a retreat centre owned and controlled by a religious organisation may sit outside that part of the ADA, while the commercial lodge down the road does not. This is a real distinction with a real consequence for a congregation whose members include wheelchair users, people with walkers, and anyone who cannot manage a flight of stairs twice a day.

Three things need saying alongside it, and all three matter.

The exemption is about this part of federal law. State accessibility statutes, local building codes and the conditions attached to any public funding are separate questions. We did not find a single national source that settles how they apply to a given faith-owned property, so we are not going to pretend one exists. Ask the venue.

An exemption is permission, not a description. Many faith-owned centres are step-free, have accessible bathrooms and have thought about this more carefully than the hotels have, because their own congregations are ageing. The point is not that these venues are inaccessible. The point is that you cannot infer accessibility from the law here, so you have to ask and, where it matters, go and look.

And ask in specifics, not in the abstract. How many bedrooms are on the ground floor. Whether there is a lift, and whether it reaches every floor with beds on it. Whether there is a roll-in shower anywhere on the property. Whether the route from parking to the main room has steps. Whether the bathrooms nearest the main room are accessible.

Insurance, and what a certificate can tell you

Congregation retreats are often led by volunteers, which makes the cover question worth asking properly.

The Texas Department of Insurance, a state insurance regulator, states that a certificate of insurance “may not use terms that would alter, amend, or extend coverage that is provided in the insurance policy”, and that “a certificate cannot say anything that is not the same as what is stated in the insurance policy”.3

That turns the usual advice inside out. The instruction is not “collect the certificate”. It is to read what the certificate lists, because a coverage that is not on it does not exist, and no wording on the certificate can add one. Ask your own congregation’s insurer the matching question about your group’s activities away from your own premises.

These are Texas descriptions of a form used nationally. Treat them as the regulator explaining what the document is, not as a rule binding a venue in your state.

The same bar, applied on a dated day

The Retreat Register applies the same four-question bar to every venue: real lodging, attractive grounds, professional operation, and a welcome to outside groups. Faith-based centres go through it like everything else. On July 10, 2026, faith-based retreat centres were reviewed one by one against that same quality bar, and the outcome was recorded in the build log: 7 promoted, 2 declined.4

That is the whole claim, and it is deliberately a small one. It is a count, on a date, of a review that happened.

Because of this bar, 91% of venues (1,928 of 2,125) list a direct phone number, only 182 venues publish a starting price, pricing in this industry is quote-only by default, and the deepest coverage today is California (162), New York (98), Louisiana (80).

The quote-only default is the reason the questions above have to be asked out loud. There is no page to read them off. There is a phone call, and 91% of the register can be reached on one.

If you run a faith-based retreat centre, submit your property for the four-question review. If you are planning, use the register to find curated sites, and take this list to the call.

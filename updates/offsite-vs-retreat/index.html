Executive Offsite or Company Retreat? How Planners Match the Venue to the Group

September 9, 2026 · The Retreat Register

An executive offsite and a team retreat share a word and need different buildings. Decide which one you are planning before you open a single venue page. A small leadership group needs a private room and a short trip. A whole team needs a bed for everyone and one room that seats the whole group at once.

Name the group before you name the place

The word offsite gets used for almost anything held away from the office. For venue hunting, two meanings matter. One is a working session for a handful of senior people who have decisions to make, sometimes about things they would rather not discuss in an open office. The other is a gathering of a whole department or company, where the main business is time together and the agenda is lighter.

Write the guest list before you write the brief. Count the people, then mark who has to be in every session and who joins for part of the time. A rule of thumb helps here. A short list where everyone makes decisions points to an offsite. A long list that crosses every level of the organization points to a retreat. Some events are both, with leaders meeting for a day before the rest of the team arrives, and those need a venue that can change shape halfway through.

That one choice settles most of what follows. It sets the number of bedrooms, the size of the main room and how far people can reasonably travel.

Some trips need a leadership day and an all-team gathering together. The cleanest version uses one venue in two stages. Leaders arrive first and work in a small private room. The rest of the group arrives the next afternoon, the large room opens, and the small room becomes a breakout space.

Ask the venue whether both rooms are free across all the dates. Then check the bed count for the night when everyone is there, since that night sets the size of the booking. If one property cannot hold the full group, two venues a short drive apart can work, with the leaders at the small one and the team at the larger one.

Plan the moment the two groups meet. Leaders who have spent a day deciding things will want to announce them, and the team will want a chance to react. Give that its own session in a room where everyone can sit, and leave time for questions.

What an executive offsite asks of a venue

If the leaders spend most of the trip in one room, that room needs a door that closes and walls that keep the conversation inside. Ask the venue in writing whether any other event is booked on the property for your dates, and whether the meeting room sits near a public lobby, a restaurant or a pool.

Short travel matters more for a small senior group than for a big one. If leaders fly in the night before and leave the minute the last session ends, a venue far from an airport eats into the working day. Map the drive from the nearest airport with regular flights to the venue's street address. Check it at the hour people would actually land.

Food for a small group can stay simple. A working lunch in the room keeps the discussion going, and one good dinner does more than three formal ones. What you need to know is how the staff serve when the door is shut. Ask whether they can set up lunch during a break, so nobody walks in halfway through a hard conversation.

A small property can suit this kind of meeting. A lodge with eight rooms can sometimes be booked whole, which takes strangers out of the building. Ask whether exclusive use is possible for your dates and which spaces it covers, such as the dining room and the grounds.

What a whole-team retreat asks of a venue

A team retreat needs one room where everyone can sit at the same time. Make that the first question you send, with the exact headcount and the layout you want, such as round tables or rows of chairs. A room listed for a standing reception can hold far more people than it seats at tables, so ask for the seated number in your layout.

Lodging can be the harder part. Thirty or sixty people need beds, and a venue may house only part of the group on site. Ask how many guest rooms the property has, how many beds are in each, and where the rest of the group would sleep. If some people stay off site, add their drive to the morning start time.

Plan for the spaces around the main room as well. Breakout groups need corners where they can talk without shouting over each other. People also need somewhere to sit between sessions. Ask the venue to list every indoor space you could use and how many people each one seats.

Plan access for everyone on the list

The bigger the group, the more likely it is that someone on it needs an accessible room, a step-free route or another change. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reports that more than one in four adults in the United States has some type of disability, and that the most common types affect cognition, mobility, independent living, hearing, vision and self-care.1 Plan a whole-team retreat as if some guests need these features, whether or not they have said so yet.

Federal rules give planners a baseline for hotels and inns. Under the Department of Justice regulations for the Americans with Disabilities Act, a place of lodging has to let guests reserve accessible rooms during the same hours and in the same manner as other guests. It also has to describe the accessible features of its rooms in enough detail for a guest to judge, on their own, whether a room meets their needs.2 So ask for that description in writing. A line that says accessible rooms are available is not enough to plan with.

Watch for one gap. The same regulation says several of those reservation duties do not apply to individual rooms or units that the operator of the overall facility does not own or substantially control.2 A retreat spread across separately owned cabins or rental houses can fall into that gap. If your venue is a set of units with different owners, ask about each unit by name.

Access covers the program too. Check the route from the bedrooms to the main room, the restrooms near it and any outdoor spot where a session might run. Before you book, send every attendee a short note that asks what they need to take part fully, and give them a private way to answer.

Write the brief that sorts venues fast

Send every venue the same short message so the replies line up side by side. For an offsite, give the number of people and the dates, ask for a private room with a door that closes, and ask what else is booked on the property. For a retreat, give the headcount and the seated layout you want, ask how many people can sleep on site, and ask for written detail about accessible rooms and routes.

Ask each venue to put its offer in writing and to list what the offer covers, room by room and meal by meal. That makes two very different properties comparable.

A venue that answers every question clearly, in writing, has also shown you how it handles detail. Put the replies in one shared document and mark which kind of event each venue suits. Shortlist from there. Here is a sentence you can paste into the first message: "Please tell us how many people your largest room seats at round tables, how many guests can sleep on site, and which rooms and routes are accessible, with details for each."

Plan Retreat Arrivals: How Planners Choose a Shuttle, Charter Bus or Cars

September 7, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Choose how the group arrives from where people are starting. One airport suits a shared shuttle. Several cities suit people making their own way in. A large group leaving from one place suits a chartered coach. Look up any bus company in the federal safety search before you book it, then send one arrival sheet that every guest confirms in writing.

Start from where people are coming from

Make a list of every attendee and how they plan to travel: the airport they will fly into, the train they will take or the city they will drive from, and roughly when they expect to arrive. The pattern in that list picks the arrival model for you.

If most people land at one airport within a few hours of each other, a shared shuttle in two or three pickup windows works well. Set each window to catch a cluster of flights, and tell people which one is theirs.

If people are spread across several airports and cities, one shuttle can mean a long wait for whoever lands first. Let them come in their own cars or by ride service, and plan for arrivals across the whole afternoon. Carpools from the same city cut the number of cars at the venue.

Train arrivals need the same care. A station in a small town may have no taxis waiting, so treat it like a small airport. Give it a pickup window of its own, or name the person who collects anyone arriving there.

If a large group leaves from one office or one city, a chartered coach moves everyone at once. The trip itself becomes part of the retreat, and nobody has to find the venue on unfamiliar roads after dark.

Check a bus company before you sign

A coach can move a big group at once, and it comes with the most to check. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, the agency that oversees commercial buses and trucks, urges travel planners to "Look Before You Book." Its Bus Safety Search lets you look up a bus company, see whether it is authorized to operate, review its safety records, and make sure it is licensed and insured.1

The agency's planning guide adds the basics. Generally, an interstate, for-hire passenger carrier that operates a motorcoach must have both a USDOT number and operating authority registration from the agency. It must carry the federal minimum level of insurance, and it must provide accessible motorcoach service, with wheelchair lifts and securement areas, under the Americans with Disabilities Act.2

Ask the company in writing for its USDOT number, then look it up yourself. The agency issues three safety ratings: Satisfactory, Conditional and Unsatisfactory. It says bus companies with an Unsatisfactory rating should not be operating, and that a company without a rating has not had its operations evaluated against the agency's safety fitness standards.2 If anyone in the group uses a wheelchair, say so in the first message, so the company sends a coach with a lift. Ask too how much luggage the vehicle takes, since suitcases and session equipment for a full group can fill the hold.

Ask about the driver and the hours

The same guide says drivers for for-hire motorcoach companies must hold a commercial driver's license with a passenger endorsement, along with a valid medical examiner's certificate. A commercial license is required to drive a vehicle designed to carry sixteen or more people, counting the driver.2 The guide also suggests asking whether the company bans drivers from texting or using a cell phone while driving, and whether it has a drug and alcohol testing program.2

Hours matter on a long trip. The guide states that no interstate motorcoach driver can drive more than ten consecutive hours. If the route and schedule need more driving than the rules allow, it says to make sure a safe backup driver is available.2 Build that limit into the plan when the venue is far away. Ask the company in writing how it will cover the full trip, the return included. Ask too how long the driver waits at the venue between the two.

Airport shuttles get the same check. Ask the shuttle company in writing for its USDOT number, and look it up in the Bus Safety Search before the booking. FMCSA notes that a company with no USDOT number may operate within a single state, and points planners to the state oversight agency, usually the state Department of Transportation, for its safety information.1 Keep the written confirmation with the arrival plan.

Plan the moment people arrive

Arrival is the first session of the retreat, whether or not it appears on the agenda. Put a named host at the venue from the earliest expected arrival. The host greets people and hands out keys or directions. They also keep the list of who is still on the way. Give the host a printed sheet of every guest with their pickup window and their flight or train, so the host can see at a glance who is missing.

Give early arrivals somewhere to go. Ask the venue in writing whether rooms are ready when the first people land, and if they are not, where luggage can be left and where people can sit. A lounge with water and coffee turns a long wait into a chance to meet colleagues.

Hold the first session until most of the group is in. A kickoff with a third of the seats empty means repeating the opening later. Pick a start time after the last planned pickup window, with some slack for a slow road.

Plan food for the gap. A group that lands at midday and waits until the evening for dinner arrives hungry. Set out a simple meal or snacks timed to the main pickup windows.

Drivers need their own plan. Ask the venue in writing how many cars it can park and where. Give drivers the exact spot to leave their car, and if spaces are short, set a meeting point where people can leave cars and share rides for the last stretch.

Write one arrival sheet, and decide what happens when plans slip

Put everything a traveler needs on one page. Include the venue's street address, the pickup points and times for each window, the name of the shuttle or coach company, the host's name and how to reach them on the day, and the plan for a late flight. Send it two weeks before the trip. Ask every guest to reply by email with their arrival time and their pickup window. The replies give you a list to check against, and they show you who has not read the sheet.

Flights run late and roads close. Decide now what happens in each case, so the host does not have to invent it at the door. For a late flight, say whether the traveler waits for the next pickup window or comes in on their own. For a late coach, agree with the company in writing how it will tell the host. If it is running well behind, move the first session back.

Plan departures the same way. End the last session with enough time for the final pickup window to reach the airport, and put the departure times on the same arrival sheet.

A sentence to send the coach company with your first request: "Please send your USDOT number, the license and endorsement your driver holds for this vehicle, and how you will cover driver hours for the full trip, including the return."

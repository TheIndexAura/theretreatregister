Methodology

"Quote only" is a feature, not a failure

August 1, 2026 · The Retreat Register

You have seen the “from $X” price on a venue listing. It promises a budget and frequently delivers a different one. The gap between the two is not usually a lie about the venue. It is a number that was never describing your booking in the first place.

Most venues do not publish a price at all. On July 12, 2026 the register held 1,374 curated venues and 133 of them published a starting price on their own website, or 9.7%. On July 19, 2026, with 2,152 venues on file, the count was 184.1 Across eight dated readings taken between July 12 and July 19, 2026, computed from the counts each page printed, the share never reached one in ten. Roughly eleven venues in twelve quote each group directly.

So when a listing site shows a price for a venue that does not publish one, that price came from somewhere other than the venue’s rate sheet.

Where a “from $X” actually comes from

A starting price is built from the cheapest true statement anyone can find. It is usually the lowest room type, on the lowest night of the lowest month, at single occupancy, before the resort or facility fee, before any food and beverage minimum, before the service charge, and before tax. Each one of those choices is defensible on its own. Stacked together they describe a booking almost nobody makes.

For a group the number is worse than incomplete, because the levers that decide a group price are not in it. A room rate does not carry the buyout threshold, the food and beverage minimum, or the percentage the venue adds for service. Those are the things that move the total, and a starting price is silent on all of them.

What a first number does to a budget

The first figure a planner sees becomes the shape of the conversation that follows. It sets what “expensive” means, it decides which venues get a second look, and it is the number the finance team remembers three weeks later when the real quote arrives. Correcting it costs more than never having it, because by then the budget has been written, circulated, and approved by somebody who never saw the venue site.

A blank field costs nothing. It tells you the price is not knowable from the web page, which is true, and it tells you the next action is a phone call, which is the correct next action.

The rule that now governs printed lodging prices

This stopped being only an etiquette question in 2025. The Federal Trade Commission’s Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees, 16 CFR Part 464, took effect on May 12, 2025.2 It covers short-term lodging by name: “Covered good or service means: (1) Live-event tickets; or (2) Short-term lodging, including temporary sleeping accommodations at a hotel, motel, inn, short-term rental, vacation rental, or other place of lodging.”2

The rule defines the number a reader is owed. “Total price means the maximum total of all fees or charges a consumer must pay for any good(s) or service(s) and any mandatory ancillary good or service, except that government charges, shipping charges, and fees or charges for any optional ancillary good or service may be excluded.”2 Section 464.2(a) then makes it “an unfair and deceptive practice” to “offer, display, or advertise any price of a covered good or service without clearly and conspicuously disclosing the total price”.2

Section 464.3 goes further and reaches the description of a fee rather than only its size. It is a violation to “misrepresent any fee or charge, including: the nature, purpose, amount, or refundability of any fee or charge; and the identity of the good or service for which the fee or charge is imposed”.2

Read the scope before you lean on it. The rule reaches prices offered, displayed or advertised to consumers. A negotiated group contract is a different transaction, and the rule was not written for it. What the rule gives a planner is the vocabulary. “Total price”, and “the nature, purpose, and amount of any fee or charge imposed on the transaction that has been excluded from total price”, are the words the federal government chose for what an honest price disclosure contains.2 They work just as well in an email to a sales manager.

What the register does with a missing price

Where a venue publishes real, current prices, the register shows them. Where the price is quote-only, the listing says quote only.

The register screens for essentials before listing a venue, focusing first on a direct phone number and dedicated meeting or lodging space. Listings are also cut in ongoing quality passes. On July 11, 2026 a person read every published row, every name and every website, and 274 entries were cut that no automated filter had caught, including budget hotel chains and template-name fakes pointing at unrelated websites.1 Three separate entries in the build log record passes that removed venues, on July 18, July 22 and July 24, 2026.1

None of that produces a price for a venue that does not publish one. It produces an accurate empty field, which is the only honest thing to put there.

How to read a price field on any directory

Four checks, in order, and they take about a minute.

  1. Find the as-of date. A price with no date attached is not a price, it is a sentence someone typed once. Look for the month the figure was measured.
  2. Find out what unit it is. Per person or per room changes the total by a factor of two on most bookings, and per night against per stay changes it by the length of the trip.
  3. Look for the word “from”. A “from” price is the floor of a range whose ceiling is not shown, and the ceiling is where your group probably lands.
  4. Check whether fees are inside or outside. If the page does not say, assume outside, and assume a service charge percentage sits on top of the lines you negotiate.

If a listing survives all four, it is a genuine anchor. If it fails any of them, use it to decide who to call and nothing else.

The anchors that are real

Two public numbers survive those four checks.

The first is the set of venues that publish their own rates. That was 133 venues on July 12, 2026 and 184 on July 19, 2026.1 A published rate at a comparable property, in the same county, for the same month, is the closest thing this industry gives a planner to a market reference.

The second is the federal per diem table. The General Services Administration sets a lodging ceiling for each county and each month of the federal fiscal year, and it builds those rates from average daily rate data collected from local lodging properties.3 For fiscal year 2026 the standard lodging rate is $110 a night and the standard meals and incidental expenses rate is $68 a day.3 It is a reimbursement ceiling rather than a market rate, so it will not tell you what a lodge charges. It will tell you what the federal government decided a night in that county in that month is worth, which is enough to judge whether a quote sits inside the ordinary range.

That is the whole argument for an empty price field. A gap points you at a phone call and two public references. A fabricated number points you at a budget that will not survive the quote.

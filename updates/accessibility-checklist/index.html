Check Venue Accessibility With Written Questions on Routes and Restrooms

August 13, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Before you book, turn the word accessible into written questions for the venue. The federal Access Board and ADA.gov give you wording for asking about routes, restrooms and service dogs, one building at a time.

Turn the word accessible into a route

Start with the walk a guest actually takes. A person arrives by car, by shuttle or on foot, finds the entrance, reaches a sleeping room, reaches the meeting room and finds a restroom along the way. Ask about each of those links one at a time, so that a gap in any one of them shows up before the booking is signed. The federal Access Board publishes a guide to the ADA Accessibility Standards, and its chapter on accessible routes gives you a vocabulary for asking about that walk.

The guide says at least one accessible route must be provided within the site to accessible facility entrances from site arrival points, and it lists accessible parking and accessible passenger loading zones, public streets and sidewalks, and each public transportation stop.1 It also says at least one accessible route must connect all accessible spaces and elements.1 Turn those two sentences into one question for the venue: is there a single continuous route from where my guests arrive to the sleeping rooms, the meeting rooms, the dining room and the restrooms, and can you describe it in writing?

Ask about the pieces of that route one at a time. The guide names the components of an accessible route as walking surfaces, doorways, ramps, curb ramps and elevators, with platform lifts included where they are permitted.1 A venue may have a good doorway and a good ramp and still have a gravel path between them, so ask about the surface as well. Ask which buildings on the property have an elevator and which rely on a ramp or a lift. Ask who holds the key or the code to a lift, and whether a guest can use it without waiting for staff.

Stairs, long routes and the back of the building

The same guide chapter says accessible vertical interior circulation must be in the same area as stairs and escalators.1 That sentence describes a standard, and a venue may or may not meet it in every building, so use it as a prompt. When a guest has to go to the far end of a hallway, through a service corridor or around the outside of the building to reach the one elevator, ask the venue to say so plainly before you sign.

The guide also lists handrails at ramps with a rise greater than six inches, and at stairs that are part of a means of egress.1 On a site visit, look at the handrails on every ramp and stair that your group will use. If a ramp has no rail, or a rail stops short of the bottom, write down where it is and ask the venue whether it is part of a planned repair.

Distance matters on a retreat property, because guests may walk between the lodge, the dining hall and the meeting room more than once in a day, so ask the venue how those buildings sit in relation to each other. The guide says passing space is required every two hundred feet along a route, and that the passing space can be a square or a T-shaped space.1 You do not need to measure that yourself. Ask the venue whether the longest routes on the property have places where two people, one of them using a wheelchair, can pass each other. Ask how long the longest walk is between the sleeping rooms and the meeting room, and decide whether your group can manage it twice before lunch.

The chapter has its own section headed Alterations and Additions, and it does not say how any particular building on a retreat property is treated. Ask the venue for property facts, meaning when each building was constructed and when it was last altered. Which accessibility rules apply to a given building is a legal question, so put that to a lawyer.

Check the restrooms before you check the rooms

A venue can have a perfect ramp and a restroom that does not fit a wheelchair, so restrooms deserve their own check. The Access Board guide to toilet rooms says turning space is required in the room, and that it can be a circle sixty inches across or a T-shaped space.2 Rather than carry a tape measure into every restroom, ask the venue to confirm that each restroom your group will use has turning space of that kind, and to say which restrooms do not.

The guide says doors can swing into the turning space.2 Stall doors and fixture clearances are layout questions for the venue. Ask whether the stall doors and the fixtures leave room for a wheelchair, ask which way each restroom door opens and whether the door sits in the way of the toilet or the sink. When you visit, stand in the doorway and picture a person in a wheelchair coming in, turning around and reaching the sink.

Look at the stalls. The guide says at least one toilet compartment must be wheelchair accessible where toilet compartments are provided.2 It describes a second kind, the ambulatory accessible compartment, which has parallel grab bars and does not accommodate wheelchairs or scooters.2 Ask which kind the restroom has, and ask whether there is a wheelchair accessible compartment on the same floor as your meeting room.

Smaller fittings count too. The guide says grab bars can have circular or non-circular cross-sections but must have rounded edges, and that dispensers, receptacles, shelves and coat hooks must comply where they are provided.2 It adds that paper dispensers must allow continuous paper flow.2 You can check these quickly during a visit. Touch the grab bars, look for a towel or paper dispenser that a seated person could reach, and write down anything that looks out of place.

Service dogs and what a venue may ask

If a guest arrives with a service dog, decide whether to tell the venue ahead of time. The ADA.gov page on service animal requirements says service animals are defined as dogs that are individually trained to do work or perform tasks for people with disabilities.3 It also says dogs whose sole function is to provide comfort or emotional support do not qualify as service animals under the ADA.3 Ask the venue about its own pet policy separately if a guest travels with an animal that is not a service dog.

When the need is not obvious, the page says staff may ask two questions. The first is whether the dog is a service animal required because of a disability, and the second is what work or task the dog has been trained to perform.3 It says staff cannot ask about the person's disability, require medical documentation, require a special identification card or training documentation for the dog, or ask that the dog demonstrate its ability to perform the work or task.3 Ask the venue whether its front desk staff know these two questions before arrival day.

The page also addresses money. It says that if a business requires a deposit or fee to be paid by patrons with pets, it must waive the charge for service animals.3 Check the venue contract and the booking confirmation for a pet fee and ask how it treats service dogs. The page adds that a business such as a hotel that normally charges guests for damage may also charge a customer with a disability for damage caused by the person or the service animal, so read the damage clause in the contract too.

The page says a service animal must be under the control of its handler, and that staff are not required to provide care for or supervision of a service animal.3 Plan the schedule with that in mind. Ask the guest whether they want an outdoor spot for the dog during long sessions, a place to settle during meals, and a seat with room for the animal. Ask the venue to point out the nearest suitable outdoor area to the meeting room, and share the answer with the guest before they travel.

Keep the answers and send them back

After you have asked about routes, restrooms and animals, you will have a pile of replies in different forms. Put them on one sheet with a row for each building and a column for each question: arrival route, interior route, elevator or lift, restroom turning space, restroom compartment type, door swing and service dog policy. A venue that answered in vague words gets a blank cell, and the blank cell tells you what to ask next.

Send the sheet to the guest who relies on these details and ask whether it covers what they need. Ask that guest which details they need to see in writing, and whether they would like to speak with the venue directly. Decide with the guest whether photographs would help, and if so ask the venue to send them for the arrival path, a restroom and a sleeping room.

Keep the sheet with your venue comparison. When two venues look equal on price and setting, the one that answered every cell in writing is the one you can plan around. Which accessibility rules apply to your event, and what they require of the venue, is a question for a lawyer.

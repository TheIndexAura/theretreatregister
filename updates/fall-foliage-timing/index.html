Plan a Fall Retreat Around Peak Foliage: How Planners Pick the Week

September 10, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Set the dates for a fall retreat by when the leaves turn at the place you are going. Color starts high and moves down, and the peak shifts from year to year. The peak weeks also bring crowds. Decide first whether the leaves matter to the program. Then pick a week and ask venues about the weeks on either side of it.

Decide whether the view is part of the program

Two groups can book the same lodge in the same week for different reasons. One spends the days in a meeting room with the blinds half down. The other has planned a hike every morning and a long drive on the last afternoon. The second group needs the color. The first is paying for a view it will see at breakfast.

Write one sentence in the plan: the program needs the leaves, or it does not. If it does, you are planning around a moving target and everything below applies in full. If it does not, you are free to choose the week for other reasons, such as the weather you want, the dates people can travel or the venue's own calendar.

Be honest about it. A planner who loves autumn can talk a whole group into a peak weekend that the agenda never uses, and the group then pays for the busiest roads of the year without stepping outside.

There is a middle path. Hold the meeting days in a quieter week near the color, and add one optional outing for the people who want it, on a free afternoon at the end.

Learn how color moves through a region

In Great Smoky Mountains National Park, leaves change in waves, and height above sea level sets much of the order. The National Park Service explains that color can begin at the highest elevations as early as mid-September. It typically develops above four thousand feet from early to mid-October. At lower and middle elevations, the most brilliant displays commonly come between mid-October and early November.1 The park says the colors appear in waves, starting at the highest peaks and moving down over several weeks.1

The same logic is worth applying to the property you book. A lodge on a ridge and an inn in the valley a short drive away may not turn in the same week. Ask each venue for its elevation, and set it beside the pattern for the area around it.

If the leaves matter, ask for photos too. Request pictures of the grounds taken in the same week of past years, with the date on each one. A dated photo shows what that address looked like in that week.

The park also explains why leaves change at all. Color emerges as days grow shorter and temperatures drop. As the green pigment fades, yellow pigments show through. Reds and purples form when sugars in the leaves break down in bright autumn sun.1 The park says the timing depends on many environmental factors, which is why it calls the exact peak dates often difficult to predict.1 No planner can book the peak with certainty. You can book a window.

Read the foliage reports, and know what they measure

New York publishes a weekly foliage report during the season, and it shows how these reports work. Its reports come from more than one hundred volunteer field observers and are issued every Wednesday afternoon. Each one describes expected color conditions for the coming weekend.2 The program defines peak as the best overall appearance the foliage will have during the season, taking into account color transition, brilliance and leaf drop.2

Look at what that means for booking. A weekly report describes the weekend ahead. That is useful for the final plan and too late to book a venue by. For the booking itself, use past seasons. Where a program keeps earlier reports online, read the same weeks in past years and note when your area was called near peak and when it was called peak. The spread across those years shows how wide your window really is. Treat a report from the next county over as a guide for your own address, and check the difference in elevation before you lean on it.

Keep the current reports for the end. In the last two weeks before the trip, they help you decide whether the long drive belongs on the first afternoon or the last, and whether the hike should head uphill or stay low.

Book a window of weeks

Once you know the window, pick a week inside it that fits the group. Early in the window, color is still building and some trees are green. Late in it, more leaves are on the ground and more branches on the hills are bare. Neither is wrong. A group that hikes may enjoy watching the color build, and a group that wants one memorable drive may prefer the middle.

Ask each venue about the weeks on either side of your choice. A written reply that lists availability and terms for three weeks in a row shows how the venue treats the season. It also lets you move by a week without starting the search again. Ask, too, how the venue handles a date change inside that window, and get the answer in writing before you pay a deposit.

Expect crowds in the peak weeks. In the Smokies, the National Park Service names October as the busiest month of the year.1 Schedule outings early in the day. Name a second spot in advance, so the group has somewhere to go if the first parking area is full.

Plan a program that works in any color

Even a careful booking can miss. The park ties the timing to many environmental factors, so the peak can come early or late. Build the agenda so the trip works whatever the leaves do. Put the sessions that matter most indoors, and give the outings flexible slots, so a hike can move a day earlier or later without breaking the schedule. If the venue has covered outdoor space, such as a porch or a pavilion, ask how many people it seats, so an outdoor session can still run in the rain.

Watch the light as well. The park's explanation of fall color starts with days growing shorter,1 and a short day changes the program as much as the leaves do. Look up the sunset time for your dates, and plan outings to finish well before it, with the drive back included.

Choose at least one outing that has nothing to do with color, such as a waterfall, a farm visit or a walk down a small town's main street. If the leaves fall early, the group still has a good afternoon.

Say it plainly in the welcome note. Leaf timing changes every year, and the plan allows for that.

When you write to venues, ask for the property's elevation and how its color usually compares with the area nearby. Ask what else is booked on the property and in town during your window, since other events can take the rooms you were counting on. A sentence to paste: "Please tell us your elevation and what else is booked nearby in those weeks. Please also send your availability and written terms for each of the three weeks listed below."

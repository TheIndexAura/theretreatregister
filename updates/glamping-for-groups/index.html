Plan a Group Glamping Retreat: Storm Shelter and Heating Questions

August 19, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Glamping gives a group comfortable beds in a field, a forest or beside a lake. It also spreads people out and puts heat and shelter in the hands of the site. Before you book, ask where the whole group goes in a thunderstorm and what heats each unit, and ask for both answers in writing.

What changes when the group sleeps outdoors

A hotel puts a group under one roof. A glamping site spreads it across a field or a hillside in tents, domes, cabins or yurts, with a walk between every bed and every shared space. That arrangement is the appeal, and it is also the planning problem. Nobody can knock on a neighbor's door in a hurry, nobody has a corridor to the dining room and nobody can assume that a unit holds in warmth the way a building does.

Two questions deserve an answer before you sign. Where does the whole group go when a thunderstorm arrives, and how do people get there from the farthest unit? And what heats each unit when the nights are cold? Both answers belong in writing, because both are easy to describe loosely on a website and hard to fix on the day.

Ask where the whole group shelters in a storm

The National Weather Service gives a short instruction for thunderstorms. When you hear thunder, move to a safe shelter at once, which means a substantial building with electricity or plumbing, or an enclosed, metal-topped vehicle with the windows up.1 It adds that you should wait thirty minutes or longer after you hear the last sound of thunder before you leave that shelter.1 It also warns that no place outside is safe when thunderstorms are in the area.1

Apply that to a glamping site and the questions write themselves. Which building on the property meets that description, and can it hold your entire group at once? Is it close enough to every unit that people can reach it quickly, or does the farthest tent sit a long walk away? If the answer is a vehicle, how many people does it carry and who drives it? Ask the venue to name the building it would use, and judge that building against the description above.

The answer shapes the walk at night as well. A group scattered over a wide field needs a longer warning than a camp built around one hall. Find out who watches the weather, how the group is told and what the venue expects people to do if a storm arrives after dark. Find out how far the path from each unit to the shelter runs, and whether it is lit. Request the distances so you can compare sites on the same terms, and note which sites keep the shelter in sight of the units.

Think about your own agenda too. A day built around an outdoor session needs an indoor version that fits the same group. Check whether the shelter building has the space, the seating and the connection you need to hold that session indoors, and whether it is free at the hours you would use it.

Ask what heats each unit

Cold-season glamping adds a heat question. The Environmental Protection Agency describes carbon monoxide as a colorless, practically odorless and tasteless gas, and it lists unvented kerosene and gas space heaters, gas stoves, and generators and other gasoline-powered equipment among the sources.2 At low levels, it says, the gas can cause fatigue in healthy people. Higher levels can bring impaired vision and coordination, headaches, dizziness, confusion and nausea, and at higher concentrations exposure can be fatal.2

Those early symptoms are easy to blame on something else. A group that has traveled, hiked and eaten a big dinner may put fatigue and a headache down to a long day. Do this before the trip, while there is still time to choose another site if the answers fall short.

Put the questions to the venue in one email. What heats each unit, and is the heater vented? Do the units use a gas stove or a gas lantern inside? Does each unit have a working carbon monoxide alarm, and how does the venue test it? Where does the generator sit relative to the sleeping units? If a unit is heated by electricity, ask how the venue keeps the circuit from overloading when a whole row of heaters runs at once.

Ask about the practical details between the two

Once shelter and heat are settled, the remaining questions are ordinary ones, and they matter just as much on a site where everything is spread out. Find out how guests reach the bathrooms and showers from each unit, and whether the paths are lit at night. Find out how a guest who uses a mobility aid gets from a unit to the dining room, and which units are on the flattest ground. It also helps to ask how the venue handles a guest who wants to leave in the middle of the night, because a shuttle or a walk to a car park can take longer on a large site.

Learn who is on the property overnight and how a guest reaches them. Request the answer as a written plan, with a name and a place to find the person, so that a guest with a problem at midnight has somewhere to go. Find out what the venue does if a storm closes the road. A site that sits down a long track can be hard to leave when the weather turns.

The units themselves need questions too. What are the walls and floors made of, and how does each unit handle wind and rain? What bedding does the venue supply, and is there extra for a cold night? Where does drinking water come from, and how far is it from the farthest unit? A glamping site can look the same in photographs as a cabin resort, and these answers show how different the two are at midnight.

Fire is another topic. If the site has fire pits, ask who lights and watches them, what rules apply to guests and what happens when the wind picks up. If the units have candles, lanterns or stoves, ask what the venue allows inside. These are simple questions, and a well-run site has simple answers.

Keep the answers with your booking papers

Ask for the important answers to be written into the contract. If the venue says the lodge holds the whole group in a storm, ask it to confirm that in the contract along with the walking distance. If it says every unit has an alarm, ask it to put that in the contract too. A property that stands behind its answers may be happy to put them on paper, and hesitation is something to weigh. Read the replies once more before you send the deposit, and check that every question you asked has an answer beside it.

Share the shelter plan and the heating details with the group before the trip. A short note that says where to go in a storm, how long to stay and who to find helps guests feel looked after, and it gives them what they need if you are not nearby when something changes.

9 in 10 retreat venues won’t show you a price — here’s how to read that

July 12, 2026 · The Retreat Register

On July 12, 2026 the register held 1,374 curated venues. Of those, 133 published a starting price somewhere on their own website, which is 9.7%.1 The other 1,241 quote each group directly.

That share has not improved as the register has grown. On July 17, 2026 the register held 2,281 venues and 188 of them published a starting price. On July 19, 2026, with 2,152 venues on file, 184 published one.1 Computed from the counts those pages printed, the share sat between 8.2% and 9.7% across eight dated readings taken between July 12 and July 19, 2026. It did not reach one in ten on any of them. As the sample grew, the published-price share fell. One venue in twelve is a fairer description of this industry than one in ten.

Most planners hit that wall, assume something shady is going on, and start distrusting every venue site they open. Quote-only pricing is not hiding. It is structural, and the plainest evidence for that comes from the federal government rather than from the hospitality industry.

The government cannot publish one price either

The U.S. General Services Administration sets the per diem rates that federal employees are reimbursed at when they travel. For fiscal year 2026 the standard lodging rate is $110 a night and the standard meals and incidental expenses rate is $68 a day.2 Those are ceilings rather than averages. GSA states it directly: “The lodging per diem rates are a maximum amount; the traveler only receives actual lodging costs up to that maximum rate.”2

Two features of that program answer the pricing question better than anything a venue could write.

The first is that the government does not trust its own ceiling. The same regulation permits actual expense reimbursement of up to 300 percent of the applicable maximum per diem rate when the rate is not enough to cover the trip.2 A pricing system that has to allow a fourfold override is a pricing system admitting that lodging costs vary more than one published number can carry.

The second is that GSA does not publish one figure per town. It publishes twelve. The lodging rates are built from average daily rate data collected from local lodging properties, and they change month by month.2 For fiscal year 2026 the lodging ceiling in Napa County, California is $172 in January and $246 from February through November. In Teton County, Wyoming, which covers Jackson, it is $212 for eight months of the year and $420 from June through September.2

If the federal government needs twelve numbers to describe one county for one year, a venue selling lodging, meeting space, meals and staff time as one bundle is not going to reduce your group to one number on a web page.

What actually moves the number

The register’s own advice on this has been published since July 12, 2026, and it still holds: ask the four levers up front, which are your date flexibility, the buyout threshold, food-and-beverage minimums, and service fees. Those four determine the real number, not the nightly rate.

  • Head count is the lever most planners underestimate. It does not scale a single price up. It moves you between price structures. Twelve people might be sold rooms at a published rate with a meeting room thrown in. Forty people is a room block with a contracted minimum. Ninety people at a small property is a buyout whether anyone uses that word or not, because there is no version of the booking where other guests still fit.
  • Season changes the lodging line and nothing else on the sheet. Food costs roughly the same in February as in October. Staff costs the same. The room is what moves, which is why a date change can shift a quote further than any other single request you can make.
  • The buyout threshold is a specific number, and most venues know theirs. It is the point at which the property stops selling rooms individually and starts selling itself whole. Ask for it as a room count, not as a price.
  • Food and beverage minimums are a floor on spend, not a price for food. If a venue sets a $12,000 minimum and your group eats $9,000 worth, you owe $12,000. The gap is not a penalty and it is not negotiable after the fact, because you agreed to the floor when you signed.
  • Service fees are a percentage applied to lines you have already agreed to, which is why they surprise people. They compound rather than add. There is more on how those lines stack up in the invoice ambush.

How a group quote is actually built

A quote is assembled in a fixed order, and knowing the order tells you where to push.

Lodging comes first, priced per room per night or per person per night. Meeting space is priced next, and it is frequently reduced or removed entirely if the room block and the food spend are large enough, which means the rental line is often a negotiating chip rather than a cost. Food and beverage follows, usually per person per meal, measured against the minimum. Audio visual, setup, and anything the venue has to staff outside normal hours sits after that. A service charge is then applied as a percentage of some or all of those lines. Tax is applied last, and in most places it is applied to the service charge as well.

The order is why a quote can grow after the part you negotiated. Suppose a venue agrees to $18,000 of food and beverage and applies a service charge of 22 percent. That is $3,960 before a cent of tax, and it was created by the number you just negotiated down. Percentages of the specific figures in your own quote are the only honest way to check this, which is why an all-in total matters more than any single rate.

What a published price is good for

Use the 133 as anchors. Venues that publish rates give you a real baseline for their market before you send a single inquiry. A published rate in the same county, at a comparable property, in the same month, is the closest thing to a market reference this industry offers a planner working without a procurement team.

The federal per diem table is the second anchor, and almost nobody in this niche uses it. It is public, it is set from real lodging data, and it is broken out by county and by month.2 It is a reimbursement ceiling rather than a market rate, so it will not tell you what a lodge charges. It will tell you what the government decided a night in that county is worth in that month, which is enough to tell you whether a quote is in the ordinary range or well outside it.

The questions, in order

Ask these in this sequence, because each answer changes what the next one means.

  1. What is your buyout threshold in rooms? A good answer is a number. A vague answer means the property has not thought about groups.
  2. What does the total look like at that head count, all in? A good answer names lodging, meeting space, food and beverage, service charge and tax as separate lines that sum to one figure.
  3. What is the food and beverage minimum for our dates, and what counts toward it? A good answer says whether bar spend, staff meals and delivered coffee count.
  4. What is the service charge percentage, and which lines does it apply to? A good answer is a percentage and a list. An answer that is only a percentage is half an answer.
  5. Which of these lines moves if we shift the dates? A good answer separates the lodging line from the rest, because that is the line that moves.
  6. What is not in this quote? A good answer includes parking, resort or facility fees, and anything billed on consumption.
  7. Send that as one written total. A venue that answers a group inquiry inside one business day with a real number tends to run its retreats the same way.

What the register does with a missing price

Some listing platforms fill the gap by printing an estimate, a made-up “from $X” that makes the card look complete. A wrong number is worse than a gap, because it anchors your budget to fiction and you find out at quote time. Where a venue publishes pricing, the register shows it. Where it is quote-only, the listing says quote only. There is more on that choice in why we say quote only.

There is now a federal rule about printed prices in lodging. Since May 12, 2025, 16 CFR Part 464 has made it “an unfair and deceptive practice” for a business to “offer, display, or advertise any price of a covered good or service without clearly and conspicuously disclosing the total price”, and the rule defines covered goods to include “short-term lodging, including temporary sleeping accommodations at a hotel, motel, inn, short-term rental, vacation rental, or other place of lodging”.3

Read the scope carefully before you rely on it. The rule governs prices offered, displayed or advertised to consumers. A negotiated group contract is not an advertised consumer price, so the discipline the rule imposes on a nightly rate is something a planner still has to ask for in writing. The wording of the rule is a useful thing to borrow when you do, because it names exactly what you want: the total price, plus “the nature, purpose, and amount of any fee or charge imposed on the transaction that has been excluded from total price”.3

Straight answers on price, including “this venue does not publish one”, are set out in the methodology, in writing. Quote-only markets also tend to be the negotiable ones, because a property with no competition for your dates has room to move. The coverage page shows how far off the beaten path the register goes.

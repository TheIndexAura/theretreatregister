Choose a Lake, Mountain or Desert Setting for the Retreat's Job

September 19, 2026 · The Retreat Register

What would a lake, a mountain or a desert ask of your group? Decide what the retreat has to accomplish first, then check the water and heat hazards against CDC pages and gather questions to ask about cold.

Start with the job, then pick the scenery

A retreat has a purpose before it has a location. Some groups come to repair a working relationship, and others come to settle a strategy. Write that purpose in one sentence and keep it where you can see it, so ask of each setting whether it suits the work, and not only how it looks in a photograph.

Would long, loose conversations on a dock or along the shore help your group? If the job is rebuilding trust or settling an old argument, would unhurried time help? If the job is strategy, how much distance from daily routines does your group want? If it is reflection or planning, how much quiet does it want? Ask each venue what its site is like. These are matters of fit and your own judgment, so check each against your people.

Ask a few plain questions about your people. Do they like being outdoors? Do any of them have health conditions or mobility limits, and does anyone fear deep water? What would you do if half the group were miserable by the second afternoon? The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention publish pages on water and on heat, so ask the venue about cold as well, and you will know what to raise before you commit.

Lakes: water needs a plan before the schedule does

The CDC drowning prevention page lists steps for water activities. It says to designate a responsible adult to supervise closely and constantly when children are in or near water, and it says children who have had swimming lessons still need close and constant supervision when in or around water.1 The CDC instruction covers children. As the planner's own policy, and not a CDC rule, you might also name one person for that job at every water session of a retreat that brings children or teenagers, and tell the group who it is.

The same page says life jackets reduce the risk of drowning while boating for people of all ages and swimming abilities, and it says children should use them for all activities while in and around natural water.1 It also says not to rely on air-filled or foam toys, as these are not safety devices.1 If the agenda includes paddle boards or a boat ride, ask the venue what life jackets it keeps on site and who checks the fit.

The page says to avoid drinking alcohol before or during swimming or boating, and not to drink alcohol while supervising children.1 That affects the evening plan. If the dock party and a swim session share a stretch of shoreline, decide how the two are kept apart. The page also says to always swim with a buddy and to choose swimming sites that have lifeguards when possible, and it says adults should supervise children closely even when lifeguards are present.1 Ask the venue whether its water has lifeguards at all.

The page says lakes, rivers, and oceans have hidden hazards such as dangerous currents or waves and limited visibility.1 It says to check the forecast before activities in or near water, and that local weather conditions can change quickly and cause dangerous flash floods and strong winds.1 Could you leave a flexible block on the agenda and name the person who decides on the day? Ask the venue about lake temperature.

Mountains: ask about cold and distance before you book

Start with the walking distances. Ask how far the sleeping rooms sit from the meeting room and whether the paths are cleared. Ask what guests should pack for the walk between buildings, and whether the venue keeps spare coats or gloves.

Think about what a cold day does to the agenda. A hike set for the middle of a session may need a shorter route or an indoor replacement. Write the replacement before you need it, and tell guests in the welcome note which outdoor blocks may move. Ask the venue who makes the decision when the wind picks up and who tells the group.

Ask about travel as well. Find out how guests reach the site in the month you chose, what the venue does when the road is bad, and whether a late arrival is a problem for dinner and the first session. If the venue sits at a high elevation, ask what guests with heart or breathing conditions should know before they come. A planner can also check the local forecast service for the weeks around the dates, and then ask the venue what it has seen at that time of year.

Heat: plan for heat and water outdoors

The CDC heat page says that if your body gets too hot, you can get sick, and that hot days can affect anyone.2 It says some groups may have to take additional action on hot days, and its list includes people with heart disease and pregnant women, along with infants and young children.2 Check whether any guests fall into those groups, and build the plan so nobody has to announce a condition to be looked after.

The page lists symptoms of overheating: muscle cramping, unusually heavy sweating, shortness of breath, dizziness, headaches, weakness and nausea.2 Share that list with the facilitators before the first outdoor block. Ask the venue what its staff does when a guest looks unwell and where the nearest medical care sits.

For staying cool, the page says that if you are outside, especially for a long time, and the HeatRisk is red or above, you can stay in the shade as much as possible and take breaks when you can.2 It also says to do outdoor activities during the coolest parts of the day or evening, if possible.2 For drinking, it says to carry a water bottle and to drink and refill it throughout the day, and it says to consider limiting beverages high in sugars and sodium, and caffeine and alcohol.2 Put the breaks on the agenda so nobody has to ask for one, and set water out at every outdoor spot. Ask whether the venue has shade where the group will meet.

One detail changes how a room is cooled. The page says to use fans indoors only if indoor temperatures are less than ninety degrees Fahrenheit, and that in temperatures above ninety a fan can increase body temperature.2 Ask how the meeting room is cooled, since a fan alone may not do the job on a hot day. Ask about the venue's own rules for outdoor activity and fluids too.

Weigh the setting against the job

Put two lists side by side. On one, write what the retreat has to accomplish. On the other, write what each setting would ask of the group in the month you picked: supervision near water and protection from cold, plus shade and water in the heat. Does a setting that fits the job need more preparation than your group can give?

Send the same questions to every venue you consider. Ask who supervises the water and what the site offers for shelter from cold or heat. Ask how the schedule can change on short notice. Ask for the answers in writing so you can compare them. Check the specifics with the venue, and ask a lawyer any contract or liability question.

Decide if a silent retreat suits your team

August 19, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A silent retreat is quieter than most planners picture. It suits a small group doing quiet, inward work and rarely suits a first team offsite. Find out what the silence covers before you put one in front of your team.

People on a silent retreat go without speaking, often for days, and the whole format is built around that gap.

That total covers every kind of wellness trip, so it says little about silent programs on their own.

Strictness varies from program to program. Some places are fully monastic. Others set aside quiet zones and a few spots where talking is allowed. Silence can reach meals, group walks and scheduled activities, and some programs allow note-taking or journaling while others ask you to put the pen down too. Either way, it is the opposite of a normal group day, where talk is the default.

People who want time for reflection are the natural fit. A first team offsite usually wants people talking and working side by side, which a silent program rules out.

Before you shortlist one, settle a single question. Do you want people to think alone, or to work together? Then read each program's rules on talking at meals and in free time, and look at who usually attends. Today, draft three to five questions on what guests are expected to do during the silence, and email them to the venue.

What the research says about safety

Many silent retreats are built around meditation, so the safety record deserves a look before you book a group. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says meditation and mindfulness usually are considered to have few risks. It adds that few studies have looked for harm, so it cannot make definite statements about safety. See the full page at NCCIH, Meditation and Mindfulness: Effectiveness and Safety.

The agency describes a 2020 review of 83 studies with 6,703 participants. The researchers concluded that about 8 percent of participants had a negative effect, similar to the rate for psychological therapies. Anxiety and depression were the most common. Applied to twelve people, that rate works out to roughly one person. Treat it as a rough guide, since the studies covered many kinds of practice.

How common meditation is, and what is still unproven

The agency cites the National Health Interview Survey, which found that the share of U.S. adults who meditated rose from 7.5 percent in 2002 to 17.3 percent in 2022. At that rate, about two people in twelve may already meditate. Ask. A two-question survey on that point costs you five minutes.

The same NCCIH page warns that much of the research on benefits has been preliminary or not scientifically rigorous. It says results may have been interpreted too optimistically. Tell participants that up front. Describe the retreat as a trial you are running together. Skip promises about outcomes.

Questions to put to the program lead in writing

Ask what happens when a participant struggles. Can someone leave the silence early, and who checks in? Because the agency lists anxiety first among reported harms, those two questions come before the schedule. Ask for the replies by email so you can share them with the group before anyone commits.

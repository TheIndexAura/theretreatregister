Check Liability Insurance Paperwork Before You Sign a Retreat Venue

September 21, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Someone says the venue has insurance, and the talk moves on. Before signing, ask a licensed agent what a certificate of insurance can and cannot show, and write down who carries what for the venue, the organizer and each outside vendor.

Draw the three parties before you read any contract

Take a sheet of paper and write the venue at the top left, the organizer in the middle and the outside vendor at the top right. Anyone you bring onto the property, such as a caterer, a trail guide or a facilitator, would sit in that third corner. Draw a line between each pair. The line from venue to organizer is the rental agreement, and the line from organizer to vendor is the service contract. The line from venue to vendor may be missing, and that gap is worth a question before anyone signs.

The reason to draw it is that the sentence "the venue has insurance" is easy to accept without checking it in writing. Ask your licensed agent which documents are needed from each party, instead of relying on what someone says about another party's coverage. So the useful question is which policy sits behind each line, written down, with a name on it. Insurance questions are best answered by an agent. What any policy will pay is a question for your agent or broker, who can read the actual documents.

The Small Business Administration describes general liability insurance as coverage that "protects against financial loss as the result of bodily injury, property damage, medical expenses," and it goes on to list libel, slander, defending lawsuits, and settlement bonds or judgments.1 That is a general description from a federal agency. How a particular policy treats an outside contractor on someone else's property is a question for your agent, so keep a list of questions as you read.

The same SBA page says you should compare rates, terms, and benefits for insurance offers from several different agents, and it says to speak to insurance agents to find out what kinds of coverage makes sense for your business.1 A retreat host can borrow that advice. Pick an agent early, before the contract is in final form, so the documents you gather from the venue and the vendors have someone to review them.

Ask the venue what it carries and what it expects of vendors

Send the venue contact a short email and keep the reply. Ask for the name of the venue's insurance contact. Once your licensed agent has said which documents are needed, ask whether the venue can provide them, such as a certificate of insurance or the declarations page of its general liability policy. Ask whether the venue requires every outside vendor to carry its own coverage, and if so, what the venue wants to see in writing. Ask whether the venue has a preferred wording for additional insured requests.

These are questions to ask the venue. Whatever the answer, it is most useful when it arrives in writing and names a person.

Ask a second group of questions about the organizer. Does the venue expect the organizer to buy a separate event policy? Does the venue want the organizer named on any certificate? Whether the venue's own policy reaches the organizer is a question for your licensed agent, who reads the documents. If the venue states a requirement in a sentence, ask that it be added to the rental agreement or to an attached sheet. A promise made in an email is easy to lose track of when the contract itself is silent.

Ask about activities as well. If the schedule includes hiking, cooking classes, a bonfire or a ropes course, say so in the email and ask whether the venue has requirements for those activities. You are not asking the venue to give coverage advice. You are asking it to state, in its own words, what it requires from you, and your agent can say whether any policy speaks to those activities.

Read a certificate of insurance for what it is

One state's statute gives a worked example of the wording. Minnesota's insurance statute says a licensed insurer or insurance producer may provide to a third party a certificate of insurance, and it defines that term as a document that provides evidence of property or liability insurance coverage and the amount of insurance issued, and does not convey any contractual rights to the certificate holder.2 That is Minnesota's definition for Minnesota's law.

The same statute has a statement for certificates issued to someone other than the policyholder. Its wording is that the certificate "does not affirmatively or negatively amend, extend, or alter the coverage afforded by the insurance policy."2 It also says filed forms may not be amended at the request of a third party.2 These are Minnesota rules for certificates issued under that state's law. Which state's law applies to your retreat, and what any of this means for your booking, are questions for a licensed lawyer. Ask the venue only what it requires.

When a vendor sends you a certificate, ask your agent how to read it and what it leaves out. Then look at the insurer's name, the policy period, the type of policy and the amounts, and compare them with whatever the venue asked for. Check that the policy dates cover every day the vendor is on the property, including setup and teardown. Ask the agent whether anything else should come with the certificate.

If a certificate lists the wrong dates or the wrong business name, ask the vendor to have it reissued. Ask your agent whether a corrected copy should come from the vendor or be reissued by the insurer or producer, and what to do with the first copy.

Ask for what you need from each outside vendor

Send each vendor a short written request. Once your licensed agent has said which documents are needed, ask the vendor to provide them, which may include a certificate of insurance showing the carrier name, the policy period and the limits. Ask whether the vendor brings employees or subcontractors, and who they are. Whether the vendor's policy applies at a location owned by someone else, and whether those people are covered, are questions for your agent, and only the agent, reading the policy, can say how it answers them.

If your agent says additional insured status is worth asking about, ask the vendor what endorsement wording it can provide, in writing. This is where the planner and the agent need to sit down together. A certificate may show a name in a box, and Minnesota's statute says a certificate does not amend, extend, or alter the coverage. Whether that holds where your retreat is takes a lawyer to answer. If the venue asks for additional insured status, your agent can tell you whether the vendor's endorsement language lines up with the venue's request. Ask the agent about any conflict before the vendor arrives instead of after a problem.

Ask each vendor to describe its insurance in plain words, then ask for the documents. If a vendor is slow to answer, send a second note, record the dates, and keep every reply in one folder labeled with the retreat date and the word insurance. If a vendor cannot produce a document your agent listed before signing, pause and ask the vendor why.

Territory belongs on the agent's list too. Ask your agent whether a vendor's policy applies at the location of your retreat, and what documents would show it.

Build a packet for your agent and set the signing order

Once the replies arrive, put everything in one packet. Include the venue rental agreement, the vendor service contracts, whichever documents your agent listed from the venue and from each vendor, any additional insured wording from either side, and the retreat dates and headcount. Add a one page summary that lists each party, its role, its carrier, the amounts shown, and the status you asked for. Leave a blank where an answer is missing, and write the word missing beside it. Do not guess.

Then ask your agent a short list of questions. Does the organizer need a separate event policy, given what the venue wrote? Do the vendors' endorsements name the organizer, and what do they say? Do any policies exclude an activity on your schedule? The SBA page says to think about the accidents, natural disasters or lawsuits that could damage a business, and that same habit works for a retreat.1 List the risks of your own schedule first, then ask the agent which documents speak to them.

Decide the order of signing. Ask your agent which insurance documents should be in hand before each agreement is signed, and ask a lawyer what the agreements say and in what order they should be signed. If a vendor insists on starting work before the paper arrives, ask the venue in writing whether it will allow that.

Today, open two email drafts. Send the venue its questions, send each vendor its questions, and drop both sent messages into a new folder named with the retreat date and the word insurance. When the replies come in, forward the folder to your agent with the questions you sent and ask for a written answer on each one.

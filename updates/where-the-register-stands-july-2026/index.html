Coverage Snapshot

Where the register stands, July 2026

July 12, 2026 · The Retreat Register

As of July 12, 2026, the register held 1,374 curated corporate retreat venues.1 Software checked each one, and on July 11 a person read every published row, every name and every website, cutting 274 entries no automated filter had caught.2 That is why the number above is smaller than the raw catalog it came from, and that is the most important thing on this page.

Smaller on purpose

Three days before this snapshot, on July 9, 2026, the raw catalog stood near 1,850 candidates going in to a full re-judging.3 The published figure today is 1,374. The register spent the days in between taking things out.

What came out is specific and it is all in the build log.

  • On July 9, reading sample rows caught what volume metrics hid. Messy public data had slipped a luxury fashion retailer and an online dictionary into the catalog as “venues.” Both were cut.3
  • On July 10, 95 convention and expo facilities were moved off the public list to a review shelf, described as “real businesses, wrong category.”4 Two days later, 88 of those borderline properties were still on the shelf waiting for a decision from a person.5
  • Also on July 10, faith-based retreat centres were reviewed one by one against the same quality bar as everyone else: 7 promoted, 2 declined.4
  • On July 11, one person read every row and cut those 274 entries. Budget hotel chains dressed up as retreat centres, and dozens of template-name fakes pointing at unrelated websites.2
  • Separately, and on its own running tally, the classification judge had rejected 369 candidates to date as businesses that simply are not retreat venues.5

A directory that counts junk is not bigger. It is just heavier.

The rule behind all of it was set on day one, July 7, 2026, and it has not changed since: a venue is either good enough to be in the register, or it is not in it.6

Those twenty states hold 715 venues between them. Against a register of 1,374, that is 52.0% of everything, which leaves 659 venues spread across the other thirty states and the District of Columbia. An average of twenty-one each.

That is the honest shape of a young national register. Half of it sits in twenty states, and the other half is thin enough that a single good week of sweeping can change a state’s standing completely. The register does not publish which states sit at the bottom, so no number on this page names one. What the table proves is only what it shows: every state outside that twenty held 21 venues or fewer on July 12, 2026.

A number planners will appreciate

91% of venues in the register have a direct phone number attached, which is 1,247 of 1,374.1

That sounds boring until you have spent an afternoon hunting a sales-office number through a resort’s chatbot. Being able to reach a venue is part of what the register screens for. The Retreat Register screens for essentials before listing a venue, focusing first on a direct phone number and dedicated meeting or lodging space. A venue you cannot contact is not much of a venue.

The reverse of that number is worth as much. On the same date, 133 of the register’s 1,374 venues published a starting price anywhere on their own website.8 That is 9.7%, which leaves 1,241 venues that will only quote your group directly. Reachable is normal in this industry. Priced is not.

The quality bar, in full

Every property is judged against four questions.5

  1. Does it have real retreat lodging or dedicated meeting space, not just rooms, but somewhere a group can actually work and stay?
  2. Are the grounds and setting somewhere worth taking a team?
  3. Is the operation professional, meaning a real website, reachable contact, a business that answers?
  4. Is there a genuine welcome for outside groups, rather than a private facility or a members-only campus?

The fourth question is the one that removes the most properties that look right on paper. A corporate campus with beautiful grounds and forty bedrooms is not a retreat venue if it will not host your company.

What the machine is doing right now

Scouting the thin states. On July 11, every state was given a minimum coverage target sized to its market, described in the log as “depth everywhere, not token entries,” and the worst-covered states are now searched first.2 The sweeps run town by town, not just through the famous destinations, but through the lake regions and small mountain towns where your dates are not fought over.

Working the brand families. Large hospitality brands run many properties on one website, which quietly hides all but one of them from most catalogs. As of July 12 the register tracks each property individually. The build log calls it “a flaw that had been hiding whole resort markets, including most of Hawaii’s.”9 Hawaii is the one state the register admits it was missing almost entirely, and the fix is a large part of what happened to the count over the following week.

Auditing the rejection pile. On the same day, 2,811 previously rejected properties were put back on trial against a rebuilt judge that reads a venue’s meetings, groups and events pages rather than only its homepage. 606 were restored to the pipeline, 2,193 rejections were upheld, and 12 were set aside as obvious junk.10 That story has its own page.

Matching photographs to properties. A listing never shows a different property. Until an owner claims their listing and uploads their own photos, we match the venue with a close stand-in image.5

The jump from 1,374 to 2,281 in five days is 66%, and it is the brand-family fix and the 606 restored rejections arriving at once. The fall from 2,281 to 2,125 is 156 venues removed in a single day, and the build log entry for July 18, 2026 records the reason in its own words: “Quality passes removed venues, and those cuts are part of how we check.”

Two independent pages agree the register got smaller that day. It is the only day in the published series where that happened, and it is on this site rather than buried, which is the point.

July 19, 2026 is the last date the register published an exact total. Pages after that give the rounded figure of more than 2,000 venues, and pages published from July 22 onward carry no count at all. If you want a current number, this site does not have one, and this page will not invent one.

Why any of this is worth counting

Business events are not a small corner of the economy. The Events Industry Council, working with Oxford Economics, measured 1.65 billion participants at business events worldwide in 2025 and US$1.3 trillion in direct spending, which is 12.2% above 2019 levels.15

That is a lot of money moving through rooms that are difficult to find, priced on request, and photographed badly. A register that publishes its own cuts is a small answer to a large problem, and it is the answer we can actually prove.

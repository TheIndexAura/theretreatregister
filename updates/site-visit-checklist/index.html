Tour a Retreat Venue: A Site Visit Checklist for Planners

August 22, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A site visit is for the things a listing cannot show: how far the walks are, how the rooms sound and whether the building works for every guest. Bring a tape measure, a written list of questions and someone to stand in the next room. Write the answers down before you drive away.

Plan the visit before you arrive

Photos and floor plans already tell you what the main rooms look like. The visit should answer what they leave out, so send the venue your dates, your headcount and a rough daily schedule a week before you go. Ask them to show you the actual rooms your group would use, including the meeting room, the dining room and a typical guest room, and not only the best ones.

Pick a time of day that matches your retreat. A visit at ten on a quiet weekday morning tells you little about how the dining room sounds at seven in the evening with another group in it. If you can, ask to stay for a meal or come back for an hour in the evening. Eat what the group would eat, and notice how long the kitchen takes to serve a full room.

Bring a second person if the budget allows it. One of you talks with the venue host while the other walks, listens and takes notes. The second person can stand in a bedroom while you test the walls.

Walk every route at the pace of the slowest guest

Start at the car park or the drop-off point, with your bag in hand. Walk to a guest room, then from the guest room to the meeting room, then from the meeting room to the dining room. Time each walk. Do it slowly, at the pace of the guest who will find it hardest, and notice the surface under your feet as you go.

Gravel paths, steep slopes, steps without rails and long outdoor routes all matter more in the rain or after dark. Ask whether the paths are lit at night and whether there is a covered way between buildings. Ask how luggage gets from the car to the rooms, and who carries it.

Write the times down. A five-minute walk between lodging and the meeting room is fine once a day and a real cost five times a day, so count how often the group will make each trip on your schedule.

Bring the access questions and a tape measure

The ADA National Network, in its factsheet on accessible lodging, says customer service staff at places of lodging covered by the law must be trained to answer questions about accessible routes, the layout of accessible guest rooms and bathrooms, equipment such as bath benches and visual alarm and alert devices, and the accessibility of common spaces such as meeting rooms.1 Your host on the tour is a fair person to ask. Go through each of those topics with them, and ask to see an accessible guest room and its bathroom, not only a photo of one.

The same factsheet notes an exception for a place with no more than five rooms for rent where the owner lives on site.1 A small inn or farmhouse may fall under that exception, so a different answer there may simply reflect that exception. Ask anyway, and plan around what you learn.

For older buildings, the factsheet says owners must remove barriers where doing so is readily achievable.1 If you find a step at the meeting room door, ask the owner whether a ramp or another entrance is planned before your dates, and ask for the answer in writing.

Carry a tape measure. The Justice Department's ADA Standards for Accessible Design set a clear opening of at least thirty-two inches for doors on an accessible route, and a clear width of at least thirty-six inches for the walking surface along that route.2 Measure the door of the meeting room, the door of the accessible guest room and the narrowest hallway between them. Take a photo of each one with the tape in the shot, and label it with the room name. Weeks later, when you compare venues, the photo settles any doubt about which door was which.

The factsheet says those standards set minimum requirements for newly built or altered buildings.1 An older building that is not being altered may not meet them, though its owner must still remove barriers where that is readily achievable. If a measurement falls short, it becomes a question for the owner, and it tells you which guests may need a different room or a different route. If someone in your group uses a wheelchair, share your measurements with them and let them decide what works.

Test sound, water, signal and the evening room

Then test for sound. Stand in one guest room while your partner talks at a normal volume in the room next door, then swap. Listen for footsteps from the floor above and for doors closing in the hallway. Do the same in the meeting room while staff work in the kitchen or the room next to it.

Run a shower in a guest room for a few minutes. Check how long the water takes to warm up and whether the pressure holds. Ask how the venue copes when the whole group showers between the morning session and dinner, and whether the hot water has ever run out for a full house.

Check the cell signal and the wireless network inside a guest room and in the meeting room, not only in the lobby. Open a large file or a video and see how it loads. If the signal is weak, ask whether that is true all over the site or only in some buildings.

Then think about the evenings. Every group ends up somewhere at ten at night. Ask where that is: a lounge, a fire pit, the bar or someone's room. Then ask how close it sits to the guest rooms, and whether there are quiet hours. A group that wants to talk late and a guest who wants to sleep early both need an answer.

Look for a small room where a guest can step away, take a work call in private or rest for an hour. Ask whether it is free during your dates or shared with other guests. Ask whether another group has booked the site for the same dates, and which spaces the two groups would share.

Write it down before you leave

Memories of a site visit blur by the second venue. Sit in the car for ten minutes and write down every time, measurement and answer while they are fresh. Note anything the host promised, such as a ramp, a changed room or a later checkout, and send those back to the venue in an email the same day.

A written reply turns the promise into something you can quote later. Compare venues on the same list of questions. The walking, water and sound answers say more about the week itself than the view from the terrace.

Here is a sentence you can paste into your follow-up email: "Thank you for the tour today. Please confirm in writing the rooms discussed for the group, the accessible room and its bathroom, the meeting room door width, and any changes you said you would make before the retreat dates."

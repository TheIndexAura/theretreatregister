Write a Rain Plan for a Retreat: Thunder, Floodwater and Indoor Rooms

August 24, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Every outdoor block on the agenda needs an indoor twin. Write both versions, learn the National Weather Service rules for thunder and floodwater, and find out which rooms hold the whole group.

Plan the wet version before you need it

The agenda says lakeside session. The sky says otherwise, and now the whole group is standing in a hallway while someone looks for a room. That scene is avoidable. A rain plan is a second version of the agenda, written in advance, that says where each outdoor block goes if the weather will not allow it.

Go through the schedule and mark every block that depends on being outside. For each one, write the indoor alternative next to it, with the room name and the number of people it holds. Check that the alternative can hold the whole group, with its chairs, tables and equipment. A room that works for a small breakout may not fit an activity built for everyone.

Name who decides. Someone has to say, at a set time, that the plan has changed. Pick the group lead or a facilitator, agree on the hour by which the decision is made, and tell guests how they will hear about it. A decision made at breakfast leaves time to set up. A decision made after the group has walked down to the water does not.

Tell guests what the wet plan means for them. A line in the welcome note can say that outdoor sessions may move indoors, suggest a light rain jacket and shoes that cope with wet ground, and explain how a change is announced. Guests who know the plan in advance have time to prepare for a change.

Learn the rule for thunder

The National Weather Service gives a short instruction. When you hear thunder, immediately move to safe shelter, which it describes as a substantial building with electricity or plumbing or an enclosed, metal-topped vehicle with the windows up.1 It says to stay in safe shelter for thirty minutes or longer after you hear the last sound of thunder, and it warns that no place outside is safe when thunderstorms are in the area.1

Put that into the plan. Decide which building the group will use, and make sure it is close enough to every outdoor spot on the schedule that people can reach it quickly. A pavilion, a tent or an open-sided shelter does not match the description, so find out what the venue has that does. Tell the facilitators the rule, and tell them they may stop a session on their own authority when thunder is heard.

Plan for the wait. A group that has moved inside for half an hour needs something to do. Keep a short indoor activity ready, such as a discussion prompt or a break with drinks, so that the delay does not feel like lost time. Thirty minutes after the last thunder is the shortest wait, and the group should go back out only if conditions are safe. Someone has to make that choice, which is another reason to name a decision maker.

Respect floodwater

Heavy rain brings another hazard. The National Weather Service runs a campaign called Turn Around Don't Drown, and its page says a mere six inches of fast-moving flood water can knock over an adult, that it takes just twelve inches of rushing water to carry away most cars, and that two feet of rushing water can carry away SUVs and trucks.2 It says it is never safe to drive or walk into flood waters, and that people should never drive around the barriers blocking a flooded road.2

For a retreat group, the practical points are about transport and paths. If guests drive between lodging and a venue, or if the group walks across a low field or a stream crossing, decide in advance what happens after heavy rain. Ask the venue which roads and paths flood, and agree that the group does not cross water to keep to the schedule. The same page says that people underestimate the force and power of water, so the plan should leave no room for a debate on the day.2

Give drivers a rule: if a road is covered with water or blocked, they turn around and choose another route. If your group relies on a shuttle, ask the venue what the driver does in heavy rain and who can authorize a delay. Putting this in a short message before the trip helps guests understand that a late arrival is better than a risky one.

Ask the venue about its wet-weather rooms

Find out whether the venue has a rain plan of its own. Ask which indoor rooms are free at the hours your outdoor blocks run, and whether another group has a claim on them. Ask how many people each room holds in the layout you need. Ask who moves the chairs and who sets up the screen, and how long that takes.

Think about equipment as well. Projectors, speakers, flip charts and printed material do not like rain, and a group that moves quickly can lose track of them. Decide who carries what, where it goes in a storm and who checks that nothing is left outside. A small box or bag for the session materials makes a quick move easier.

Find out how long it takes to set up the wet-weather room and who is available to help, since an alternative that needs an hour of setup does not suit a short break.

Find out how the venue communicates a weather change. Does the staff send a message, post a notice or knock on doors? Where do guests wait if a storm arrives while they are outside? Which building does it use as storm shelter? A covered walkway is not shelter under the description above. Clear answers to these questions give you something to plan around.

Ask about the practical side of a wet day. Are there places to dry coats and shoes? Is there a mat at each entrance, and who keeps the floors safe? Can the kitchen adjust meal times if the group moves inside late? These are small matters that decide whether a wet day feels managed or chaotic.

Write it down and share it

Put the rain plan on one page: the original agenda, the wet alternative for each outdoor block, the shelter building, the decision maker and the decision time. Give it to the group lead, the facilitators and the venue before the trip. Mention it in your welcome note so that guests know what to expect if the weather changes.

After the retreat, note what worked. If the group had to use the wet plan, write down how long the move took, which room served well and what you would change. Those notes make the next rain plan better, and they give you something specific to share with the venue about the rooms and the routes.

Ask that the rooms you rely on be named in the booking agreement. A room that is promised in an email may be given to another group if the contract does not mention it. When the plan is written and shared, everyone already knows where they are going.

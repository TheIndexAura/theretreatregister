From the Data

Check beds, meals, chores and seasons at a farm retreat

August 17, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Many planners book a resort out of habit. The comforts are familiar and the services are set, but one resort tends to look a lot like the next.

A working farm or ranch is a different kind of week. Some groups find that a simpler, more grounded setting gives them something to talk about afterward.

A farm stay needs its own questions, and four of them cover most of the ground. Start with beds. Does the property sleep your whole group? A venue that lists a phone number and a dedicated meeting or lodging space has answered part of that before you write.

Then ask about meals. Some properties have a chef. Others expect the group to cook for itself, so ask which one you are getting. Pricing comes by quote, which means you should also ask what the quote covers.

Third, find out what the group does on the land. Watching a farm run is one thing. Harvesting and animal care are hands-on, and your group should know that before it books.

Last, ask what the season changes. The activities on offer shift through the year, and so does how comfortable the place feels.

Write down what your group needs for sleeping and for activity, then send written inquiries to the properties that fit.

How common farm stays are

The USDA Economic Research Service defines agritourism as on-farm services for recreation, hospitality or entertainment, and it names overnight accommodations as an example of hospitality. Its read of the 2022 Census of Agriculture puts farm and ranch income from agritourism at $1.26 billion, up 12.4 percent from 2017 after adjusting for inflation. About 57 percent of counties reported some. So a working farm near your target region is plausible, though 43 percent of counties reported none (USDA Economic Research Service, 2022 Census of Agriculture chart).

The same note links higher agritourism income to farms that specialize in grapes or fruit and tree nuts, and to places near natural amenities. If you want a vineyard or an orchard, start your search there (USDA Economic Research Service on where agritourism income concentrates).

Read the risk notice before the group arrives

State law can shift risk to your group. Virginia is one example. Its code defines an agritourism activity as one on a farm or ranch that lets the public view or enjoy rural activities, whether or not the participant paid. It treats the behavior of animals and the ordinary dangers of farm equipment as inherent risks (Code of Virginia 3.2-6400, definitions).

An operator who posts the required warning is not liable for injury from those inherent risks. The protection ends if the operator is negligent, or knew of a dangerous condition and did not make it known (Code of Virginia 3.2-6401, liability limited). The notice must also appear in a written contract for instruction or equipment rental, and an operator who skips it loses the protection (Code of Virginia 3.2-6402, notice required). This is one state. Look up the statute where your property sits, and ask the owner to send the warning text in advance.

If the group will touch animals

The same Virginia chapter has a separate rule for places that keep animals for three or more consecutive hours, such as a petting zoo or fair. The operator's protection from claims about animal-borne illness depends on a posted warning sign and a hand-washing station (Code of Virginia 3.2-6403, domesticated animal premises). Ask in writing where the hand-washing station is and what the sign says. Then tell your group before they pack.

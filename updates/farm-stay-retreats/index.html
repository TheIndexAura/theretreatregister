Plan a Farm Stay Retreat: Beds, Meals, Chores and Questions for the Owner

August 17, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A farm stay changes the questions you ask. Count the beds, settle who cooks, decide what the group does on the land, ask the venue about animal areas, and find out what each season changes.

Count the beds before anything else

A farm stay is a different kind of booking from a hotel, and the first question is the plainest one. Can the property sleep your whole group, in the arrangement your group needs? Ask whether the beds are in a main house or in separate cabins, and ask for the number of beds in each building. Find out who would share a room with whom, and whether anyone has to cross open ground at night to reach a bathroom. Write the answers next to your headcount before you tell anyone the dates.

It helps to know how the sector is described. The USDA Economic Research Service defines agritourism as on-farm services for recreation, hospitality or entertainment. It gives overnight accommodations as its example of hospitality.1 The same page says about fifty-seven percent of counties reported agritourism income in the twenty twenty-two Census of Agriculture.1 That is a statement about counties and income. It does not say how many farms sleep groups, so a planner still has to ask each property directly.

The page also says farms in more populated counties tend to earn more agritourism income. It says the same about farms in or near counties with abundant natural amenities, and about those specializing in certain types of crop or livestock production, specifically grapes, fruit and tree nuts, and specialty livestock.1 If your group wants a particular kind of farm, that gives you a place to start searching. Treat it as a hint about where to look. The page does not describe the quality of a stay, the size of the group a farm can host, or whether a farm is open in the month you want, so those stay on your list of questions.

Settle who cooks and who eats where

Meals are the part of a farm stay that a planner can least guess from a listing. Ask which version you are booking. Does a cook prepare the meals, does the group get a kitchen and feed itself, or is it a mix, such as a shared breakfast with the other meals on your own? Get the answer in writing, in the same message that lists the beds.

If the group will cook, decide who is in charge of each meal and who cleans up after it. Ask about the kitchen itself: how many burners there are and whether the dishes cover the full headcount. Ask who buys groceries and how far the nearest store is. A group that arrives late on the first day needs to know whether dinner is waiting or whether someone has to shop before anyone eats.

Ask what happens with dietary needs. A planner who knows about an allergy should say so in the first inquiry, in plain words. If the group is cooking, ask whether the kitchen can store a separate set of food for a guest who needs it. A shared kitchen is a good place for a mix-up, so decide in advance which shelf or container belongs to whom.

Think about where people eat, too. Ask the owner which spaces are fine for meals and which are not. A picnic lunch in a pasture or a snack at the barn door sounds pleasant, but the owner knows the property and you do not. Ask whether animals are kept near the dining area, and ask where the group should wash up before a meal. Put the answers into the schedule so no one has to guess with a plate in hand.

Decide what the group does on the land

Watching a farm run and joining in are different activities. One group may want a tour and a quiet walk, while another wants to help with morning chores. Before you promise anything to your participants, ask the owner which tasks guests may do and which are off limits. The owner may also want the group out of the way at certain hours, so ask which hours those are and what the group can do instead.

Decide how the group will divide its time. Decide how much hands-on work suits the group, and how much open time it wants. Build a schedule with a few set blocks and some open time, and mark which blocks take place outdoors. Tell guests what to wear in advance. Ask the owner whether closed shoes are needed, whether boots can be borrowed, and whether the ground is rough or muddy on the paths the group would use.

Ask the venue what its rules are for guests in work areas. Ask whether a staff member goes along on each activity and who gives the safety talk before it starts. A group lead should know who to find if someone is hurt or feels unwell, and how far the nearest medical help is. Write the answers into a one page sheet that goes to every participant before arrival, so the instructions do not depend on one person remembering them.

Ask about the group's own limits as well. Ask whether any guest cannot walk far or would rather not be near animals. Find out which activities have an alternative, and whether the farm can offer one. A retreat goes better when everyone has something to do that suits them, and the owner can tell you what is realistic on that property.

Ask about animals, health and visitors

Questions about animals, hand washing and visitors belong in the first inquiry. For the current guidance on contact with farm animals, read the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention pages yourself before the trip, and ask the venue what it expects from guests. The venue answers for its own policy on animal areas, hand washing stations and shoes.

Start with the animals themselves. Ask which animals live on the property and which areas guests may enter. Ask whether any activity involves touching them, and who supervises it. If the group includes families, tell parents what the venue says before they pack, and decide who watches the youngest guests while the others take part. A planner who has written the owner's answers down can pass them on word for word.

Plan for the place where people wash. Ask the owner where the hand-washing stations are and whether they are stocked with soap and water. Ask where the group should leave muddy shoes. Tell the group about the venue's rules at the start of each outdoor activity, not only in the welcome note, because a note sent weeks ahead is easy to forget.

Ask whether any guest is allergic to animals or hay, and let that guest decide whether to join the animal activities. Ask the venue whether it keeps a first aid kit and where it sits. For legal questions about guest injuries on a farm, ask a lawyer about the rules that apply to your case.

Ask what the season changes

Only the owner can say how the seasons change a farm stay. Ask which activities run in the month you want. Ask which farm jobs fall in that month, and whether anything hands-on is on offer if the work is slow. Ask what the owner would suggest for a quiet week.

Ask about comfort. Find out how the buildings are heated and whether the walkways turn muddy or icy. Ask whether the lodging is open all year and whether the water and the kitchen work in cold weather. Ask the owner which weeks suit a group best, and why.

Ask about the group's schedule against the farm's. Ask when the animals are cared for, what hours the staff is busy, and how that affects meals and tours. Ask what happens to an outdoor task when it rains. Decide with the owner what the group does indoors, and which room it uses.

When you have the answers, put them in one inquiry for each property on your list. Include the dates and the headcount, and ask for a written reply that covers beds and meals. Compare the replies side by side, and keep the written ones in one folder so that anyone helping you plan can find them.

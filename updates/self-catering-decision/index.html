Self-Catering or a Caterer for Your Retreat? How Planners Decide

September 14, 2026 · The Retreat Register

Choose self-catering when the group has willing cooks, a simple menu and real free time in the agenda. Choose a caterer when the schedule is full or the list of special diets is long. Either way, cooking for a group comes with food safety rules, and whoever is in the kitchen should know them before anyone shops.

Treat cooking as a job on the agenda

A rental house with a big kitchen can look like the cheaper choice on a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet leaves out the hours. Someone plans the menu, shops, cooks, serves and cleans up, for everyone, at every meal. On a retreat those hours come out of the program or out of people's rest.

Some groups want exactly that. Cooking together can be the best part of the trip for a team that enjoys it, and chopping onions side by side gets people talking in a way a facilitator would struggle to plan. Other groups find out on the second evening that the same two colleagues have cooked every meal while everyone else sat in sessions.

So start with the work, and let the cost follow. Who does it, and when? The answer comes from three counts you can run before you sign anything.

Count the cooks, the diets and the free hours

First, count the cooks. Ask in writing who is willing to take a turn in the kitchen and who has cooked for more than a handful of people before. A group of twenty with two willing cooks is in a very different position from a group of eight where everyone wants to help. Ask the willing cooks what they would make for a crowd. Someone happy to cook dinner for four may not want to cook it for twenty.

Second, count the separate diets. Each allergy or medical diet that needs its own preparation adds a track to every meal. FoodSafety.gov, the federal government's food safety site, says a caterer should receive a list of guests' allergies before an event.2 A volunteer cook needs the same list, and needs to keep those meals apart from shopping bag to plate.

Third, count the free hours. Mark every block where the whole group has to be in a session. If the only gaps left are early morning and late at night, nobody has time to cook a proper meal. A caterer or a venue kitchen earns its place.

Set the counts side by side. Plenty of willing cooks, few special diets and open afternoons point to self-catering. Few cooks, a long diet list or a packed schedule point to hiring help. A mixed result points to a mix, which the last part of this guide covers.

Know the food safety rules the kitchen has to follow

Cooking for four at home and cooking for thirty at a retreat are different jobs. Food sits out longer and dishes get made hours ahead, while the fridge fills up by the first afternoon. The federal guidance is short, and every volunteer cook should read it before the trip.

FoodSafety.gov says perishable food should never be left out of refrigeration for more than two hours, or more than one hour if it is exposed to temperatures above ninety degrees Fahrenheit.1 Food that is not served right after cooking should be kept at one hundred forty degrees or above. The fridge should be set at forty degrees or below. Use one cutting board for fresh produce and a separate one for raw meat, poultry or seafood.1

Those rules shape the plan more than the menu does. A long buffet lunch on a warm terrace needs chafing dishes, slow cookers or trays set in ice.2 A make-ahead dinner needs fridge space, and a house fridge stocked for thirty people may not have it. Leftovers go into shallow containers and into the fridge promptly.1

Ask the venue in writing how many fridges, ovens and burners the kitchen has, and whether it has a dishwasher that can keep up with a group. One fridge for thirty people fills fast.

Shopping for a group is its own errand. A week of food for twenty people may not fit in one car, and a small-town shop may not stock enough of anything for a crowd. Ask whether a grocery delivery reaches the venue. If not, plan the big shop in the nearest larger town on the way in, with a cooler for anything cold.

What to ask a caterer before you hire one

Hiring help moves the work. It does not remove the questions. FoodSafety.gov publishes a set of questions to ask a wedding caterer, and they work for a retreat too. Ask where the food is prepared and what steps the caterer takes to avoid cross-contamination. If the food is cooked off site, ask how it travels and where it is stored until serving time. Ask when it goes out and how long it sits. Then ask whether anyone monitors the temperature.2

Then add the retreat questions. Ask who cleans the kitchen after each service, and read what the rental agreement says about cleaning before you sign it. Ask whether the caterer stays on site between meals or drops the food and leaves, and who sets the tables and clears them. Ask too whether the caterer brings its own serving equipment or expects to use the venue's. Get every answer in writing and attach it to the contract.

Send the caterer your allergy list early, along with any vegetarian, vegan or gluten-free needs.2 Ask for a written reply that confirms each line.

The middle options, and how to run self-catering well

You do not have to pick one model for the whole trip. Breakfast can be the easiest meal to hand to the group. People wake at different times, and a stocked table of fruit, bread and yogurt lets them eat when they are ready. Dinner asks more, since it comes at the end of a long day and everyone eats at once. One split is to have the venue or a caterer handle dinners while the group makes breakfast and lunch from stocked supplies. Another is a private cook for one or two evenings, with the group cooking the rest of the time. Whatever the split, write down who owns each meal. A grid with days across the top and meals down the side, with a name or a company in every box, saves the quiet moment at six in the evening when everyone thought someone else was cooking.

If you self-cater, name a meal lead for each day. That person plans the menu, works from a written shopping list and hands out tasks. Rotate the lead and the cleanup, so the same people are not at the sink every night. Shop with the diet list in hand, and keep allergen-free food apart and labeled.

Keep the first night simple, since people arrive at different times and tired. Stock food that keeps safely in the fridge for the late arrivals. Post the two-hour rule on the fridge door, where every cook will see it.

A sentence to send the venue before you decide: "Please tell us how many fridges, ovens and burners the kitchen has, whether there is a dishwasher, and what the rental agreement says about cleaning after each meal."

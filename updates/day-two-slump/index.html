Plan Day Two of a Retreat Around the Afternoon Energy Slump

September 11, 2026 · The Retreat Register

A federal health course for nurses describes a dip in wakefulness in the middle of the afternoon. Whether to plan around it on day two of a retreat is the planner's own judgment, and breaks and room questions are things to settle with the venue.

Expect the dip before you write the agenda

A room may go quiet in the afternoon. Eyes drift from the speaker, hands go to pockets, and the facilitator may talk a little louder to compensate. It is tempting to read that as a sign the content is dull. A better first guess is to look at how alertness moves through a day, and to build the agenda with that in mind.

NIOSH, the workplace health institute within the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, publishes a training course for nurses on shift work and long work hours. The course is written for nurses, and it is about work shifts, so none of it is aimed at retreats. One page in it covers the afternoon dip in wakefulness. It says people tend to feel sleepy in the middle of the afternoon, and that at this time the circadian rhythms that promote wakefulness dip while the sleep drive builds up enough that the pressure to sleep dominates.1 The page also describes the evening, when the rhythms for wakefulness dominate over the buildup of sleep drive.1

That page does not mention retreats, and it says nothing about a second day in particular. Whatever you decide about day two is your own planning judgment, built on a general description of how alertness moves through a day. Still, the description is useful. The page describes an afternoon dip in wakefulness, so treat that time as a risk for a session that asks guests to concentrate hard.

Before you build anything, write down the hours on each retreat day when you expect the group to be lively and the hours when you expect them to sag. Mark meal times, travel times and the start and end of each session. Then ask the group lead one question: when did guests arrive, and how late did the first evening run? You are not looking for an exact answer. You are looking for a reason to keep the second afternoon light.

Put the hard work where people are alert

The simplest use of the dip is to avoid it. Place the sessions that need clear thinking, such as decisions, budgets, difficult conversations and anything that involves reading, in the morning and in the hours before the sleepy stretch. Place the sessions that run on talk, movement or hands, such as a walk and talk, a craft activity, a tasting or a group meal, in the afternoon.

It helps to name each block by the kind of effort it asks for. A block can be listening, speaking, reading, writing, deciding or moving. Lay the blocks out in a column and look for runs of the same kind. Ask yourself whether three listening blocks in a row suit your group. Alternating a listening block with a speaking or moving block gives the group a different way to take part, and that is a planning choice, not a sourced result.

Keep the most important item of day two away from the end of lunch. If a decision has to be made, put it before the meal, or put it on the next morning if the schedule allows. If it has to happen after lunch, give the group a short break first and make the item small enough that people can finish it without a long debate.

Plan the first block after lunch as the easiest of the afternoon. Try a small group exercise with a clear task instead of a presentation. Everyone then has something to do. If a guest speaker is booked for that hour, tell the speaker early so they can plan a talk with questions, stories or a short activity in the middle.

Schedule breaks like sessions

The same NIOSH course has a page on rest breaks. It states that taking rest breaks at least every two hours during work shifts will reduce the risk for on-the-job injuries and errors.2 That sentence is about working shifts, and the injuries and errors it counts belong to the work world of the nurses it was written for. A retreat is not the same setting, so treat the number as a reference point and not as a rule for your agenda. Still, a break every two hours is a reasonable starting place when you lay out a long day.

The page also gives a point about how to schedule. It says managers can systematically promote meal and rest breaks, and that at the beginning of the shift they can schedule the breaks along with the other work assignments.2 Think of the agenda in the same way. Put each break on the page at the same time you put each session there, with a start time, an end time and a person who announces it. Decide who has the authority to protect a break when a speaker runs late.

The page says managers can encourage people to take their breaks and create a system for coverage of their duties while on break.2 At a retreat, the equivalent is simple. Decide who watches the clock. Decide who tells a speaker, kindly, that time is up. If the venue staff are serving coffee or setting up the next room, tell them when the break starts so the guests are not standing in a corridor while the room is still being arranged.

One more line on that page is worth keeping in mind. It says researchers do not know how long the alerting effect of a rest break lasts, and it lists factors that influence the effect: how long a person has been awake, the amount of sleep debt, the time of day, and time on task, particularly with monotonous tasks.2 Because the page gives no length or formula, adjust to your own room. Plan the breaks, watch the room, and be ready to add one if the group needs it.

Make the break do something

A break does not have to be empty. The NIOSH page suggests planning enriched breaks that include activities such as exercise or stretching, and novel or amusing diversions.2 For a retreat group, that might mean a stretch led from the front of the room, a short walk outside, or a game that takes five minutes and has nothing to do with the agenda. Keep the idea light, and keep it optional for guests who would rather sit.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has a page on the benefits of physical activity. It says physical activity helps you immediately feel better, function better and sleep better, and that some benefits for brain health happen right after a session of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity.3 The page gives no session length for those brain benefits, and it does not describe a break in a meeting room, so do not promise your group a specific result from a short walk. Offering movement in the middle of the afternoon is the planner's own judgment, and the page does not say to do it.

The same page says adults who sit less and do any amount of moderate- to vigorous-intensity physical activity gain some health benefits.3 In planning terms, that supports a simple habit: do not leave the group seated for the whole afternoon if there is a way to stand, walk or change rooms. Think about mobility and comfort as you plan. A hallway walk, a standing discussion and a seated stretch can all sit on the same list, so that every guest has a choice that suits them.

Think, too, about what the afternoon break looks like in bad weather. Ask whether there is an indoor space for a walk, whether the room can be rearranged for a standing session, and whether the venue has a quiet corner for guests who want a rest.

Build the day-two agenda and test it with the venue

With those pieces in hand, you can sketch a day two. Open with a gentle block that gets people talking. Place the hardest session in the late morning, after one break. Hold lunch at a fixed time, with the next block set up as a light, hands-on activity. Add a movement break in the middle of the afternoon, and finish the day with a short session that asks for reflection and not new material. Adjust every piece of that to your group, since the NIOSH page describes a general pattern of alertness and gives no formula for an agenda.

Then take the draft to the venue and ask practical questions. Can the room be reset during a break, and how long does it take? Is there space for a short walk if it rains? Can coffee, tea and water be set out before each break so that guests do not queue? Can the room temperature and lighting be adjusted during the afternoon? Which staff member can be reached if the schedule needs to change?

Ask the group lead as well. Has a particular session run long before? Were guests tired from travel? Which activities have worked with this group and which have not? Would the group rather have a longer lunch, or a shorter one with an extra afternoon break? The answers help you decide where a buffer belongs.

Keep a short list of backup items beside the agenda: a quieter activity, a prompt for a table discussion, and a way to end a session early if the room is flat. After the retreat, write down which blocks worked and which did not, with the time of day beside each. Keep those notes as material for the next day-two agenda.
